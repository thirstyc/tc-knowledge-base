What is malolactic fermentation?

Topic

Fermentation

Difficulty

Beginner

Short Answer

Malolactic conversion is a second process after alcoholic fermentation, in which bacteria turn sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid. It makes wine rounder and less tart.

Think of the difference between a green apple, which is full of malic acid, and milk, which contains lactic acid. That's the shift in the glass.

It's technically not a fermentation at all, since bacteria rather than yeast do the work and no alcohol is produced, which is why many people now call it malolactic conversion.

Nearly all red wines go through it, both to soften them and to make them microbially stable. For whites it's a style decision: many Chardonnays, especially in Burgundy, use it for a rounder texture, while crisp styles like Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and most Pinot Grigio block it to keep their zip.

One by-product is diacetyl, the compound behind the buttery aroma in some Chardonnays. That famous butter note comes from here, not from oak.

Winemakers can encourage it by warming the cellar or adding bacteria, or prevent it with sulfur, cold and filtration.
