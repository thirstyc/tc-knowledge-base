Why is Marsala so affordable?

Short Answer

Marsala is produced at real industrial volume in western Sicily, and a large share of what gets bottled is destined for the stove, not the table. That reshapes the whole economics of the category. The rules governing entry-level Marsala (the Fine grade) require only about a year of aging, so producers aren't sitting on inventory for a decade the way a Port house does. Base wines are often made from workhorse Sicilian grapes like Grillo, Catarratto and Inzolia, which are cheap and abundant rather than prized. Fortification and the oxidative aging style are also fairly forgiving of imperfect fruit, since you're not chasing the pristine, delicate character a still white needs. Because so much of the demand comes from restaurants and home cooks buying it by the litre for sauces, pricing across the whole category gets pulled down to meet that kitchen market, even for the better sipping-grade bottles like Superiore or Vergine, which age longer and taste noticeably more complex.

Marsala gets its punch from fortification: grape spirit is added to the base wine, pushing alcohol up to roughly 17 to 19 percent and stopping fermentation, which is part of why sweeter styles retain so much residual sugar.
