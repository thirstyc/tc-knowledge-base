How Wine Works

Fermentation

The chemical reaction that turns grape juice into wine: yeast eats sugar and produces alcohol and carbon dioxide. Temperature, oxygen, and a second fermentation called MLF all shape the final style.

What fermentation does

Fermentation is how grape juice becomes wine. Yeast eats the natural sugars in the juice (glucose and fructose) and turns them into alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving off heat as it works.

Roughly 17 grams of sugar per litre become 1% alcohol, so riper, sweeter grapes make stronger wine. Fermentation stops when:

  • the sugar runs out, leaving a dry wine;
  • the alcohol reaches around 15–16%, which most yeast can't survive;
  • or the winemaker stops it on purpose, by chilling, filtering or adding grape spirit, to keep some sweetness.

Yeast also makes small amounts of other compounds, like glycerol and fruity esters, which add texture and aroma. A lot of what we call a wine's "fruit" is built during fermentation.

Wild or cultured yeast

Almost all wine is fermented by one species, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same family used for bread and beer. The choice is where it comes from.

  • Cultured yeast: selected strains bought by the winemaker. They start quickly, finish reliably, and some are chosen to bring out particular aromas.
  • Wild or native yeast: the yeasts already living on the grapes and in the cellar. Several species start the job before Saccharomyces takes over.

Wild fermentations can add complexity and a sense of place, but they are less predictable: they can stall before all the sugar is used, or produce off-aromas. Neither choice is better in itself; many excellent wines are made both ways.

Temperature and time

Temperature is one of the winemaker's main controls, because fermentation makes its own heat.

  • Whites and rosés are usually fermented cool, around 12–18°C, over two to four weeks. Cool fermentation keeps fresh, fruity and floral aromas.
  • Reds are fermented warmer, around 20–30°C, usually for one to three weeks, on their skins. The heat helps extract colour, tannin and flavour.

Too hot and the yeast struggles and aromas cook off; too cold and fermentation can stop. That's why modern cellars use temperature-controlled tanks.

The second fermentation

After the main fermentation, many wines go through malolactic conversion. This isn't done by yeast but by bacteria, which turn sharp malic acid (the acid of green apples) into softer lactic acid (the acid of milk).

  • Almost all red wines go through it, which makes them rounder.
  • Some whites do, especially Chardonnay, where it can add a buttery note.
  • Crisp whites like Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc usually don't, to keep their freshness.

Sparkling wine has a different second fermentation: a second alcoholic fermentation in a sealed bottle or tank, which traps the carbon dioxide as bubbles.
