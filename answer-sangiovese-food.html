Why Does Sangiovese Require Food?

Short Answer

Sangiovese's tannin comes largely from its skins and stems, and Tuscan tradition ferments with a fair bit of skin contact, so the wine arrives grippy and drying, layered on top of acidity high enough to make your jaw ache if you sip it alone. Tannin binds to protein - that's not folklore, it's the same chemistry that makes tea taste less astringent with milk - so fat and protein in food soften the wine's grip while the wine's acid slices through the food's richness in return. It's a genuine feedback loop, which is why Sangiovese was essentially built around the Tuscan table rather than the other way around. Tomato-based dishes are a particularly good match because tomatoes carry their own acidity, so the wine doesn't taste sour beside them - it matches rather than clashes. Aged pecorino, cured meats, ragù, anything with rendered fat or a bit of char all work the same way. Drink it solo and you get all edges and no reward; put something salty and fatty in front of it and the wine reorganizes itself into something generous.
