Why does Vinsanto taste so sweet and complex?

Short Answer

Drying the grapes concentrates sugar, acid and glycerol before fermentation even starts, so you're working with must that's already syrupy. Fermentation then happens painfully slowly in small barrels over years, and eventually the rising alcohol stalls the yeast out, leaving natural sweetness behind rather than fortified sugar. Years of oxidative aging in wood add the complexity: nutty, caramelized, dried-fig and orange-peel notes from slow contact with oxygen and heat swings, not unlike sherry. There's also a rosé version, Occhio di Pernice, made the same way with Sangiovese. Serve it a touch cool, around 12 to 14°C, not fridge-cold, or you'll mute the toffee and dried-fruit layers. The classic move is dunking cantucci, almond biscotti, straight into the glass. Skip that and you're missing half the point of the wine.
