Why does Sherry taste so complex and dry?

Short Answer

Palomino, the base grape, is genuinely neutral and almost boring on its own, which is exactly why Sherry's flavour comes from what's done to it after fermentation rather than the grape itself. The wine is fortified with spirit to stop fermentation and stabilize it, then aged in one of two ways. Fino and Manzanilla age under a living film of flor yeast that shields the wine from oxygen while still transforming it, producing that dry, yeasty, almost bread-dough sharpness. Oloroso and Amontillado age oxidatively in barrels left partially empty on purpose, letting oxygen slowly caramelize the wine and build notes of walnut, dried fig, and toffee. Either way, the solera system blends multiple vintages together continuously, so complexity builds up layer by layer rather than coming from a single great year. Serve Fino and Manzanilla well chilled, around 7-9C, since warmth kills their freshness fast; Oloroso and Amontillado are better slightly warmer, around 12-14C, to let the deeper flavours open up.
