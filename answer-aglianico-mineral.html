Why Is Aglianico So Mineral and Structured?

Short Answer

Aglianico is one of Italy's oldest cultivated grapes, likely brought by Greek settlers over two thousand years ago, and it's grown mainly around Campania and Basilicata on volcanic soils shaped by Vesuvius and Mount Vulture. Volcanic soil is porous and mineral-rich, forcing vine roots to dig deep for water and nutrients, which concentrates flavor and gives the wine that distinctive smoky, ashy, iron-like minerality. The grape itself is naturally high in both acidity and tannin, thick skins packed with phenolic compounds that need a long growing season to fully ripen, which is why Aglianico is often harvested later than most Italian reds, sometimes into November. That late ripening under volcanic soil stress is what builds the wine's serious structure: firm tannin for aging, bright acidity for freshness, and a mineral backbone that keeps it from tasting simply fruity. It's frequently compared to Barolo for its ability to age decades, but the volcanic terroir gives it a distinctly different, smokier signature.
