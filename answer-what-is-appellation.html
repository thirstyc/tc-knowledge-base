What is appellation control?

Short Answer

An appellation is a legally defined wine region, and appellation control is the set of rules that go with it: where the grapes come from, which varieties are allowed, and often how the wine is made.

France's system, the AOC or AOP, was built in the early twentieth century largely to stop fraud, and the model spread. Italy has DOC and DOCG, Spain DO and DOCa, Portugal DOC, and the EU groups them under PDO.

Typical rules cover the boundary of the region, permitted grapes, maximum yields, minimum alcohol, and sometimes ageing requirements, like Rioja's Crianza and Reserva categories.

Most systems also have a looser tier, such as IGP in France or IGT in Italy, which allows grapes or methods the strict appellation forbids. Some superb wines sit in these categories by choice, the Super Tuscans being the famous example.

The United States uses AVAs, which define geographic boundaries but say almost nothing about grapes or methods.

The key thing to understand: an appellation guarantees origin and compliance with rules, not quality. There are dull wines inside famous appellations and brilliant ones outside them.
