Why does Argentinean Syrah taste so ripe and structured?

Short Answer

Syrah in Argentina is planted at some of the most extreme altitudes used for wine anywhere, including sites in Salta's Cafayate valley that push past 1700 metres, higher even than most Mendoza Malbec. At that elevation UV intensity is severe, so vines thicken their skins for protection, and thicker skins mean more tannin and colour extracted during fermentation, which is where the structure comes from. Daytime heat ripens sugars fully, giving the wine its dense, ripe fruit, while the sharp overnight temperature drop, sometimes over 15 degrees, locks in acidity that keeps the wine from tasting flabby despite its richness and often fairly high alcohol. This is the same mechanism behind Malbec's balance, just expressed through a grape that naturally runs firmer in tannin and spicier in aromatics. Producers have increasingly chased these extreme sites specifically because the resulting wines age well and show real complexity, which is why Argentinean Syrah's reputation has climbed steadily rather than staying a curiosity. Decant an hour before serving at 16 to 17 degrees to let the tannins soften.

What pairs with Argentinean Syrah?

Argentinean Syrah carries more black pepper and spice than its Malbec neighbour, largely thanks to a compound called rotundone that develops more readily in the grape under Syrah's typical ripening conditions, and that spice note is the key to pairing it well.
