What pairs with Mosel Riesling?

Short Answer

Mosel Riesling's trick is that it carries real sugar alongside searing acidity, and that combination is what makes it work with food that would flatten most wines. With spicy Thai, the touch of sweetness tames capsaicin's burn while the acid keeps the wine from tasting cloying or heavy against chili heat — dry wines just intensify the burn, which is why they're the wrong call here. With sushi, the acid mirrors the vinegar in the rice and the faint sweetness softens soy sauce's salt without dulling the fish's delicacy. Same logic with fresh trout: high acid cuts through any richness, low alcohol (Mosel Rieslings often sit around 8-10%) keeps the pairing light rather than boozy. Serve it cold, around 8-10°C, cold enough to keep the acidity feeling crisp rather than sharp. It's one of the few wines flexible enough to handle both delicate raw fish and aggressive spice in the same lineup, which is rarer than it sounds.

Why does Montérégie wine taste so approachable?

Proximity to the US border and Lake Champlain gives Montérégie a longer frost-free window than most of Quebec — the growing season starts earlier and ends later, which is the single biggest lever on how ripe and approachable a wine tastes.

The Mosel's steep, slate-covered slopes have been farmed for wine for roughly two thousand years in some spots, but it's the slate itself that matters practically: it absorbs heat during the day and radiates it back at night, helping grapes ripen on a river this far north where sun alone wouldn't be enough.
