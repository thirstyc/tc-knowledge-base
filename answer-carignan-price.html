Why is Carignan sometimes expensive?

Short Answer

It comes down to yield, not reputation. Carignan is naturally a heavy-cropping, workhorse grape, historically planted for bulk wine in the south of France and parts of Spain. But once vines pass fifty or sixty years old, they simply stop producing much — the root systems are deep, established, and the plant channels its limited energy into fewer, smaller berries instead of a big harvest. Less juice per vine means the grower has to spread the same fixed costs (land, labor, barrels) across a fraction of the fruit, and that gets priced in. What you get in return is real: old-vine Carignan loses its harsh, jammy edge and gains concentration, savory depth, and a kind of structural seriousness the young-vine version never has. So the expense isn't branding or scarcity marketing, it's arithmetic — tiny yields from ancient vines, sold at a price that actually reflects how little wine each vine gives up. Nobody's paying for a name here; they're paying for patience.
