Why is Turkey a genuine wine curiosity?

Short Answer

Anatolia has produced wine, in some form, for roughly 6,000 years — archaeologists keep finding grape residue in pottery shards that predates most of what we consider 'old world' wine culture. That alone makes Turkey a curiosity, but the real hook is what's happening now: a Muslim-majority country where wine consumption is modest, yet a small, genuinely serious industry persists, built almost entirely on grapes you won't find anywhere else. Boğazkere and Öküzgözü aren't international varieties dressed up in local packaging; they're indigenous survivors, adapted over millennia to Anatolia's high plateaus and continental swings. So you get two rare things stacked together — one of the oldest viticultural lineages on earth, and grapes with zero global footprint outside their home turf. Most wine regions offer either deep history or genuine novelty. Turkey offers both, largely untouched by the marketing machinery that's flattened so much of the wine world into recognizable, interchangeable styles.
