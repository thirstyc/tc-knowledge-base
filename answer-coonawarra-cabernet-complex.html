Why does Coonawarra Cabernet taste so structured and complex?

Short Answer

The structure comes down to geology and wind. Coonawarra sits on a narrow strip of terra rossa, red clay-loam over limestone, which drains well but keeps just enough moisture for slow, even ripening. More important is the climate: cool air off the Southern Ocean rolls in and creates a big swing between day and night temperatures. That diurnal range lets the grapes build sugar and flavour during the day while retaining acidity overnight, since acid degrades faster in heat. The result is fruit that ripens fully in flavour without turning flabby or jammy, which is what gives the wine its backbone. You'll often pick up a eucalyptus or mint note too, a real aromatic compound that drifts from nearby gum trees into the vineyard air and, some argue, into the grape skins themselves. Add firm tannins from that limestone-influenced fruit and you get a wine built for the table and for time in a bottle, not something to gulp on a hot afternoon.
