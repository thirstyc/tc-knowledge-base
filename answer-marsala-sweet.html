Why does Marsala taste so sweet and fortified?

Short Answer

Marsala gets its punch from fortification: grape spirit is added to the base wine, pushing alcohol up to roughly 17 to 19 percent and stopping fermentation, which is part of why sweeter styles retain so much residual sugar. The sweetness itself often comes from mosto cotto, grape must that's been cooked down until it's dark and caramelized, then blended in for colour and flavour as much as sugar. From there the wine ages oxidatively in wood, sometimes using a solera-style system that blends younger and older vintages, which is what produces those nutty, toffee, dried-fruit notes rather than fresh fruit character. Categories matter here: Fine is aged briefly and simplest, Superiore and Superiore Riserva spend longer in barrel and show more depth, and Vergine is unfortified-must-free and aged the longest, tasting drier and more sherry-like. Serve sweet Marsala around 12 to 14°C as a dessert wine on its own, or pull out a dry Vergine as an aperitif instead.
