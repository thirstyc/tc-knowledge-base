Why does South African Chenin Blanc fly under the radar?

Short Answer

Chenin got stuck with the wrong reputation for decades — it was the grape used for cheap bulk wine and brandy base, planted wherever, cropped high, asked to be nothing in particular. Meanwhile some of the oldest bush vines in the world, many dry-farmed and untrellised in Swartland and Stellenbosch, sat there quietly producing tiny yields of intensely concentrated fruit. No irrigation means the vine struggles, roots dig deep, and you get real complexity — waxy texture, quince, baked apple, a saline grip — without needing new oak to dress it up. Because Chenin never built Loire-level prestige or Sauvignon's easy name recognition, none of this got priced in. You're paying grocery-store money for wine made from vines older than most Napa Cabernet vineyards. Styles range from steely and unoaked to richer barrel-fermented versions closer to white Burgundy in weight. Drink it young for freshness or give the serious old-vine bottlings a few years — they hold up far better than the price tag suggests they should.
