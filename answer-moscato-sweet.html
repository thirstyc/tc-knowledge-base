Why does Moscato taste so sweet and low-alcohol?

Short Answer

The sweetness and low alcohol come from the same decision: fermentation gets stopped early. Winemakers chill the must and filter out the yeast before it can finish turning grape sugar into alcohol, so a good portion of natural sugar stays in the finished wine while alcohol lands around 5-7%, well below a typical dry white's 12-13%. The floral, grapey character isn't added, it comes from the Muscat family itself, one of the very few grape groups where the wine actually smells and tastes like the fresh fruit, thanks to a high concentration of aromatic terpene compounds. Moscato d'Asti, the classic version from Piedmont, gets a light, gentle fizz from a single tank fermentation rather than a second one like sparkling wine typically requires. None of this is corner-cutting, it's a precise style built around interrupting a natural process at exactly the right moment. Serve it cold, straight from the fridge, and drink it soon after buying, it's made for freshness, not the cellar.

What pairs with Moscato?

Sweetness is the mechanism here: it directly counters capsaicin heat, so Moscato is genuinely one of the better matches for Thai, Sichuan or anything with real chili kick, and the low alcohol (5-7%) means it won't fan the flames the way a bigger, boozier wine can.
