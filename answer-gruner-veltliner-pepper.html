Why does Grüner Veltliner taste so mineral and peppery?

Short Answer

That white pepper note isn't a metaphor, it's an actual aroma compound called rotundone, the same molecule responsible for pepperiness in cool-climate Syrah. Rotundone builds up more in grapes that ripen slowly under cooler nights, which is exactly what happens on the terraced vineyards above the Danube in Wachau and Kamptal, where cold air rolls off the river at night even as the days stay warm. The mineral streak comes from what those vines are rooted in — loess and gneiss soils that give a stony, saline edge rather than actual minerals you're tasting. Add in stone fruit like peach and a medium-to-high acid backbone, and you get Austria's calling card: a white that's savoury and structured rather than just fruity. Serve everyday Grüner around 8 to 10°C; the richer Smaragd-level bottlings from steep single vineyards deserve a touch warmer, 10 to 12°C, so the spice and texture aren't muted.
