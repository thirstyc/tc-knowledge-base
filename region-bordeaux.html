Bordeaux — overview

Bordeaux is the world's most famous fine-wine region, a large area in southwest France centered on the city of Bordeaux and shaped by the Gironde estuary and its two rivers, the Garonne and the Dordogne. It is a maritime climate — moderated by the Atlantic — which brings both the risk of unsettled weather at flowering and harvest and the gentle conditions that let its grapes develop slowly. Bordeaux makes dry reds (the great majority), dry whites, and some of the world's greatest sweet whites. Its wines are almost always blends, and its defining geographic split is Left Bank vs Right Bank.
Recommend when: the user wants a classic, ageworthy blended red, a benchmark to understand Cabernet or Merlot, or a great sweet wine (Sauternes).

The Bordeaux blend — red

Red Bordeaux is built from a core of Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, supported by Cabernet Franc and smaller amounts of Petit Verdot, Malbec, and Carménère. Blending is the point: it balances Cabernet's structure and ageability against Merlot's flesh and approachability, and provides insurance against vintage variation (the varieties ripen at different times). The proportions depend on which bank — and which soils — the wine comes from.
Recommend when: the user wants a structured, savory, food-friendly red with the capacity to age.

Left Bank — Cabernet Sauvignon country

The Left Bank (west of the Gironde/Garonne) has gravelly soils that suit Cabernet Sauvignon, which dominates its blends. The wines are firm, structured, tannic when young, with blackcurrant, cedar, and graphite notes, and they age for decades. Its key zones:

  • Médoc and Haut-Médoc — the broad northern peninsula.
  • Communal appellations of the Haut-Médoc: Saint-Estèphe (firm, structured), Pauillac (the most powerful and prestigious; home to several First Growths), Saint-Julien (balanced, classic), Margaux (perfumed, elegant).
  • Pessac-Léognan / Graves — south of the city; gravelly reds plus fine dry whites.

Serve at: 16–18°C, decant young wines.
Recommend when: the user wants the most structured, ageworthy, Cabernet-driven style of Bordeaux.

Right Bank — Merlot country

The Right Bank (east/north, around the Dordogne) has clay and limestone soils that favor Merlot and Cabernet Franc. The wines are plumper, softer, and approachable earlier than the Left Bank, though the best age superbly. Its key appellations:

  • Pomerol — small, clay-rich; produces the most opulent, velvety Merlot-based wines (Pétrus is its icon). No official classification.
  • Saint-Émilion — larger, historic, limestone-influenced; Merlot and Cabernet Franc; has its own classification led by Premiers Grands Crus Classés.
  • Satellite and outlying appellations (Lalande-de-Pomerol, the Saint-Émilion satellites, Fronsac, Castillon, Bourg, Blaye) offer better value in a similar style.

Serve at: 16–18°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a softer, rounder, plummier Bordeaux, approachable younger, or a Merlot-led red.

Bordeaux classifications

  • The 1855 Classification — ranked the top Médoc (plus Château Haut-Brion in Graves) reds into five tiers of Cru Classé, from First to Fifth Growth, based on price/reputation at the time. Still influential; largely unchanged. The First Growths are Lafite Rothschild, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion, and (promoted in 1973) Mouton Rothschild.
  • 1855 also classified Sauternes/Barsac sweet wines, with Château dYquem alone at the top (Premier Cru Supérieur).
  • Saint-Émilion has its own classification, revised periodically (Premier Grand Cru Classé A and B, Grand Cru Classé).
  • Pomerol has no classification at all — reputation is set by the market.
  • Cru Bourgeois — a separate tier for quality Médoc estates below the classified growths; good value.

Practical use: classification signals prestige and price tier, but the sommelier should stress that Cru Bourgeois and satellite appellations often over-deliver on value.

Dry white Bordeaux

Dry white Bordeaux blends Sauvignon Blanc (zip, citrus, grassy aromatics) with Sémillon (body, waxy texture, ageability), sometimes with a little Muscadelle. Styles range from crisp, unoaked, everyday whites (much labeled simply Bordeaux Blanc or Entre-Deux-Mers) to serious barrel-fermented whites from Pessac-Léognan that rival white Burgundy and age for years.
Serve at: 8–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a citrusy dry white for seafood (everyday style) or a textured, ageworthy white for richer dishes (Pessac-Léognan).

Sweet white Bordeaux — Sauternes & Barsac

Sauternes and its neighbor Barsac make some of the world's greatest sweet wines from Sémillon (mainly), Sauvignon Blanc, and Muscadelle affected by noble rot (Botrytis cinerea). The botrytis fungus shrivels the grapes and concentrates their sugars and flavors, giving lusciously sweet, honeyed, apricot-and-marmalade wines balanced by high acidity, capable of ageing for decades. Château dYquem is the benchmark. Neighboring appellations (Cadillac, Loupiac, Sainte-Croix-du-Mont) offer similar styles for less.
Serve at: 8–10°C.
Classic pairing: foie gras, blue cheese (Roquefort), fruit tarts.
Recommend when: the user wants a great dessert wine, or asks about foie gras or blue cheese pairings.

Entre-Deux-Mers and everyday Bordeaux

Entre-Deux-Mers (between the two rivers) is a large zone known mainly for crisp dry white. Much of Bordeaux's volume, though, is everyday red labeled Bordeaux or Bordeaux Supérieur — Merlot-dominant, soft, and for early drinking. Quality is variable, but good producers make honest, affordable claret.
Recommend when: the user wants an affordable, easy-drinking weeknight red or a crisp inexpensive white.

Bordeaux vintage sensitivity

Because of its maritime climate, Bordeaux is genuinely vintage-sensitive — weather at flowering and harvest makes or breaks a year, more so than in reliably warm regions. Great recent red vintages are talked about specifically (e.g. 2005, 2009, 2010, 2015, 2016). This matters for cellaring and buying: a modest vintage is for earlier drinking, a great one rewards patience.
Practical use: when a user is buying to age or spending seriously, vintage guidance matters more in Bordeaux than in most regions.

Quick regional summary bordeaux

  • Left Bank: gravel, Cabernet Sauvignon-led, firm and ageworthy; Médoc communes (Pauillac, Margaux, Saint-Julien, Saint-Estèphe) + Pessac-Léognan.
  • Right Bank: clay/limestone, Merlot-led, plusher; Pomerol and Saint-Émilion.
  • Whites: dry Sauvignon/Sémillon blends (crisp to serious); great sweet wines in Sauternes/Barsac.
  • Classifications: 1855 (Médoc + Sauternes), Saint-Émilions own, Cru Bourgeois for value; Pomerol unclassified.
  • Character throughout: blends, maritime climate, vintage sensitivity, ageworthiness.
