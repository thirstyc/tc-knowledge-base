Why does Vinho Verde taste so fresh and fizzy?

Short Answer

The fizz isn't a stylistic choice like Champagne's — it's a light natural spritz, traditionally from the wine being bottled very young while still holding a trace of dissolved CO2, or in cheaper commercial bottlings, a small dose of gas added just before bottling. Either way it's a prickle, not true sparkling pressure, and its main job is to sharpen the sensation of acidity on your tongue. The freshness itself is about place: the grapes — mostly Alvarinho, Loureiro, Arinto — grow in Minho, a cool, rainy, Atlantic-influenced corner of northern Portugal where fruit struggles to build much sugar or ripeness. Low sugar at harvest means low potential alcohol, often under 11%, and grapes that hang onto their malic acid rather than losing it to heat. Serve it very cold, 6-8°C, straight from the fridge — any warmer and the whole effect collapses, the fizz goes flat and the wine tastes thin instead of lively. This is not a wine that improves at room temperature, ever.
