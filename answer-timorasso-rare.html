Why is Timorasso hard to find outside Piedmont?

Short Answer

Timorasso is a genuine comeback story, not a marketing one. By the mid-20th century growers in the Colli Tortonesi had largely ripped it out in favour of Cortese, which ripens earlier, yields more generously, and is far easier to farm and sell young. Timorasso is finicky: low yielding, thin-skinned enough to rot in a wet year, and it needs bottle age to show what it can really do, which makes it a hard sell to a market that wants wine ready to drink now. It survived because a single producer kept replanting it through the 1980s when almost everyone else had given up, essentially betting his vineyard on a grape nobody wanted. Vines take three to five years before they produce anything worth bottling, and a genuine reputation takes decades, so even with renewed interest today the total planted area is still small and concentrated in a handful of hillside communes. That scarcity, not hype, is why bottles are hard to find and rarely cheap.

What pairs with Timorasso?

Timorasso's high acidity and saline, chalky texture exist to cut through fat, so pair it with things that have fat to cut.
