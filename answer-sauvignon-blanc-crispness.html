Why is Sauvignon Blanc so crisp?

Short Answer

Sauvignon Blanc keeps high acidity because it's usually picked early, grown in cooler places, and made without oak or softening techniques.

The grape naturally holds onto acidity as it ripens, and growers pick it before the fruit gets heavy, which keeps that zing intact.

Winemaking protects it too. Most Sauvignon Blanc ferments in stainless steel at cool temperatures, which preserves its aromas, and it usually skips malolactic conversion, the process that turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid. No oak, no lees stirring, nothing to round it off.

Its aromas add to the impression of crispness. Compounds called pyrazines give green, herbaceous notes of grass, green pepper and gooseberry, while thiols give grapefruit and passion fruit. Both read as "fresh" to most people.

Cool-climate regions bring it out best: the Loire Valley, Marlborough, coastal Chile and parts of South Africa.

That freshness is why it works with goat cheese, salads, herbs, asparagus and seafood, and why it's one of the few wines that can handle vinaigrette.

Most Sauvignon Blanc is best drunk within a couple of years, while that acidity and aroma are at their peak.
