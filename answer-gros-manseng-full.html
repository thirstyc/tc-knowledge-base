Why does Gros Manseng taste so fruity and full-bodied?

Short Answer

Gros Manseng is grown mainly in southwest France, around Gascony and Jurançon, and it's got thick skins that let it hang on the vine well into late harvest without rotting. That extended ripening is the key: sugar builds up steadily, pushing alcohol and body higher, while the grape's naturally high acidity holds firm underneath so the wine doesn't turn flabby. It's also aromatically loud, packed with terpenes that translate into tropical and stone fruit notes, pineapple, mango, ripe peach, sometimes with a waxy or honeyed edge depending on how ripe the fruit gets. Producers use it both for dry whites, where that late-harvest concentration reads as fullness and texture, and for the sweeter wines of Jurançon, where botrytis or simple overripening pushes it further. Serve the dry versions around 10-12°C, a touch warmer than a lean Sauvignon Blanc, so the fruit and weight actually show. Serve the sweet styles a little cooler still, closer to 8°C, to keep the sugar in balance.
