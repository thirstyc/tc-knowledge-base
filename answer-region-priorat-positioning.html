Why did Priorat go from forgotten to elite?

Short Answer

By the 1970s Priorat was nearly a ghost region, its terraced hillsides too steep and unproductive for modern farming, young people leaving, old vines abandoned. What changed it wasn't marketing, it was geology: a small group of winemakers realized that the same llicorella slate that made farming brutal was exactly what forced old Garnacha and Cariñena vines to produce tiny, intensely concentrated fruit. Low yields on punishing terrain, treated with real intent instead of neglect, turned out to make some of Spain's most powerful wines. Recognition followed the wine, not the reverse: Priorat earned DOCa status, the country's top classification, shared with only Rioja. Prices for its best bottles now rival Bordeaux and Barolo. It's a genuine rags-to-riches story, proof that a region written off as unfarmable can become one of the most sought-after on the map, provided someone's willing to work impossible slopes for very little juice.
