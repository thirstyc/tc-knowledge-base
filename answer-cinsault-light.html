Why is Cinsault so light and fruity?

Short Answer

Cinsault has thick skins, but they're naturally low in the tannin and colour pigments that give red wine its grip and depth, so however long you leave the juice in contact with them, you won't extract much structure — what you get instead is pale colour, soft texture and bright red fruit like raspberry and cranberry up front. It also holds onto acidity well even in genuinely hot climates, which is why it thrives in places like the Languedoc, Lebanon's Bekaa Valley and South Africa's Swartland, where old bush vines turn out something fresher than the heat would suggest. That combination of natural paleness and low tannin makes it a workhorse for rosé across southern France, and increasingly a base for juicy, chillable reds in their own right. Serve it a touch cool, around 13-14°C, to keep the fruit lively rather than warm and jammy. Don't expect it to age like Cabernet or Syrah — there's too little tannin to slow the wine down, so it's built for drinking within a few years, not cellaring for decades.
