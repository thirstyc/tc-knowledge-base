Why does Schiopettino taste so mineral and peppery?

Short Answer

Quick correction first: Schiopettino is a red grape, not a white, grown mainly in the Colli Orientali del Friuli near the Slovenian border. Its signature peppery character comes from a compound called rotundone, the same one responsible for the black pepper note in cool-climate Syrah, and it shows up more strongly when the grape ripens in cooler sites or slightly cooler vintages. The mineral, almost stony impression people pick up isn't really minerals dissolved into the wine, vines can't absorb rock that way, but the flavour language people reach for to describe high acidity meeting a savoury, non-fruity finish. Friuli's mix of marl and sandstone soils, called ponca locally, drains well and stresses the vines just enough to keep sugars and acids in balance rather than pushing pure ripeness. The result is a red with real spine: bright acidity, moderate tannin, dark fruit undertones, and that telltale peppery lift on the nose. Serve it around 16°C, a touch cooler than a big red, to keep that spice character sharp rather than blurred.
