Why does Malbec taste so dark and velvety?

Short Answer

Malbec has thick skins loaded with anthocyanins, the pigment molecules responsible for its near-opaque color, and long, already-polymerized tannin chains, which is why the tannin feels smooth rather than gritty compared to young Cabernet. In Cahors, its French birthplace, cooler skies slow ripening and keep a rustic, tobacco-leaf, earthy edge — historically the wine was even nicknamed vin noir for its density. Transplant it to Mendoza, often planted at 900 to 1500 metres, and the equation shifts: intense high-altitude sunlight builds pigment and sugar while cold nights preserve acidity, giving ripe plum and blackberry fruit without losing freshness. That combination — soft tannin, saturated color, ripe dark fruit, still-present acidity — is what reads as velvety rather than just heavy. Serve it around 16-17°C; too cold and the fruit shuts down, too warm and the alcohol takes over.
