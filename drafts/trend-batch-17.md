## 1. What is pet-nat wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-pet-nat`

Pét-nat is sparkling wine bottled before its first fermentation has finished, so the last of the bubbles form inside the bottle. The name is short for pétillant naturel, French for "naturally sparkling".

The technique is older than Champagne's. It's sometimes called the ancestral method, and it involves one fermentation rather than two: the winemaker bottles the wine partway through, and the remaining yeast and sugar create carbon dioxide with nowhere to escape.

Because the yeast stays in the bottle, most pét-nat is cloudy, and it's usually sealed with a crown cap, like a beer bottle. No corkscrew needed.

Expect softer, foamier bubbles than Champagne, lower alcohol, and flavours that lean fresh, yeasty and sometimes a little cidery or funky. Bottles vary more than conventional sparkling wine, which is part of the appeal and part of the risk.

It can be made from almost any grape, white, red or rosé, and it's a staple of natural wine bars.

Serve it very cold, open it over a sink since it can foam, and drink it young. Decide for yourself whether to pour the sediment or leave it behind.

## 2. What is minimal wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-minimal-intervention`

"Minimal intervention" describes winemaking that adds and removes as little as possible: no additives beyond a little sulfur, native yeasts, no fining or filtering, and nothing to correct the wine's acidity, colour or alcohol.

Conventional winemaking has a large toolkit. Cultured yeasts, added acid or sugar, tannin powders, colour adjustment, enzymes, fining agents and sterile filtration are all legal and widely used, and almost none of it appears on a label.

Minimal-intervention producers put those tools down, arguing that the wine should show the vineyard and the vintage, including the difficult ones.

The term overlaps with natural wine but isn't identical. It's a description of approach rather than a certified category, and plenty of conventional-looking producers work this way without using the phrase.

What you might notice: more variation between vintages, more texture, sometimes a slight haze, and flavours that lean savoury rather than polished.

The trade-off is risk. Less sulfur and no filtration mean the wine is more fragile, so storage and handling matter more.

It isn't automatically better or worse. It's a different set of choices, and the wine still has to taste good.

## 3. What is natural wine certification?

- **source_doc:** `qa-natural-wine-certification`

There's no global certification for natural wine, which is the main reason the category causes arguments. A few national schemes exist, but most producers simply describe their own practices.

The best-known scheme is France's "Vin Méthode Nature", introduced in 2020. It requires certified organic grapes, hand harvesting, wild yeasts, no additives and no heavy processing, with strict limits on sulfur. Wines that qualify carry a logo, with different versions for no added sulfites and for small amounts.

Other countries have producer associations with their own charters, but these are voluntary and vary in rigour.

Where certification does exist is in farming. Organic and biodynamic certifications are regulated, audited and mean something specific, but they cover the vineyard, not the cellar. A wine can be certified organic and still be heavily manipulated in the winery.

What this means in practice: for natural wine, the trustworthy signals are the producer, the importer and the shop, not a logo. People who taste what they sell are a better filter than any label.

If a bottle's methods matter to you, ask. Most natural producers are happy to explain exactly what they did.

## 4. Why are natural wines cloudy?

- **source_doc:** `qa-natural-wine-cloudy`

Because they aren't fined or filtered. Both processes strip out the tiny particles that make wine hazy, and natural winemakers usually skip them.

Fining uses an agent, like bentonite clay or egg white, that binds to particles so they can be removed. Filtering physically strains them out. Both give a bright, clear wine, and both remove some texture, aroma and flavour along the way.

Skipping them leaves yeast cells, proteins and fine sediment in suspension, so the wine looks hazy, especially when the bottle is shaken or poured to the last drop.

Cloudiness by itself says nothing about quality. It's a style choice, not a fault, and plenty of superb wines are cloudy.

When haziness is a warning sign: if the wine also smells of vinegar or nail-polish remover, tastes unexpectedly fizzy, or has a stale, mousy aftertaste, something has gone wrong.

Practical tips: stand the bottle upright for a day if you'd rather drink it clear, and pour gently, stopping before the sediment reaches the neck.

Serve these wines cool, and expect them to change in the glass more than conventional wines do.

## 5. Are natural wines worth the hype?

- **source_doc:** `qa-natural-wine-hype`

Some are excellent, some are faulty, and the category as a whole is neither a revolution nor a scam. It's worth trying with an open mind and a critical palate.

The case for: at its best, natural wine tastes vivid, energetic and distinctly of somewhere. Low-intervention winemaking can preserve character that heavy processing smooths away, and the movement has pushed the whole industry toward better farming and more transparency.

The case against: the absence of rules means quality varies wildly. Faults like mousiness, excessive volatile acidity and unwanted refermentation appear more often than in conventional wine, and some drinkers are told these are features rather than flaws.

A fair middle position: natural is a method, not a quality rating. A well-made natural wine is a joy; a badly made one is a bad wine, whatever its philosophy.

How to try it without disappointment: buy from a shop or bar that tastes what it sells, start with lighter, fresher styles like chillable reds and pét-nats, and don't be talked into liking something you don't.

The best natural wines don't need the label as an excuse.

## 6. Why are natural wines so expensive?

- **source_doc:** `qa-natural-wine-expensive`

Mostly because they're made in small quantities by people farming labour-intensively, with more risk of losing part of the crop or the batch.

The main cost drivers:

- Farming: organic and biodynamic viticulture needs more passes through the vineyard, more hand work and more labour, and usually yields less per hectare.
- Hand harvesting: natural winemaking almost always means picking by hand, which costs far more than machines.
- Scale: most natural producers are small, so they can't spread costs the way large wineries do.
- Risk: with little or no sulfur and no filtration, more batches fail or have to be sold off cheaply.
- Demand: fashionable producers sell out, and scarcity pushes prices up, sometimes well past what the wine costs to make.

It's worth separating those last two. Some natural wine is expensive because it's genuinely costly to produce; some is expensive because it's hard to get.

Where to find value: less fashionable regions like the Languedoc, southern Italy, Portugal and parts of Spain, where good low-intervention wine can still be found at everyday prices.

And plenty of conventional producers farm carefully without the premium, so the label isn't the only route to honest wine.

## 7. How to taste orange wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-taste-orange-wine`

Treat it like a light red, not a white. Orange wine has tannin, so it needs a slightly warmer serving temperature and food that can stand up to it.

Serve it cool but not cold, around 12–14°C. Ice-cold makes the tannins taste harsh and shuts down the aroma.

Use a normal wine glass with a decent bowl, and give it air. Many orange wines are tight when first opened and unfold over an hour, so don't judge the first sip.

What to expect: colour from pale gold to deep amber, aromas of dried apricot, orange peel, tea, nuts and sometimes a savoury, almost cidery note, and a grippy texture that surprises people expecting a white.

How to taste it: notice the texture as much as the flavour. That tannic grip is the point of the style, and it's what makes orange wine so good with food.

Pair it with bold dishes: curries, roast vegetables, aged cheese, charcuterie, tagines, anything with warm spice.

Starting out? Choose a short-maceration wine, which is fresher and gentler, before moving to long-macerated, deeply amber styles.

Some are cloudy and sedimented, which is normal for the category.

## 8. What is zero-alcohol wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-zero-alcohol-wine`

Zero-alcohol wine, also called dealcoholised or non-alcoholic wine, is made like ordinary wine and then has the alcohol removed.

The grapes are grown, pressed and fermented as usual. The alcohol is then stripped out, most often by vacuum distillation, which uses low pressure so the wine can be heated gently, or by reverse osmosis, which filters the wine under pressure. Spinning cone technology is another method.

Labelling varies by country. "Alcohol-free" usually means below about 0.05% alcohol by volume, while "de-alcoholised" or "low alcohol" can mean up to around 0.5%, similar to some fruit juices and breads.

The hard part is what removing alcohol takes with it: body, texture and much of the aroma. Producers often add grape juice, a little sugar or carbonation to compensate, which is why many taste sweeter than the wine they came from.

Quality has improved a lot, particularly for sparkling styles, where bubbles and acidity distract from the missing weight.

If you're avoiding alcohol entirely, check the label carefully, since 0.5% is not the same as zero.

## 9. Why are some wines vegan?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vegan-wine`

Because of fining, the process that clarifies wine before bottling. Traditional fining agents come from animals, so wines made without them, or with mineral and plant-based alternatives, can be labelled vegan.

Common animal-derived fining agents include egg white, casein from milk, gelatine, and isinglass, which comes from fish. They work by binding to particles in the wine so both can be removed, and they're filtered out before bottling, though traces can remain.

Vegan-friendly alternatives include bentonite, a clay, activated charcoal, pea protein and potato protein. Many producers also simply skip fining altogether, which is common in natural and low-intervention wine.

So the grapes were never the issue. It's a processing question, not an ingredient question.

Labels rarely explain any of this, since fining agents usually don't have to be declared. Some wines carry a vegan certification logo, and many retailers now tag vegan wines on their websites.

Bottles labelled "unfined and unfiltered" are usually vegan by default, though it's worth confirming.

If it matters to you, ask the shop or check the producer's website, which is often the quickest answer.

## 10. Best vegan wines?

- **source_doc:** `qa-best-vegan-wines`

Look for the ones labelled unfined and unfiltered, or carrying a vegan logo, rather than a particular region or grape. Vegan status depends on how a wine was clarified, not on what's in it.

Practical ways to find them:

- Look for "unfined and unfiltered" on the back label. Those wines are vegan by default in almost every case.
- Check for a vegan certification logo, increasingly common on supermarket bottles.
- Use retailer filters. Many online shops now let you filter by vegan.
- Ask at an independent wine shop, which will usually know or can check quickly.
- Look at natural and low-intervention producers, who often skip fining altogether.

Styles that are frequently vegan: many natural wines, orange wines, unfiltered reds, and traditional-method sparkling wines, where fining is less common.

What doesn't help: assuming organic or biodynamic means vegan. Those certifications cover farming, and biodynamic practices in particular can involve animal products in the vineyard.

The good news is that this has become far easier in the past decade, as more producers move to mineral and plant-based fining or skip it entirely.
