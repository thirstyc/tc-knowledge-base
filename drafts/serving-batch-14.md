## 1. What temperature to serve red wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-serving-temp-red`

Cooler than most people do: 14–18°C, not room temperature. "Room temperature" comes from an era of unheated stone houses, and a modern living room is far too warm for red wine.

Too warm and the alcohol dominates, the wine smells hot and the fruit turns flat. Too cold and the tannins feel harsh and the aromas shut down.

A rough guide:

- Light reds like Beaujolais, Gamay, Frappato and Lambrusco: 12–14°C.
- Medium reds like Pinot Noir, Sangiovese and Grenache: 14–16°C.
- Full reds like Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and Malbec: 16–18°C.

The easy trick: put a red in the fridge for 20–30 minutes before serving. It will warm up in the glass anyway, especially in a warm room or held by your hand.

In hot weather, err cooler still. A lightly chilled red on a summer evening is far more refreshing than a warm one.

If a red tastes harsh and closed, it may simply be too cold. Cup the glass for a couple of minutes and try again.

## 2. What temperature to serve white wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-serving-temp-white`

Chilled but not freezing: 8–12°C for most whites. Straight from a cold fridge, around 4°C, is too cold, and it hides everything the wine has to offer.

A rough guide:

- Light, crisp whites like Pinot Grigio, Muscadet and Vinho Verde: 7–9°C.
- Aromatic whites like Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Grüner Veltliner: 8–10°C.
- Fuller whites like oaked Chardonnay, Viognier and white Rioja: 10–12°C.
- Sparkling wine: 6–8°C.
- Sweet wine: 6–8°C, since cold keeps sweetness in balance.

The better the wine, the warmer it should be served, within those ranges. A serious white Burgundy served ice-cold is a waste.

Practical approach: take the bottle out of the fridge about 15 minutes before pouring, or serve it straight from the fridge and let the first glass warm up naturally.

If you're using an ice bucket, add water as well as ice. Water conducts cold far better than ice alone.

Pour smaller glasses more often instead of filling one to the top and watching it warm up.

## 3. How to chill wine quickly?

- **source_doc:** `qa-chill-wine-quickly`

Ice, water and salt in a bucket. It cools a bottle in about 10–15 minutes, far faster than a fridge or a freezer.

Fill a bucket or deep pot with half ice and half cold water, then add a generous handful of salt. Water transfers cold much faster than air, and salt lowers the temperature of the ice bath. Submerge the bottle up to its neck and give it a spin every few minutes.

Rough timings in an ice-water bath: about 10 minutes for a light chill, 15–20 for a cold white or sparkling wine.

Other methods that work:

- Wrap the bottle in a wet paper towel and put it in the freezer for 15 minutes. The evaporating water speeds things up.
- Use frozen grapes instead of ice cubes in the glass, so you don't dilute the wine.
- Pour into a chilled glass, which drops the temperature immediately.

What to avoid: leaving a bottle in the freezer and forgetting it. Wine freezes at around -5°C, and it can push the cork out or crack the bottle.

Planning ahead beats every trick: a couple of hours in the fridge is best.

## 4. How to open wine without a corkscrew?

- **source_doc:** `qa-open-without-corkscrew`

The shoe method works: put the bottle base inside a shoe, hold both, and tap the shoe firmly against a wall. The cork edges out gradually as pressure builds.

Do it against a solid wall, keep the bottle horizontal to the floor, and stop as soon as enough cork sticks out to pull by hand. Don't hit so hard that the bottle cracks.

Other methods, roughly in order of how much they'll worry your neighbours:

- Push the cork in. Use the handle of a wooden spoon and press steadily. Wine may splash, and you can't reseal it, but it always works.
- Screw a long screw most of the way into the cork with a screwdriver, then pull it out with the claw of a hammer.
- Wrap a towel around the bottle's base and tap it against a wall, the same principle as the shoe.
- Some people use a bicycle pump needle to push the cork out with air pressure. It works, but be careful with old or fragile bottles.

What not to do: heat the neck, or hit the bottle against anything hard enough to chip the glass.

If the cork pushes in and crumbles, pour the wine through a fine sieve.

## 5. Do wine glasses matter?

- **source_doc:** `qa-do-glasses-matter`

Yes, but less than the industry suggests. Shape and size affect how a wine smells and tastes, but you need two or three good glasses, not a cupboard full of shapes.

What genuinely helps:

- A bowl wide enough to swirl, which releases aroma.
- A rim that narrows slightly, to concentrate that aroma.
- Thin, clear glass, so you can see the wine and nothing gets in the way of the rim.
- Enough size to pour a normal serving into the widest part, roughly a third full.

What matters less: the dozens of grape-specific shapes. The differences are real but small, and they matter mainly if you're comparing wines closely.

The one shape worth reconsidering is the Champagne flute. It keeps bubbles longer but hides aroma. A normal white wine glass shows a good sparkling wine much better.

In practice, one decent universal glass, plus a slightly larger one for reds, covers almost everything.

Keep them clean and free of detergent smell, and store them upright so the rim doesn't take on cupboard odours.

Stemless glasses are fine, though your hand warms the wine.

## 6. How to store wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-how-to-store-wine`

Cool, dark, stable and away from vibration. Temperature swings do more damage than a cellar being a few degrees warmer than ideal.

The four conditions that matter:

- Temperature: around 12–14°C is ideal for long-term storage, but anything steady between 10 and 18°C is workable. Avoid swings.
- Light: UV damages wine. Keep bottles in the dark, and remember that dark green glass exists for exactly this reason.
- Humidity: around 60–70% keeps corks from drying out. Only relevant for long-term storage.
- Stillness: constant vibration, like on top of a fridge, is best avoided.

Lie bottles down if they have corks, so the cork stays in contact with the wine. Screwcapped bottles can stand up.

Places to avoid: above the fridge, next to the oven, in a sunny room, in an uninsulated garage or attic where summer heat can cook a bottle in days.

Good improvised options: an interior cupboard on a north-facing wall, the bottom of a wardrobe, a basement, or a box under the bed.

If you're keeping wine for years rather than months, a small wine fridge is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

## 7. How long does wine last after opening?

- **source_doc:** `qa-open-wine-how-long`

Most wine is good for 3–5 days after opening if you re-cork it and keep it in the fridge. Sparkling wine is the shortest-lived; fortified wine lasts the longest.

A rough guide, refrigerated and closed:

- Sparkling wine: 1–3 days with a proper sparkling stopper.
- Light whites and rosés: 3–5 days.
- Full-bodied whites: 3–5 days.
- Light reds: 3–5 days.
- Full-bodied reds: 3–5 days, sometimes better on day two.
- Port and sweet fortified wines: 1–3 weeks.
- Madeira and Fino Sherry: Madeira lasts months; Fino is best within a week.

Yes, put reds in the fridge too. Cold slows oxidation, and you can take the bottle out 30 minutes before you pour.

What helps: re-cork immediately, keep the bottle upright to reduce the surface touching air, and finish a half bottle rather than leaving a third of a full one.

Vacuum stoppers help a little. Argon gas sprays help more.

How to tell it's gone: the fruit flattens out and the wine smells of bruised apple, vinegar or sherry. It isn't harmful, just dull.

## 8. Does wine need to be stored horizontally?

- **source_doc:** `qa-store-bottles-horizontally`

Only if it has a natural cork and you're keeping it for more than a few months. Lying the bottle down keeps the cork moist, so it doesn't dry out, shrink and let air in.

For screwcapped bottles, and for glass or synthetic stoppers, it makes no difference. Store them any way you like.

For short-term storage, a few weeks or months, upright is also perfectly fine, even with a cork.

There's a second argument for lying bottles down: humidity. In a dry room, a cork in an upright bottle dries out faster. If your storage area is humid enough, around 60–70%, the risk is lower.

Practical reasons also favour it: bottles on their side stack neatly and take up less space, which is why wine racks are built that way.

One exception: Port and other bottles with a stopper-style cork are traditionally stored upright, because those corks aren't made for long contact with wine. Vintage Port, which uses a driven cork, is stored on its side.

If a bottle has stood upright for years, stand it up before serving anyway, so any sediment settles.

## 9. How to store wine without a wine fridge?

- **source_doc:** `qa-store-wine-no-fridge`

Find the coolest, darkest, most stable spot in your home and use that. An interior cupboard, the bottom of a wardrobe, a basement or a box under the bed all beat a kitchen wine rack.

What you're looking for is stability more than cold. A steady 18°C is kinder to wine than a cupboard that swings between 14 and 24°C.

Ideas that work well:

- A basement or cellar, if you have one. It's the classic answer for a reason.
- An interior closet away from exterior walls and heating vents.
- The bottom shelf of a north-facing cupboard, since heat rises.
- A polystyrene wine shipping box, which insulates surprisingly well, kept somewhere cool.

Places to avoid: above or beside the fridge, next to the oven or dishwasher, in a sunny room, in the garage or attic, and on top of the fridge, which is warm and vibrates.

Your kitchen fridge is fine for a few weeks but too cold and too dry for long-term storage, and its vibration doesn't help.

If you're mostly buying wine to drink within a few months, none of this is urgent. Any cool, dark cupboard is enough.

## 10. How to travel with wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-travel-with-wine`

Pack bottles in the middle of a suitcase, wrapped in clothes and sealed in plastic, or use a purpose-made wine sleeve or shipping box. Heat and breakage are the two risks.

For flights, wine must go in checked luggage: bottles are well over the liquid limit for carry-on. Duty-free bottles bought after security can be carried on, though a connecting flight may complicate that.

Practical packing tips:

- Wrap each bottle in a plastic bag first, so a breakage doesn't ruin your clothes.
- Pad with clothing on all sides, especially the neck and base.
- Keep bottles away from the suitcase edges.
- Inflatable wine sleeves and padded shipping boxes are cheap and much safer.

Check the rules for your destination. Countries and some regions limit how much alcohol you can bring in duty-free, and going over means paying duty.

By car, keep wine in the passenger compartment rather than the boot, which gets much hotter, and never leave bottles in a parked car in the sun. A few hours of heat can cook a wine.

After any journey, let bottles rest for a week or two before opening.
