## 1. Wine with Mexican food?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-mexican-food`

Match the wine to the dish: crisp whites for fresh, zesty food, off-dry whites for heat, and juicy reds for grilled meat and mole. Mexican cooking is built on acidity, spice and smoke, so wines with bright acidity and ripe fruit tend to work best.

Guacamole, ceviche and fish tacos want something crisp and citrusy: Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño or Verdejo.

Tacos al pastor, with pork, pineapple and chile, suit an off-dry Riesling or a fruity dry rosé.

Carne asada and grilled steak tacos can take a juicy red like Malbec, Zinfandel or Tempranillo.

Mole, rich with chocolate, dried chiles and spices, is a great match for ripe, spicy reds like Zinfandel, Grenache or Primitivo.

Enchiladas verdes and other dishes with tomatillo and green chile work well with Sauvignon Blanc or Grüner Veltliner.

Very spicy salsas and dishes call for lower alcohol and a touch of sweetness. Off-dry Riesling is the safest choice.

Worth knowing: Mexico makes wine too, mostly in the Valle de Guadalupe in Baja California. Its reds and whites are an easy, fun match for the food.

What to avoid: oaky whites and heavy, tannic reds, which clash with chile and lime.

## 2. Wine with cheddar cheese?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-cheddar`

It depends on the age of the cheddar. Mild cheddar suits a fruity white or light red, while aged, sharp cheddar can take something bolder: a structured red, a Tawny Port or a dry Sherry.

Mild, young cheddar is creamy and gentle. Try an unoaked Chardonnay, a Chenin Blanc or a light, fruity red like Pinot Noir or Beaujolais.

Aged cheddar is sharper, saltier and often has crunchy crystals. Its intensity can handle a medium to full-bodied red. Rioja Reserva, Cabernet Sauvignon and Zinfandel all work, because the cheese's fat and salt soften their tannins.

For a more interesting match, try Tawny Port or a dry Amontillado Sherry. Their nutty, caramel notes suit aged cheddar perfectly.

Traditional farmhouse cheddar from England is also great with English sparkling wine or a dry cider.

Smoked cheddar pairs well with smoky, spicy reds like Shiraz or Zinfandel.

Cheddar with apple or chutney, as on a cheese board, suits an off-dry Riesling or Chenin Blanc, which match the fruit.

What to avoid: very delicate wines with aged cheddar, which the cheese simply overwhelms.

## 3. Wine with Brie?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-brie`

Champagne or Chardonnay. Brie is soft, creamy and buttery, and it wants a wine with enough acidity to cut through that richness.

Champagne is a classic partner. Brie comes from just outside Paris, not far from the Champagne region, and the bubbles and acidity cleanse the palate after every bite. Other sparkling wines, like Crémant or English sparkling wine, work just as well.

Chardonnay is another natural match. A crisp Chablis cuts through the cream, while a lightly oaked white Burgundy echoes the buttery texture.

Prefer red? Keep it light and low in tannin. Pinot Noir and Beaujolais work; big, tannic reds can taste bitter and metallic against the rind.

Baked Brie, often served with jam, honey or nuts, is sweeter and richer. An off-dry Riesling, a Gewürztraminer or a fruity Beaujolais handles those flavours better than dry whites.

Truffle Brie pairs especially well with Champagne or a Pinot Noir.

Brie with fruit, like grapes, figs or pears, works well with Chenin Blanc or a light sparkling wine.

What to avoid: heavy, oaky reds and very tannic wines. They overpower the delicate cheese and clash with the rind.

## 4. Wine with cream sauce?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-cream-sauce`

A full-bodied white with good acidity, like Chardonnay, white Burgundy or Chenin Blanc. Cream sauces are rich and heavy, so the wine needs enough body to match them and enough acidity to keep each bite fresh.

Oaked Chardonnay is the classic choice. Its buttery, creamy texture mirrors the sauce, while a white Burgundy adds a little more acidity and elegance.

Chenin Blanc, especially from Vouvray or South Africa, balances richness and freshness very well. Viognier is another option for creamy dishes with a bit of spice.

The rest of the dish matters too.

Chicken or pork in cream sauce suits Chardonnay or Chenin Blanc.

Seafood in cream sauce, like scallops or salmon, pairs well with Chablis or a white Burgundy.

Mushroom cream sauce opens the door to red: Pinot Noir's earthy notes are a lovely match.

Creamy pasta, like fettuccine Alfredo, works well with a lightly oaked Chardonnay or an Italian white like Soave or Fiano.

A cream sauce with lots of herbs or lemon can take a crisper white like Sauvignon Blanc.

What to avoid: tannic reds. Tannin and cream clash, leaving the wine tasting bitter and the sauce heavy.

## 5. Wine with cake?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-cake`

A sweet wine that's at least as sweet as the cake. A dry wine next to a sweet dessert tastes sour and thin, so skip the Brut Champagne and reach for something with some sugar.

Light, fluffy cakes, like sponge, vanilla or birthday cake, pair well with Moscato d'Asti or a demi-sec Champagne. Their bubbles and sweetness keep things light.

Lemon cake suits Moscato d'Asti or a late-harvest Riesling, both with bright acidity to match the citrus.

Cheesecake is rich and tangy. Sauternes, late-harvest Riesling or Tokaji Aszú balance the creaminess.

Carrot cake, with its spices and cream cheese frosting, works well with a late-harvest Riesling, a sweet Chenin Blanc or Tokaji.

Fruitcake and Christmas cake are dense and full of dried fruit. Tawny Port, sweet Madeira or Pedro Ximénez Sherry match those flavours perfectly.

Chocolate cake calls for Banyuls, Ruby Port or a Late Bottled Vintage Port.

Coffee and walnut cake suits Tawny Port or a sweet Oloroso Sherry.

What to avoid: dry wines of any kind. Even a great bottle will taste harsh against a sweet dessert.

## 6. Wine with fruit desserts?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-fruit-desserts`

A sweet wine with plenty of acidity, like Moscato d'Asti, late-harvest Riesling or Sauternes. Fruit desserts are sweet and often tart, so the wine needs both sweetness and freshness to keep up.

Match the wine to the fruit.

Peach, apricot and other stone-fruit desserts pair well with Moscato, Sauternes or a late-harvest Riesling, which share the same stone-fruit flavours.

Apple and pear desserts, like tarte Tatin or apple crumble, suit a sweet Chenin Blanc from the Loire, such as Coteaux du Layon, or a Tokaji Aszú.

Berry desserts, like raspberry tart or strawberry shortcake, work beautifully with Brachetto d'Acqui, a sweet, lightly sparkling red from Piedmont.

Citrus desserts, like lemon tart, want a wine with bright acidity: Moscato d'Asti or a late-harvest Riesling.

Tropical fruit, like mango or pineapple, pairs well with late-harvest wines or ice wine, including the ice wines made in Canada's Niagara region.

Baked fruit with warm spices, like a plum crumble, suits a Tawny Port.

Fruit desserts with cream or custard can take a richer wine like Sauternes.

What to avoid: dry wines and heavy reds. They'll taste sour and bitter against the fruit.

## 7. Wine with BBQ sauce?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-bbq-sauce`

A ripe, juicy red with soft tannins, like Zinfandel, Shiraz, Malbec or Grenache. BBQ sauce is sweet, smoky and tangy, and it needs a wine with enough fruit to match that sweetness.

Zinfandel is the classic choice. Its jammy fruit and peppery spice pair beautifully with ribs and pulled pork.

Australian Shiraz and Argentine Malbec also work well, especially with beef.

The sauce style matters.

Sweet, thick sauces, like Kansas City style, pair best with very fruit-forward reds like Zinfandel and Shiraz.

Vinegar-based sauces, like Carolina style, are tangy and lighter. A juicy Beaujolais, a dry rosé or an off-dry Riesling handles the acidity well.

Texas-style brisket, often served with little sauce and lots of smoke, can take a more structured red like Syrah, Malbec or Cabernet Sauvignon.

Spicy BBQ sauces call for lower alcohol and some sweetness, so an off-dry Riesling or a fruity rosé is a smart pick.

Alabama white sauce, made with mayonnaise and vinegar, suits a crisp white like Sauvignon Blanc or an unoaked Chardonnay with chicken.

What to avoid: austere, very dry or delicate wines. The sweet sauce makes them taste thin and sour.

## 8. Best wine for a dinner party?

- **source_doc:** `qa-dinner-party-wine`

Plan one wine for each stage of the meal, and choose crowd-pleasers that work with lots of food: sparkling to start, a crisp white, a versatile red, and something sweet for dessert.

Start with bubbles. Champagne, Crémant or Cava welcome guests and pair with almost any starter.

For the white, pick something fresh and easy to like, such as Sauvignon Blanc, Chablis or an unoaked Chardonnay.

For the red, go for a medium-bodied, food-friendly wine that won't overwhelm the dish. Pinot Noir, Côtes du Rhône and Rioja Crianza are all good choices.

For dessert, a small pour of Moscato d'Asti, Sauternes or Tawny Port finishes the evening nicely.

How much to buy: a standard 750 ml bottle gives about five glasses. Plan on roughly half a bottle per person over a long dinner, and buy an extra bottle or two. Magnums look generous and are easy to share.

A few practical tips:

- Chill whites and sparkling wine in advance.
- Open reds 30 minutes before serving.
- Serve wines from lightest to fullest.
- Keep water on the table.

If guests bring wine, you don't have to open it that night. It's a gift, not a contribution to the menu.

## 9. Wine for Christmas?

- **source_doc:** `qa-christmas-wine`

It depends on what's on the table, but sparkling wine, Pinot Noir and a good red Bordeaux cover most Christmas dinners, with Port or Sauternes for dessert.

Start with something sparkling. Champagne, Crémant or English sparkling wine suits canapés, smoked salmon and the festive mood.

For turkey, pick a fruity, medium-bodied red like Pinot Noir or Beaujolais, or a rich white like Chardonnay.

For roast beef, choose a structured red: Bordeaux, Rioja Reserva or Cabernet Sauvignon.

For ham, which is sweet and salty, try an off-dry Riesling, a Beaujolais or a dry rosé.

For goose or duck, go with Pinot Noir or a northern Rhône Syrah.

For seafood, including the oysters served at a French Christmas Eve feast, Chablis or Champagne works beautifully.

Foie gras is traditionally paired with Sauternes, whose sweetness balances the rich liver.

For Christmas pudding and fruitcake, pour Tawny Port, sweet Madeira or Pedro Ximénez Sherry.

For the cheese board, Stilton with Port is a British Christmas tradition.

Buying for a crowd? Pick versatile, crowd-pleasing wines that work with several dishes, and make sure you have enough of each.

## 10. Wine for a picnic?

- **source_doc:** `qa-picnic-wine`

Light, fresh wines that taste good slightly chilled: rosé, sparkling wine, crisp whites and light reds you can chill. Picnic food is usually simple, and the wine should be easy to drink outdoors.

Rosé is the classic picnic wine. A dry Provence rosé suits sandwiches, salads, cheese and charcuterie.

Sparkling wine feels festive and works with almost any picnic food. Prosecco, Cava and pét-nat are easy choices, and pét-nat often comes with a crown cap, which means no corkscrew is needed.

Crisp whites like Sauvignon Blanc, Vinho Verde and Picpoul de Pinet are refreshing on a warm day.

Light reds, like Beaujolais, Gamay, Frappato and Lambrusco, taste great with a light chill and pair well with cold meats.

A few practical tips:

- Screwcaps and cans make life easier.
- Bring an insulated bag or a cooler with ice packs.
- Pack a corkscrew just in case.
- Use unbreakable glasses.
- Bring plenty of water.

What to avoid: heavy, high-alcohol reds and oaky whites. They feel heavy in the heat and don't taste their best warm.

Before you go, check whether drinking alcohol is allowed where you're picnicking. Rules vary a lot between parks and cities.
