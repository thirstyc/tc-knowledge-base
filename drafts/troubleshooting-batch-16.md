## 1. Why is my wine cloudy?

- **source_doc:** `qa-cloudy-wine`

Usually because it wasn't filtered, or because sediment got stirred up. Cloudiness on its own is not a fault, and plenty of good wine is deliberately hazy.

The common causes:

- Unfined and unfiltered wine: many natural and low-intervention wines skip these steps to keep texture and flavour, so they arrive cloudy by design. The label often says "non filtré" or "unfiltered".
- Disturbed sediment: older reds throw a deposit, and carrying or pouring the bottle roughly mixes it back in. Stand the bottle upright for a day and decant carefully.
- Protein haze: a harmless cloudiness in white wine, usually caused by heat during storage or transport.
- Cold: chilling can make tartrate crystals form. Those are little glassy crystals, not a haze, and they're harmless.

When cloudiness is a warning sign: if the wine also smells of vinegar, nail-polish remover or rotten eggs, or tastes fizzy when it shouldn't, something has gone wrong microbially.

The test is simple. Smell it and taste it. If it smells and tastes good, drink it.

## 2. Why does my wine smell bad?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-smells-bad`

Most bad smells come down to five culprits: cork taint, reduction, brettanomyces, volatile acidity and oxidation. Each has a distinctive signature.

Damp cardboard, wet dog or musty basement means cork taint, caused by the compound TCA. It won't improve, and the wine is a write-off.

Rotten eggs, struck match or burnt rubber means reduction, from too little oxygen. This one often fixes itself: swirl hard, or decant, and it frequently blows off within minutes.

Farmyard, horse blanket or band-aid means brettanomyces, a yeast. Small amounts add savoury complexity that some people like; large amounts flatten the fruit.

Vinegar or nail-polish remover means volatile acidity. Tiny amounts lift the aroma; obvious amounts are a fault.

Bruised apple, sherry or stale nuts means oxidation, from too much air, whether from a failed cork, bad storage or a bottle left open too long.

Not faults: the petrol note in mature Riesling, and a slight funk in natural wine that blows off with air.

If it's faulty and recently bought, take it back. Shops and restaurants generally replace them.

## 3. Why does my wine taste like vinegar?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-tastes-vinegar`

Because it's turning into vinegar. Acetic acid bacteria, which are everywhere in the air, convert alcohol into acetic acid when wine is exposed to oxygen.

The same bacteria are used deliberately to make wine vinegar, so this is the wine's natural fate if left open. It just isn't what you want in your glass.

Two related compounds are involved. Acetic acid gives the sharp, vinegary taste. Ethyl acetate, produced alongside it, smells of nail-polish remover or solvent.

Common causes: an open bottle left too long, a faulty or dried-out cork that let air in, poor storage, or occasionally a fault that developed during winemaking.

Small amounts of volatile acidity are normal and can even add aromatic lift. What you're tasting is when it goes well past that.

There is no way to reverse it. Once the acetic acid is there, it stays.

What you can do: use it in cooking, where a splash of slightly sharp wine does no harm in a stew or a braise, or let it go all the way and make vinegar with it.

To prevent it: re-cork promptly, keep open bottles in the fridge, and store unopened bottles cool and away from air.

## 4. Why did my wine go bad?

- **source_doc:** `qa-why-wine-went-bad`

Heat, oxygen, light and time are the four enemies. Heat is the most common and the most damaging.

Heat is the usual culprit. Above roughly 21°C, wine ages fast and badly, and a hot car, a sunny room or an uninsulated garage can cook a bottle in a single afternoon. Telltale signs: a cork pushed up past the capsule, sticky residue at the neck, and a stewed, flat taste.

Oxygen gets in through a faulty, dried-out or failed cork, and turns wine brown and sherry-like. Storing corked bottles upright for long periods makes this more likely.

Light, especially UV, damages wine over time. That's why bottles are made of dark glass, and why a bottle stored on a sunny windowsill fades fast.

Time is the quietest one. Most wine is made to drink young, so a forgotten bottle at the back of a cupboard may simply be past its best.

None of this is reversible. What you can control is storage: cool, dark, stable and away from vibration.

If a bottle you just bought is faulty, take it back.

## 5. Is this wine safe to drink?

- **source_doc:** `qa-is-wine-safe`

Almost always yes. Wine that has gone bad tastes unpleasant, but the alcohol and acidity make it a hostile environment for the bacteria that cause food poisoning.

A corked, oxidised or vinegary wine is unpleasant rather than dangerous. You can cook with it or pour it away, but you won't get ill from a sip.

The situations where you should be more careful:

- Visible mould inside the bottle or on the wine's surface, rather than on the outside of the cork.
- A bottle that has been opened and left at room temperature for weeks, especially a low-alcohol or sweet one.
- Any bottle where the seal has clearly failed and the contents may have been exposed to contamination.
- A bottle that fizzes unexpectedly and smells actively foul, which can indicate a refermentation.

Mould on the outside of a cork is normal in a damp cellar and doesn't affect the wine.

When in doubt, use the ordinary food rule: if it smells genuinely foul rather than just disappointing, don't drink it.

If you have a specific health concern, ask a doctor rather than relying on general guidance.

## 6. How to fix a broken cork?

- **source_doc:** `qa-broken-cork`

If the cork breaks halfway, re-insert the corkscrew at an angle into the remaining piece and pull gently and straight. Angling the worm gives it more cork to grip.

If the cork is crumbling or too far down to grip, push it into the bottle instead. Use the handle of a wooden spoon and press steadily. Wine may splash, so hold the bottle over the sink, and don't expect to reseal it.

Once cork pieces are in the wine, pour it through a fine sieve, a coffee filter or muslin into a decanter or jug. Small floating pieces are harmless, just unpleasant to drink.

Prevention helps more than any rescue:

- Use a two-pronged "Ah-So" opener for old or fragile corks. It grips the sides rather than piercing the middle.
- Insert the worm centrally and all the way in before pulling.
- Pull slowly and straight, not at an angle.
- Watch out for very old bottles, where corks dry and crumble.

A cork breaking says nothing about the wine's quality; it usually means the cork was old or dry.

## 7. Wine opened early — is it still good?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-opened-early`

Almost certainly, yes. A bottle opened a day or two ago and kept sealed in the fridge is usually fine, and some wines taste better on the second day.

Most wine keeps three to five days after opening if you re-cork it and refrigerate it, including reds. Sparkling wine is the exception, lasting one to three days with a proper stopper.

Why some wines improve: air softens firm tannins and opens up tight aromas. Young, structured reds like Barolo, Bordeaux and Syrah often show better after a few hours or even a day.

Which ones fade fastest: delicate older wines, light whites and anything sparkling. If you've opened an old bottle, drink it that evening.

How to tell it's still good: smell it. If the fruit is still there, it's fine. If it smells of bruised apple, vinegar or sherry, it's past it.

To buy more time, re-cork immediately, refrigerate, and consider a vacuum stopper or an argon gas spray.

And for anything that has faded past drinking, cooking is a perfectly good second act.

## 8. Wine stored incorrectly — is it ruined?

- **source_doc:** `qa-badly-stored-wine`

Maybe, but often not. It depends on how bad the conditions were and for how long. A few warm weeks is survivable; a summer in a hot attic usually isn't.

Signs a bottle has been heat-damaged:

- The cork is pushed up, or sits proud of the bottle top.
- Sticky residue or seepage around the neck and capsule.
- The wine looks browner than it should for its age.
- It tastes stewed, flat and dull, with the fresh fruit gone.

Signs it's probably fine: the fill level looks normal for the age, the capsule is clean, and the wine smells and tastes fresh.

What matters most is the swing, not the average. Steady 18°C is kinder than a cupboard cycling between 12 and 26°C.

Nothing reverses heat damage. But a bottle that got warm for a while and still tastes good is fine to drink; wine is more robust than the folklore suggests.

The practical lesson is about where you keep the next bottles: a cool, dark, stable cupboard beats any rack in a warm kitchen.

## 9. Can you fix oxidized wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-fix-oxidized-wine`

No. Oxidation can't be reversed, and once a wine tastes of bruised apple, stale nuts or sherry, that's where it stays.

Oxidation happens when oxygen reacts with the wine, turning whites deep gold and reds brown, and stripping out the fresh fruit. It's the same process that browns a sliced apple.

Things that don't work: adding fresh wine, refrigerating it, using a vacuum stopper afterwards, or decanting. Decanting adds air, which is exactly the wrong direction.

What you can do is stop it getting worse: re-cork, refrigerate, and finish the bottle soon.

What it's still good for: cooking. Slightly oxidised wine is fine in stews, braises, risotto or a pan sauce. Sangria and mulled wine also hide a multitude of sins.

For next time: re-cork straight after pouring, keep open bottles in the fridge, use a vacuum stopper or argon spray, and pour into a half bottle if you've only drunk half.

One exception worth knowing: some wines are deliberately oxidative, including Sherry, Madeira, Vin Jaune and some orange wines. That character is the point, not a fault.

## 10. Why does wine have sediment?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-sediment`

Sediment is a natural by-product of ageing or of skipping filtration. It's harmless, and in an older red it's usually a good sign.

Three common kinds:

- Colour and tannin sediment: in red wines, tannins and colour compounds gradually bond and fall out of solution. That's why old reds taste softer and leave a dark deposit.
- Tartrate crystals: small, clear, glass-like crystals sometimes called "wine diamonds", formed when tartaric acid precipitates in the cold. Common in whites, entirely harmless.
- Lees and particles: unfiltered and unfined wines, including many natural wines, keep fine particles that settle out in the bottle.

None of it is dangerous. It just tastes gritty and bitter, so you want to leave it in the bottle.

How to handle it: stand the bottle upright for a day so the sediment settles to the bottom, then pour slowly and steadily into a decanter, stopping when you see sediment reach the neck. A light behind the bottle makes this easy.

Filtering a wine heavily removes sediment but can strip texture and flavour too, which is why many producers filter lightly or not at all.
