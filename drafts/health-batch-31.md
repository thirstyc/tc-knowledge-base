## 1. Wine and diabetes?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-diabetes`

Many people with diabetes can drink wine in moderation, but it's important to talk to your doctor first, because alcohol affects blood sugar and interacts with some diabetes medications.

The main concern is low blood sugar. The liver prioritises breaking down alcohol, which reduces how much glucose it releases. For people taking insulin or certain other medications, this can cause hypoglycaemia, sometimes many hours after drinking, even overnight.

Practical points often recommended:

- Never drink on an empty stomach; have wine with a meal.
- Choose dry wines. A glass of dry red or white contains very little sugar, usually a gram or two, while sweet and dessert wines contain much more.
- Keep to moderate amounts, and check your blood sugar more often, including before bed.
- Be aware that the symptoms of low blood sugar, like dizziness and confusion, can be mistaken for being drunk. Tell the people you're with, and carry medical identification.
- Remember that wine also adds calories, which matters for weight management.

Alcohol can also worsen some diabetes complications, like nerve damage and high blood pressure.

Everyone's situation is different. Your doctor or diabetes educator can tell you whether wine is safe for you and how much.

## 2. Wine and weight loss?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-weight-loss`

Wine can fit into a weight-loss plan in small amounts, but it makes losing weight harder. It adds calories without filling you up, and it tends to increase appetite and lower willpower around food.

A standard 150 ml glass of dry wine at about 13% alcohol contains roughly 120–130 calories, mostly from the alcohol itself, which provides 7 calories per gram, almost as much as fat. Sweet and fortified wines contain more.

Other ways wine affects weight loss:

- Your body burns alcohol first, pausing fat burning while it does.
- Alcohol stimulates appetite and lowers restraint, which is why snacks disappear during a second glass.
- It disrupts sleep, and poor sleep is linked to hunger and weight gain.

If you want to keep enjoying wine while losing weight:

- Set a limit, such as a glass on a few days a week, and have alcohol-free days.
- Choose dry, lower-alcohol wines, like Vinho Verde, German Riesling Kabinett or a dry rosé.
- Pour smaller glasses and sip slowly with food.
- Try a spritzer with sparkling water.
- Count wine in your daily calories.

Cutting back on alcohol is one of the simplest changes for many people trying to lose weight.

## 3. Wine and metabolism?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-metabolism`

Your body treats alcohol as a priority to eliminate. The liver breaks it down at a fairly fixed rate, roughly one standard drink per hour, and puts other metabolic work, like burning fat, on hold while it does.

How alcohol is metabolised:

- Most is broken down in the liver, first into acetaldehyde, a toxic compound, then into acetate, which the body uses for energy or eliminates.
- A small amount leaves through breath, sweat and urine.

The rate varies from person to person. Body size, sex, genetics, liver health, and whether you've eaten all play a part. Some people, especially of East Asian descent, have a genetic variant that slows the breakdown of acetaldehyde, causing facial flushing and discomfort.

Nothing speeds it up much. Coffee, cold showers and exercise may make you feel more alert, but they don't clear alcohol faster. Only time does.

Food slows how quickly alcohol enters your bloodstream, which lowers peak blood alcohol levels. That's one reason drinking wine with meals is gentler than drinking on an empty stomach.

Alcohol also affects metabolism indirectly: it adds calories, disrupts sleep, can lower blood sugar and acts as a diuretic.

If you have liver disease or take medications, ask your doctor how alcohol may affect you.

## 4. Does wine cause bloating?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-bloating`

It can. Alcohol irritates the digestive tract, and wine can cause bloating in some people through gas, fluid shifts and the salty food that often comes with it.

Common reasons:

- Irritation: alcohol can inflame the stomach lining and slow digestion.
- Bubbles: sparkling wine adds carbon dioxide to your stomach.
- Sugar and fermentable carbohydrates: sweet wines can ferment in the gut and cause gas, especially in people with sensitive digestion.
- Dehydration: alcohol makes you lose fluid, and the body may then hold on to water, leaving you puffy.
- Food: wine often comes with salty snacks, cheese and rich meals, which cause bloating on their own.
- Histamine and other compounds, to which some people are sensitive.

What can help:

- Drink water alongside wine.
- Choose dry, still wines if bubbles or sugar bother you.
- Eat slowly, and go easy on salty snacks.
- Keep to smaller amounts.

If bloating is frequent, painful, or comes with other symptoms like pain, changes in bowel habits or weight loss, see a doctor. It may be a sign of a digestive condition, such as irritable bowel syndrome or a food intolerance.

## 5. Wine and migraines?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-migraines`

Wine, especially red wine, is one of the most commonly reported migraine triggers, but not for everyone, and the exact cause isn't fully understood.

Possible culprits that researchers have looked at:

- Alcohol itself, which widens blood vessels and causes dehydration.
- Phenolic compounds and flavonoids from grape skins, which are more abundant in red wine.
- Histamine, higher in some red wines, which some people break down less efficiently.
- Tyramine and other compounds, though levels in wine are generally low.

Sulfites are often blamed, but there's little evidence they cause migraines. White wine usually contains more sulfites than red, yet red wine is the more commonly reported trigger.

If you get migraines:

- Keep a headache diary noting what you drank, how much, and what else happened. Triggers often combine, such as wine plus stress, poor sleep or skipped meals.
- If red wine seems to be a trigger, try a small amount of white, rosé or sparkling wine instead to see if it makes a difference.
- Drink water, eat, and keep to small amounts.

Talk to your doctor or a headache specialist about your triggers and treatment. Some migraine medications also interact with alcohol.

## 6. Wine and allergies?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-allergies`

True allergy to wine is rare, but many people react to something in it. The usual suspects are sulfites, histamine, grape proteins, or residues from fining agents.

Possible causes of reactions:

- Sulfites: most people tolerate them, but a small number, particularly some people with asthma, are sensitive. That's why labels in many countries say "contains sulfites".
- Histamine: produced during fermentation, and higher in some red wines. People who break down histamine poorly may get flushing, a stuffy nose, headaches or hives.
- Grape proteins: rarely, people are genuinely allergic to proteins in grapes.
- Fining agents: egg, milk and fish products are sometimes used to clarify wine. Tiny traces may remain, so some regions require them on labels.
- Alcohol flush: facial redness caused by a genetic difference in breaking down alcohol, which isn't an allergy but is often mistaken for one.

What can help:

- Note which wines cause problems. Many people react to red wine but not white, or the reverse.
- Try vegan wines, made without animal-derived fining agents, if you have egg, milk or fish allergies.
- See an allergist, who can test for specific sensitivities.

Seek emergency help for severe symptoms such as swelling of the lips or throat, difficulty breathing or dizziness.

## 7. How to pair wine with a healthy diet?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-healthy-diet`

If you drink wine, keep it moderate, have it with meals, and match it to fresh, whole foods, much like the Mediterranean way of eating.

Health guidance has become more cautious in recent years. The World Health Organization says no level of alcohol is completely risk-free, and Canada's 2023 guidance considers two drinks or fewer per week low risk. So wine is best seen as a pleasure to enjoy in small amounts, not a health food. Nobody should start drinking for health reasons.

Practical habits:

- Drink with food, never on an empty stomach.
- Keep portions small: a standard glass is about 150 ml, less than many people pour.
- Have several alcohol-free days each week.
- Choose lower-alcohol wines, around 11–12.5%.
- Drink water alongside.

Wine-friendly healthy pairings:

- Grilled fish and vegetables with crisp whites like Albariño or Sauvignon Blanc.
- Legume dishes, like lentil stews, with light reds such as Pinot Noir or Gamay.
- Salads with goat cheese and Sancerre.
- Roast chicken and vegetables with Chardonnay or a light red.
- Tomato-based dishes with Chianti or Barbera.

Savouring one good glass slowly with a meal is more rewarding, and healthier, than several quick ones.

If you have health conditions or take medication, ask your doctor what's right for you.
