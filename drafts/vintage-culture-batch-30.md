## 1. Best Burgundy vintages?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vintages-burgundy`

Burgundy's weather varies a lot from year to year, so vintage matters more here than in most regions. Among recent decades, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2019 and 2020 stand out for reds, with 2014 and 2017 especially admired for whites.

Widely praised vintages:

- Reds: 1990, 1999, 2002, 2005, 2009, 2010, 2015, 2019 and 2020. 2005 and 2010 are classic, long-lived years; 2009, 2015 and 2020 are riper and more generous.
- Whites: 2014 and 2017 for tension and freshness, plus 2020 and 2022.

More difficult years include 2004, 2011, 2013 and 2021, when rain, hail or frost reduced quality or quantity. They can still be lovely from good producers, and often drink well earlier and cost less.

How to use vintages in Burgundy:

- The producer matters more than the year. A great grower makes good wine in difficult vintages.
- Lighter vintages are often ready sooner. Ripe, structured years need more time.
- "Off" vintages are great value for drinking now.
- White Burgundy from roughly the mid-1990s to the late 2000s carries some risk of premature oxidation, so buy older whites carefully.

Vintage charts are a starting point, not a verdict. A good merchant can tell you how specific wines are drinking now.

## 2. Best Bordeaux vintages?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vintages-bordeaux`

Bordeaux has had a remarkable run of strong vintages. Among the most celebrated are 2000, 2005, 2009, 2010, 2015, 2016, 2019, 2020 and 2022, with 1982 and 1990 the great older benchmarks.

A quick guide:

- 2005 and 2010: classic, structured, very long-lived.
- 2009 and 2015: ripe, generous and approachable.
- 2016: balanced, fresh and widely considered outstanding.
- 2019, 2020 and 2022: a strong trio from warm years, with 2022 notable for concentration despite a hot, dry summer.

More challenging years include 2011, 2012, 2013 and 2017, while 2021 was lighter and fresher. They're often better value and ready to drink sooner.

How to use vintages in Bordeaux:

- Left Bank (Cabernet-based) and Right Bank (Merlot-based) can fare differently in the same year, since the grapes ripen at different times.
- Top wines from great vintages need ten to twenty years or more. Lesser vintages and simpler wines can be enjoyed much earlier.
- For everyday Bordeaux, vintage matters less; buy recent, good years and drink them young.

Great vintages come with great prices. For value, look at good wines from lighter years, or second wines from top estates in great ones.

## 3. Best Champagne vintages?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vintages-champagne`

Most Champagne is non-vintage, blended across years for a consistent style. Vintage Champagne is only made in years good enough to stand alone. Among the most celebrated recent ones are 1996, 2002, 2008 and 2012.

Standout years:

- 1996: famously high in both ripeness and acidity, long-lived.
- 2002: rich, balanced and widely loved.
- 2008: taut, high-acid and exceptionally age-worthy, often called one of the greats.
- 2012: small but excellent, with concentration and freshness.
- 2013, 2015 and 2019 are also well regarded, with 2019 showing great promise.

Vintage Champagne is aged much longer before release than non-vintage, usually at least three years on its lees and often far more. Many are released six to ten years after harvest, and the best keep improving for decades.

How vintage Champagne changes: young, it shows citrus, apple and brioche; with age, it develops toast, honey, hazelnut and mushroom, with softer bubbles.

Tips:

- Not every house declares every good year, so vintage quality varies by producer.
- Grower Champagnes can show vintage character more directly.
- Store it on its side, somewhere cool and dark, and serve it slightly warmer than non-vintage, around 10–12°C.

## 4. Best Riesling vintages?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vintages-riesling`

For German Riesling, which is most influenced by vintage, widely admired years include 2001, 2008, 2015, 2017, 2019 and 2021, each with a slightly different character.

A rough guide for Germany:

- 2001: a classic older benchmark, balanced and long-lived.
- 2008 and 2021: cool years with high, vibrant acidity, great for purists and for ageing.
- 2015 and 2019: ripe yet fresh, with excellent concentration across styles.
- 2017: small, frost-affected, but high quality.

Hot years, like 2003 and 2018, gave riper, softer wines, often great for sweet styles but less tense in dry ones.

Riesling is less dependent on vintage in steady climates. Australia's Clare and Eden valleys, Alsace and Austria vary, but less dramatically.

How to use vintages with Riesling:

- High-acid, cool vintages age longest and can taste severe when young.
- Ripe vintages are more approachable early.
- Sweet styles like Auslese and above are made in quantity only in years with the right conditions for extra ripeness or noble rot.
- Producer and site still matter most.

Since Riesling ages so well, older vintages from good producers are often a bargain compared with red wines of similar age and quality.

## 5. Best Pinot Noir vintages?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vintages-pinot-noir`

It depends on the region, since Pinot Noir is grown in very different climates. For Burgundy, its home, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2019 and 2020 stand out. For the New World, vintage matters less, but cool, balanced years are often the most admired.

Pinot Noir is especially sensitive to weather. Its thin skins make it vulnerable to rain, rot and heat, so small differences in a season show clearly in the glass.

By region:

- Burgundy: 2005, 2010, 2015, 2019 and 2020 are highly regarded, with 2009 and 2018 riper and more generous.
- Oregon: 2008, 2012 and 2014 are among the most widely praised.
- New Zealand and California: more consistent climates, so producer and site matter more than the year.

General patterns:

- Warm years give riper, fuller, more immediately charming wines.
- Cool years give lighter, more fragrant, higher-acid wines that often age longer.
- Wet years can bring dilution and rot, though good producers sort grapes carefully.

For drinking now, lighter vintages are often at their best sooner, and cheaper. For cellaring, choose balanced years from good producers.

A trusted shop's current advice is more useful than any chart.

## 6. What is wine pairing dinner?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-pairing-dinner`

A wine pairing dinner is a meal where each course is served with a wine chosen to match it, usually planned by the chef and sommelier together, and often featuring one winery, region or theme.

They're held at restaurants, wineries and wine shops, and often include a winemaker or sommelier who introduces each wine.

A typical format:

- A welcome glass, often sparkling.
- Four to six courses, each with its own wine.
- Smaller pours than usual, around 75–100 ml per course.
- Short explanations of why each wine was chosen.

Why go:

- You learn how food changes wine, and vice versa, in a way that's hard to grasp in theory.
- You try several wines without buying full bottles.
- It's social and relaxed, with shared tables and conversation.

Tips:

- Pace yourself. Five small glasses still add up; drink water and don't feel obliged to finish every pour.
- Tell the organisers about dietary needs in advance.
- Plan a safe way home.

You can host one at home too: pick a theme, cook three or four simple courses, and pour a different wine with each. Try one course with two wines side by side to see how pairing works.

Many restaurants also offer a pairing option with their tasting menu.

## 7. What is a wine bar?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-wine-bar`

A wine bar is a bar focused on wine, usually with a large selection by the glass, knowledgeable staff and small plates to share. It's one of the best places to taste widely without buying whole bottles.

What sets a good wine bar apart:

- A long by-the-glass list, often with tasting pours or flights of several small glasses.
- Staff who can describe wines and recommend something you'll like.
- Wine-friendly food: cheese, charcuterie, bread, olives and small dishes.
- A selection that changes often, so there's always something new.

Wine bars come in many styles, from classic French and Italian enotecas to modern natural wine bars focused on small producers and skin-contact wines.

Many use preservation systems that let them pour expensive bottles by the glass, a chance to try wines you'd rarely open at home.

How to make the most of a visit:

- Order a flight to compare grapes or regions side by side.
- Tell the staff what you usually enjoy and let them surprise you.
- Take a photo of labels you like.
- Ask what just opened.

Some wine bars also sell bottles to take home, and many charge a reasonable price to drink a bottle on site.

## 8. What is club soda wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-spritzer`

It's usually called a spritzer: wine mixed with club soda or sparkling water. It's lighter, lower in alcohol and very refreshing, especially in hot weather.

The classic is white wine with soda, in roughly equal parts or two parts wine to one part soda, served over ice. Adjust to taste.

Why people love it:

- Lower alcohol per glass, great for long afternoons.
- Fewer calories than a full glass of wine.
- Very refreshing.

Versions from around the world:

- Austria and Germany: the "Gespritzter" or "Weinschorle", often with Grüner Veltliner or Riesling, served in large glasses.
- Hungary: the "fröccs", with many named ratios of wine to soda.
- Italy: the Spritz, which adds a bitter liqueur to sparkling wine and soda.
- Red wine versions: red wine with soda or lemon soda, served over ice in Spain as "tinto de verano".

Tips:

- Use a crisp, dry, fruity wine. Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Grüner Veltliner or a dry rosé work well.
- Don't use an expensive or complex wine; the soda hides its subtleties.
- Chill everything, and add citrus or herbs for aroma.

It's a great way to enjoy wine more lightly, not a lesser drink.

## 9. What is wine aperitif?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-aperitif`

An aperitif is a drink served before a meal to stimulate the appetite. Wine aperitifs are light, dry and refreshing, usually sparkling wine, crisp whites, dry Sherry or aromatised wines like vermouth.

The tradition is strong in France and Italy, where "l'apéro" or "aperitivo" is a relaxed early-evening ritual with small snacks.

Classic wine aperitifs:

- Sparkling wine: Champagne, Crémant, Cava or Prosecco.
- Crisp whites: Muscadet, Chablis, Albariño, Sauvignon Blanc.
- Dry Fino or Manzanilla Sherry, with salted almonds and olives.
- Vermouth and other aromatised wines, served over ice with citrus.
- Kir: white wine with a little blackcurrant liqueur, or Kir Royale with sparkling wine.
- Spritz-style drinks with sparkling wine and soda.

What makes a good aperitif:

- Dry rather than sweet, since sugar blunts the appetite.
- High acidity, which makes the mouth water.
- Moderate alcohol, so it doesn't overwhelm.

Snacks should be salty and light: olives, nuts, chips, cured meats, cheese straws.

A good aperitif also sets the pace of the evening. Start light, and move to fuller wines with the meal.
