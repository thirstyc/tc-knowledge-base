## 1. What wine goes with mussels?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-mussels`

Muscadet. Mussels cooked the classic way, in white wine, garlic and shallots, want a light, crisp, slightly salty white, and Muscadet from the Loire is the textbook match.

The easiest rule: drink the same kind of wine you cook them in. Any dry, unoaked white with good acidity works, including Picpoul de Pinet, Albariño, Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino or a dry Riesling.

The sauce is what changes the pairing.

Moules à la crème, with cream and sometimes a little bacon, can take a fuller white. A Chenin Blanc or a lightly oaked Chardonnay matches the richness.

Tomato-based mussels, with chorizo or a spicy broth, suit a dry rosé or a light, chilled red such as Gamay.

Thai-style mussels with coconut, lemongrass and chile want an off-dry Riesling or a Gewürztraminer to balance the heat and aromatics.

Mussels with frites? Sparkling wine cuts through the fried potatoes beautifully.

What to avoid: oaky, heavy whites and tannic reds. They overwhelm the sweet, delicate shellfish.

And in Belgium, where moules-frites is a national dish, a good beer is also a perfectly respectable answer.

## 2. Wine with shrimp?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-shrimp`

A crisp white or a dry rosé: Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño, Pinot Grigio or a Provence rosé. Shrimp is sweet and delicate, so the wine should be fresh and not too heavy.

How the shrimp is cooked makes the difference.

Grilled or barbecued shrimp loves Albariño, Vermentino or a dry rosé, which handle the smoky char without covering the sweetness.

Garlic butter shrimp, like shrimp scampi, suits a white with a bit more body: a lightly oaked Chardonnay, a white Burgundy or a Pinot Gris.

Shrimp cocktail is cold, sweet and tangy. Champagne or a crisp Sauvignon Blanc keeps it bright.

Spicy shrimp, whether Cajun, Thai or Spanish gambas al ajillo with chile, calls for an off-dry Riesling or a fruity rosé. Keep the alcohol moderate so the heat doesn't build.

Shrimp tacos with lime and cilantro work well with Sauvignon Blanc, Grüner Veltliner or a dry rosé.

Fried shrimp or tempura? Sparkling wine is the best match.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds. Shrimp is too delicate, and the tannin can taste metallic against it. If you want red, keep it very light and chilled.

## 3. Wine with venison?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-venison`

A savoury, medium to full-bodied red: northern Rhône Syrah, an aged Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo or a Rioja Gran Reserva. Venison is lean and gamey, so it pairs best with wines that share its earthy, wild flavours.

Unlike beef, venison has very little fat, so it doesn't need a huge, tannic wine to cut through it. What it wants is flavour: pepper, dark fruit, earth and a touch of age.

Syrah is a natural match. Northern Rhône wines like Crozes-Hermitage, Saint-Joseph and Hermitage have smoky, peppery notes that echo the meat.

Aged Pinot Noir, especially red Burgundy, develops forest-floor and mushroom notes that suit venison beautifully, particularly roasted loin.

Nebbiolo from Barolo or Barbaresco brings tar, roses and firm structure, great with slow-cooked venison or venison with truffles.

Venison stews and casseroles suit rich, warming reds like Châteauneuf-du-Pape or a Rioja Gran Reserva.

Venison with fruit sauces, like blackberry, cherry or juniper, pairs well with fruit-forward Pinot Noir or Syrah.

What to avoid: light, fruity reds and most whites. They get lost next to the rich, gamey flavour. Very oaky, jammy reds can also overwhelm the meat.

## 4. Wine with asparagus?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-asparagus`

Sauvignon Blanc or Grüner Veltliner. Asparagus is famously hard to pair because its compounds can make many wines taste bitter, metallic or strangely vegetal. Wines with their own green, herbal notes handle it best.

Sauvignon Blanc from the Loire or New Zealand already tastes of cut grass and green pepper, so it meets asparagus on the same terms.

Grüner Veltliner is the classic match in Austria, where asparagus season is celebrated every spring. Its peppery, green notes work well.

In Germany, asparagus season is just as big, and dry Silvaner or dry Riesling are traditional partners.

How you prepare it matters.

With hollandaise or butter, you need a white with a little more richness: an unoaked Chardonnay, Pinot Blanc or a dry Muscat from Alsace.

Grilled or roasted asparagus picks up smoky, sweeter notes. A dry rosé or a crisp Sauvignon Blanc works well.

In risotto or with cheese, try a Soave or a lightly textured Chardonnay.

What to avoid: oaky whites and most red wines, which tend to taste harsh and metallic next to asparagus.

If you're unsure, pick a crisp, unoaked white with high acidity. It's the safest choice.

## 5. Wine with roasted vegetables?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-roasted-vegetables`

A medium-bodied, fruity red like Grenache, Pinot Noir or Côtes du Rhône, or a rich white like Chenin Blanc. Roasting brings out sweetness and caramelised, smoky notes that suit wines with ripe fruit and soft tannins.

Match the wine to the vegetables.

Root vegetables, like carrots, parsnips, beets and potatoes, are earthy and sweet. Grenache, Pinot Noir and Beaujolais are excellent partners.

Mediterranean vegetables, like peppers, eggplant, zucchini and tomatoes, suit wines from the same places: Côtes du Rhône, a Provence rosé, Sangiovese or Nero d'Avola.

Squash, pumpkin and sweet potato are rich and sweet. A white with body, like Viognier, oaked Chardonnay or Chenin Blanc, matches their texture.

Brussels sprouts, broccoli and cauliflower have a slightly bitter, sulphurous edge. Crisp whites like Sauvignon Blanc and Grüner Veltliner work better than reds here.

Mushrooms add umami and pair best with Pinot Noir or Nebbiolo.

Charred vegetables with smoky flavours can take a juicier, bolder red, such as Zinfandel or Malbec.

Herbs matter too. Rosemary and thyme point toward southern French reds; basil and parsley toward fresh whites like Vermentino.

What to avoid: very tannic reds, which can taste bitter with vegetables.

## 6. What wine goes with curry?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-curry`

An off-dry, aromatic white like Riesling or Gewürztraminer handles most curries. A touch of sweetness tames the heat, and bright acidity refreshes the palate between bites.

But "curry" covers a huge range of dishes, so it helps to look at heat, richness and sauce.

Mild, creamy curries like korma or butter chicken suit a white with some body: Viognier, Pinot Gris, off-dry Chenin Blanc, or even a lightly oaked Chardonnay.

Tomato-based curries like tikka masala work well with a dry rosé or a light, fruity red with low tannin, such as Grenache or Gamay.

Hot curries like vindaloo or phaal need low alcohol and a little sweetness. Off-dry Riesling is the safest choice. Very spicy food can overwhelm any wine, so a cold lager is a fair alternative.

Thai green and red curries, with coconut, lemongrass and lime, pair beautifully with off-dry Riesling, Gewürztraminer or Pinot Gris.

Lamb curries like rogan josh can take a soft, spicy red like Grenache or Syrah, provided the heat is moderate.

Japanese curry is mild and slightly sweet, which suits a juicy, low-tannin red.

What to avoid: high-alcohol and heavily tannic wines. Alcohol makes the chile burn hotter, and tannin turns bitter.

## 7. Wine with Thai food?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-thai-food`

Off-dry Riesling. Thai food balances sweet, sour, salty and spicy, and a Riesling with a little sweetness and high acidity can handle all four at once.

German Riesling at Kabinett or Spätlese level is ideal. The sweetness cools the chile, the acidity matches lime and tamarind, and the moderate alcohol keeps the heat in check.

Gewürztraminer is another great option. Its lychee and ginger notes echo Thai aromatics like lemongrass and galangal.

Match the wine to the dish.

Pad Thai is sweet, sour and nutty. Off-dry Riesling or Pinot Gris works well.

Tom yum soup, with lime and chile, suits Riesling or a crisp Sauvignon Blanc.

Green papaya salad and larb are fiery and sour, so pick a sweeter, lower-alcohol Riesling.

Massaman curry is rich and mild, and can take a Pinot Gris, a dry rosé or even a light, low-tannin red.

Thai green or red curry with coconut milk pairs beautifully with Gewürztraminer or off-dry Riesling.

Less spicy dishes, especially those with herbs and lime, can pair well with Sauvignon Blanc or Grüner Veltliner.

Sparkling wine is always a good choice: the bubbles refresh the palate.

What to avoid: oaky whites and tannic, high-alcohol reds, which clash with the spice.

## 8. What wine pairs with cheese?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-cheese`

White wine often works better than red. Many cheeses are rich, salty and creamy, and crisp whites handle them well, while tannic reds can taste bitter or metallic against them.

The best rule is to match intensity: delicate cheeses with light wines, strong cheeses with bold or sweet wines.

Fresh cheeses like goat cheese, mozzarella and feta want crisp, fresh whites: Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino or Assyrtiko.

Soft, bloomy cheeses like Brie and Camembert suit Champagne or a lightly oaked Chardonnay.

Hard, aged cheeses like Comté, Gruyère, aged Cheddar and Parmigiano can take more structure: aged white Burgundy, dry Sherry, Rioja or Nebbiolo.

Washed-rind cheeses like Munster and Époisses are pungent. An aromatic white such as Alsace Gewürztraminer is a classic match.

Blue cheeses love sweet wines: Sauternes, Port or late-harvest Riesling.

Regional pairings are a reliable shortcut. Sancerre with Crottin de Chavignol, Comté with Vin Jaune, Manchego with Rioja or Fino Sherry, and Stilton with Port have all been tested for generations.

For a mixed cheese board, open a few versatile bottles: a sparkling wine, a dry Riesling, a light red like Pinot Noir, and a Tawny Port for the strong stuff.

## 9. Wine with blue cheese?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-blue-cheese`

A sweet wine. Blue cheese is salty, pungent and creamy, and a sweet wine creates a sweet-and-salty contrast that works beautifully, while most dry wines taste bitter or thin next to it.

The two most famous pairings come with their own tradition.

Roquefort and Sauternes: the honeyed, apricot richness of Sauternes against the salty, tangy sheep's milk blue is a classic of French cuisine.

Stilton and Port: a British Christmas tradition. Ruby, Late Bottled Vintage or Vintage Port all work, and Tawny Port suits a milder Stilton.

Other good options include late-harvest Riesling, Tokaji Aszú from Hungary, Recioto della Valpolicella or Moscato.

Gorgonzola dolce, the creamy, milder style, can pair with sweet wines as well as a rich red like Amarone.

Prefer dry? Pick a big, fruity red with soft tannins, like Zinfandel or Amarone. The ripe fruit helps balance the salt.

Blue cheese as part of a dish, like a sauce on steak or crumbled on a salad, changes the pairing. A steak with blue cheese butter can take a Cabernet Sauvignon, because the meat does most of the work.

What to avoid: light, delicate reds and crisp, lean whites. The cheese overwhelms them.

## 10. Wine with goat cheese?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-goat-cheese`

Sauvignon Blanc, especially Sancerre. Fresh goat cheese is tangy and bright, and a crisp, high-acid white matches it perfectly.

Sancerre with Crottin de Chavignol is one of the most famous regional pairings in France. Both come from the same corner of the Loire Valley, and the wine's citrus and flinty notes cut through the creamy, tangy cheese.

Other Loire whites work too. Chenin Blanc from Vouvray is a classic partner for Sainte-Maure de Touraine, another local goat cheese.

Sauvignon Blanc from New Zealand, South Africa or Bordeaux is also a good match, as are Assyrtiko and Grüner Veltliner.

How old the cheese is matters. Fresh, soft goat cheese wants the crispest, youngest whites. Aged, firmer goat cheese develops nuttier flavours and can take a richer Chenin Blanc, a dry rosé or a light Loire red like Chinon.

Goat cheese salad is easy: Sauvignon Blanc or a dry rosé.

Warm goat cheese on toast, or baked with honey, suits an off-dry Chenin Blanc, whose touch of sweetness matches the honey.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds. The cheese's acidity makes the tannin taste harsh.
