## 1. What wine goes with steak?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-steak`

A bold red with some tannin: Cabernet Sauvignon, Malbec or Syrah. The fat in a good steak softens tannin, and the tannin makes the steak taste less heavy, so the two make each other better.

The cut changes the answer. A marbled ribeye can take the biggest wines: Napa Cabernet, a Left Bank Bordeaux, or a Barossa Shiraz. A lean filet mignon has less fat to tame tannin, so go softer: Merlot, a Rioja Reserva or even a fuller Pinot Noir.

Flank, skirt and hanger steaks, especially with chimichurri, are made for Argentine Malbec. Its plush fruit and fresh acidity handle the herbs and garlic.

Then look at what's on top. Peppercorn sauce loves Syrah, which has its own peppery streak; a northern Rhône bottle like Crozes-Hermitage is perfect. Blue cheese butter can take a big Cabernet. Béarnaise is rich and buttery, so a red with good acidity, like a Bordeaux or a Chianti Classico, keeps things fresh.

Cooked over fire? Smoke and char pull toward riper, spicier reds like Zinfandel or Grenache blends.

What to avoid: very light, delicate reds with a fatty cut, which taste thin next to it, and oaky whites, which fight the meat.

Not in a red mood? A rich, dry rosé or a skin-contact white can surprise you.

## 2. Best wine for seafood?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-seafood`

A crisp, high-acid white is the safe bet: Muscadet, Chablis, Albariño or Sauvignon Blanc. Acidity does for seafood what a squeeze of lemon does.

But "seafood" covers a lot, so match the wine to how rich the dish is.

Raw and briny, like oysters, clams and crudo, wants something lean and salty. Muscadet, Chablis and Champagne are classics for a reason.

Light white fish, grilled or steamed, suits Albariño, Vermentino, Picpoul de Pinet or a Loire Sauvignon Blanc. Keep the wine as delicate as the fish.

Rich shellfish in butter, like lobster, crab or scallops, can handle a fuller white. A lightly oaked Chardonnay from Burgundy or California matches that creamy texture.

Oily, meaty fish such as salmon, tuna and swordfish can take rosé or a light red. Pinot Noir with salmon is a classic, and chilled Gamay works with tuna.

Fried seafood loves bubbles. Fish and chips with sparkling wine is hard to beat, because the acidity and fizz cut right through the batter.

Spicy seafood, like Thai curries or Cajun shrimp, wants an off-dry Riesling or a Grüner Veltliner.

The one rule worth remembering: big, tannic reds can make fish taste metallic, so save the Cabernet for the steak.

## 3. Wine with chocolate?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-chocolate`

Something sweet and fortified, like Port or Banyuls. The golden rule: the wine should be at least as sweet as the chocolate, or the wine will taste sour and bitter.

That's why the classic idea of dry red wine with chocolate often disappoints. Chocolate's sugar strips the fruit out of a dry red and leaves the tannin and acidity exposed.

Match the wine to the chocolate.

Dark chocolate has bitterness and intensity, so it wants a rich, sweet red with some power. Ruby or Late Bottled Vintage Port works well, as do Banyuls and Maury, fortified Grenache wines from southern France that taste like dark fruit and cocoa.

Milk chocolate and anything with caramel or nuts suits Tawny Port, with its notes of toffee, dried fruit and hazelnut. A sweet Oloroso or Pedro Ximénez Sherry also works, especially with chocolate and nut desserts.

White chocolate is mostly sugar and fat, so go lighter and fresher: Moscato d'Asti, a late-harvest Riesling or a Sauternes.

Chocolate with berries, like a raspberry tart or chocolate-dipped strawberries, is perfect with Brachetto d'Acqui, a sweet, fizzy red from Piedmont.

If you really want dry red, pick a very ripe, fruity one, such as a Zinfandel, and keep the chocolate dark and not too sweet. It can work, but the sweet wines above are the safer choice.

## 4. Wine with spicy food?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-spicy-food`

An off-dry white with low alcohol and good acidity: a German Riesling, a Gewürztraminer or a Moscato. A touch of sweetness cools the heat, while alcohol makes it burn more.

The heat in chile peppers comes from capsaicin, and alcohol amplifies the burning sensation. That's why a big, 15% red can turn a pleasant curry into a painful one. Tannin makes it worse too, turning bitter against the spice.

So look for three things: lower alcohol, a little sweetness, and plenty of fruit or aroma.

German Riesling at Kabinett or Spätlese level is the classic match. Its sweetness is balanced by high acidity, so it tastes refreshing rather than sugary, and it's often under 10% alcohol.

Gewürztraminer's lychee and ginger notes work beautifully with Thai, Vietnamese and Indian dishes. Off-dry Chenin Blanc, like a demi-sec Vouvray, and Moscato d'Asti are also good options.

Sparkling wine and rosé help too. Bubbles and chill both make heat feel gentler.

If you want red, go light, fruity and low in tannin, and serve it cool. Beaujolais, a juicy Grenache rosé or a slightly sparkling Lambrusco can all work.

Match the wine's intensity to the spice. A mildly spicy dish gives you more room; a fiery one is off-dry Riesling territory.

## 5. Best wine for a date night?

- **source_doc:** `qa-date-night-wine`

Start with something sparkling, then share a versatile red like Pinot Noir or a Beaujolais cru. Bubbles set the mood, and a food-friendly red won't fight whatever you order.

Sparkling wine is the easiest opener. Champagne if you want to make a point, but a Crémant from France, a Spanish Cava or an English sparkling wine can be just as good for much less.

For the main bottle, choose something that works with lots of dishes, since you'll probably order different things. Pinot Noir is light enough for fish and substantial enough for duck or steak. Beaujolais crus like Morgon or Fleurie are juicy, fun and reasonably priced. If you both prefer white, a Chablis or a dry Riesling covers a similar range.

Keep an eye on the alcohol. A 15% red can make the evening feel heavy fast, while wines around 12–13% are easier to enjoy over a long dinner.

Ask the sommelier. Telling them your budget and what you like is not a weakness; it's the fastest way to find something great. And ordering by the glass lets you try more than one wine.

At home, pick something you already know you both like, chill it properly, and don't overthink it.

The best date-night wine is the one that lets you pay attention to each other, not the label.
