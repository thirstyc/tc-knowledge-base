## 1. What is Priorat wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-priorat`

Priorat is a small, rugged wine region in Catalonia, in northeast Spain, famous for powerful, mineral red wines from old Garnacha and Cariñena vines grown on steep slate slopes.

The region's signature is its soil, llicorella: crumbly dark slate flecked with quartz. It holds little water, so vines dig deep and give tiny yields of intensely concentrated grapes. Many vineyards are on terraces so steep they have to be worked by hand.

Priorat is one of only two Spanish regions, with Rioja, to hold the top DOCa classification (DOQ in Catalan).

Its modern story is remarkable. Vineyards were largely abandoned after phylloxera and rural decline, until a small group of winemakers arrived in the late 1980s and began making ambitious wines from the old vines. Within a decade, Priorat was among Spain's most sought-after regions.

The wines are dark, dense and structured, with black fruit, liquorice, herbs and a graphite or stony edge. Many also include Syrah, Cabernet Sauvignon or Merlot. Young wines benefit from decanting, and the best age for many years.

For a similar style at lower prices, look to neighbouring Montsant.

## 2. What is Rioja Reserva vs Crianza?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-rioja-reserva-crianza`

Both are aged Rioja, and the difference is time. Crianza is aged for at least two years, Reserva for at least three, and the extra year gives Reserva more depth and savoury character.

The legal minimums for red Rioja:

- Crianza: two years of ageing, at least one of them in oak barrels.
- Reserva: three years of ageing, at least one in oak and six months in bottle.
- Gran Reserva: five years, at least two in oak.

Producers often age their wines longer than the minimum, and Reservas are typically made from better grapes or vineyards, not just held back longer.

In the glass, Crianza is fresher and fruitier: red cherry and plum, with some vanilla and spice from the oak. It's an ideal everyday food wine.

Reserva is rounder and more complex, with dried fruit, leather, tobacco and a softer texture, as the oak and bottle time integrate.

At the table, Crianza suits tapas, burgers, pizza and grilled chorizo. Reserva is better with roast lamb, game and aged Manchego.

Price follows the ageing. Reserva costs more, but still offers some of the best value in aged red wine anywhere.

## 3. What is Vino Nobile di Montepulciano?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-vino-nobile`

Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is a red wine from the hilltop town of Montepulciano in southern Tuscany, made mostly from Sangiovese. It sits between Chianti and Brunello di Montalcino in style and price.

The name causes confusion. It has nothing to do with the Montepulciano grape, which makes Montepulciano d'Abruzzo on Italy's Adriatic coast. Vino Nobile is named after the town, and the grape is Sangiovese, known locally as Prugnolo Gentile.

The rules require at least 70% Sangiovese, often blended with small amounts of other local or international grapes. The wine must be aged for at least two years before release, and Riserva for at least three.

In the glass, expect red cherry, plum, dried herbs and earthy, savoury notes, with firm tannins and bright acidity. It's usually a little softer and rounder than Brunello, and more structured than most Chianti.

It's one of Italy's oldest named wines, praised for centuries, and a DOCG, the country's highest classification.

For something younger and cheaper from the same place, try Rosso di Montepulciano.

Serve it with roast meats, wild boar ragù, mushroom dishes or aged Pecorino.

## 4. What is skin contact fermentation?

- **source_doc:** `qa-skin-contact-fermentation`

Skin contact fermentation means fermenting grape juice together with its skins, so colour, tannin and flavour pass from the skins into the wine. It's how all red wine is made, and how orange wine is made from white grapes.

Grape juice is almost colourless, even from red grapes. The colour, most of the tannin and many aroma compounds are in the skins, so the longer the skins stay in contact with the juice, the more they give.

In red winemaking, the skins float up and form a thick "cap" during fermentation. Winemakers keep it in contact with the liquid by pushing it down (punch-downs) or pumping juice over it (pump-overs). Some let the grapes soak cold before fermentation, or leave the skins in the new wine for weeks afterwards to soften and deepen it.

Contact time shapes the style:

- A few hours: rosé.
- A few days to a few weeks: most red wines.
- Several weeks or months: very structured reds, or orange wine.

White wines are usually pressed straight away, so they get little or no skin contact. Fermenting white grapes on their skins gives orange wine, with an amber colour, grippy texture and flavours of dried fruit, tea and nuts.

## 5. What is "old-world" wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-old-world-wine`

"Old World" wine means wine from the countries where winemaking traditions began, mainly Europe, especially France, Italy, Spain, Portugal and Germany. The term is often extended to ancient winemaking regions like Georgia and Lebanon.

The label usually implies a style, though it's a generalisation:

- More restraint: higher acidity, lower alcohol and more earthy, savoury or mineral flavours alongside fruit.
- Place over grape: labels usually name the region or village, like Burgundy, Rioja or Barolo, rather than the grape.
- Rules and tradition: appellation laws control which grapes can be planted, how they're grown and how the wine is made.
- Cooler, more variable climates, which makes vintage matter more.

That's why Old World wines are often described as "food wines". Their acidity and savoury character work well at the table.

The division is less clear than it used to be. Southern Europe's warm regions make ripe, bold wines, while many New World producers now pick earlier and use less oak. Climate change is blurring the line further.

Still, the term is a useful shorthand. If a wine list or shop sorts by Old World and New World, the Old World side usually means more savoury and restrained.

## 6. What is "new-world" wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-new-world-wine`

"New World" wine means wine from countries where winemaking arrived with European colonisation and emigration: the United States, Canada, Chile, Argentina, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa, among others.

Some of these places have made wine for centuries, but the modern industries mostly grew in the second half of the twentieth century.

The traditional stylistic generalisations:

- Riper fruit: warmer, sunnier climates give fuller body, more alcohol and more upfront fruit.
- Grape over place: labels usually name the grape, like Cabernet Sauvignon, Malbec or Sauvignon Blanc, making them easy to shop for.
- Fewer rules: winemakers can generally plant any grape anywhere and experiment freely.
- Consistency: sunnier climates mean fewer difficult vintages.

New World wine was central to making wine accessible to a broad public, with clear labels and approachable styles.

The generalisations are breaking down. Cool-climate New World regions, like Tasmania, Oregon, Ontario and coastal Chile, make lean, elegant wines. Many producers now label by region and sub-region, and a growing number farm and ferment with minimal intervention.

As shorthand, New World still suggests riper and more fruit-forward. But the best approach is to judge each wine, and each producer, on its own.

## 7. What are Italian wine regions?

- **source_doc:** `qa-italian-wine-regions`

Italy has twenty administrative regions, and every one makes wine. A handful are especially important to know.

In the north:

- Piedmont: Barolo and Barbaresco from Nebbiolo, plus Barbera, Dolcetto and Moscato d'Asti.
- Veneto: Prosecco, Valpolicella, Amarone and Soave.
- Friuli-Venezia Giulia: some of Italy's best whites, and a home of orange wine.
- Trentino-Alto Adige: crisp, alpine whites and sparkling Trento.
- Lombardy: Franciacorta, Italy's top traditional-method sparkling wine.

In the centre:

- Tuscany: Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino, Vino Nobile and the Super Tuscans, mostly built on Sangiovese.
- Marche: Verdicchio, a crisp, food-friendly white.
- Abruzzo: generous, affordable Montepulciano d'Abruzzo.

In the south and islands:

- Campania: Aglianico, Fiano and Greco.
- Puglia: ripe reds from Primitivo and Negroamaro.
- Sicily: Nero d'Avola, and the volcanic wines of Etna.
- Sardinia: Cannonau and Vermentino.

Italian wine is classified as DOCG (the strictest), DOC and IGT, but some excellent wines use the broader IGT category to escape the rules.

Italy grows hundreds of native grapes, which makes it one of the most rewarding countries to explore.

## 8. What are Spanish wine regions?

- **source_doc:** `qa-spanish-wine-regions`

Spain has more vineyard area than any other country, with dozens of official regions. These are the ones most worth knowing.

Reds:

- Rioja: Spain's most famous region, mostly Tempranillo, with labels based on ageing.
- Ribera del Duero: bolder, darker Tempranillo from a high plateau.
- Priorat: intense, mineral Garnacha and Cariñena from steep slate slopes.
- Toro: powerful, ripe Tempranillo.
- Bierzo: fresh, perfumed reds from Mencía.
- Jumilla and Yecla: ripe, affordable Monastrell.

Whites:

- Rías Baixas: crisp, saline Albariño from rainy Galicia.
- Rueda: fresh, zesty Verdejo.
- Txakoli, from the Basque Country: light, spritzy and tart.

Sparkling and fortified:

- Cava, made by the traditional method, mostly in Penedès in Catalonia.
- Jerez, in Andalusia, home of Sherry, from bone-dry Fino to sweet Pedro Ximénez.

La Mancha, on the central plateau, is the largest region by area, a big source of everyday wine. The Canary Islands make distinctive volcanic wines from rare local grapes.

Spain's top classification is DOCa (DOQ in Catalan), held only by Rioja and Priorat. Most quality wines are DO. For value, look beyond Rioja to Bierzo, Montsant, Campo de Borja and Jumilla.

## 9. What makes Portuguese wine unique?

- **source_doc:** `qa-portuguese-wine-unique`

Its native grapes. Portugal grows more than 250 indigenous varieties, many found almost nowhere else, and it has largely kept them rather than replacing them with international grapes.

Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Baga, Castelão, Encruzado, Arinto and Alvarinho are just a few. They're often blended, sometimes in old vineyards where many varieties grow side by side as field blends.

Several other things set Portugal apart:

- Two of the world's great fortified wines: Port from the Douro Valley and Madeira from the Atlantic island.
- Tradition that survived: some Port grapes are still crushed by foot in shallow stone tanks called lagares.
- Remarkable variety in a small country, from light, fresh Vinho Verde in the rainy northwest to rich reds in the hot Alentejo.
- The Atlantic influence, which keeps many wines fresh despite the southern latitude.
- Cork: Portugal produces around half of the world's cork, from forests largely in the Alentejo.

Value is the other big draw. Because the grapes and regions are less famous, Portuguese wines often cost less than similar quality from France, Italy or Spain.

Where to start: a Douro red, a Vinho Verde and a Tawny Port.

## 10. What are Sardinian wines?

- **source_doc:** `qa-sardinian-wines`

Sardinia, the large Italian island in the Mediterranean, makes sun-drenched reds and saline whites from grapes that reflect its long history of trade and Spanish rule.

The key grapes:

- Cannonau: the island's main red grape, the same variety as Grenache. It makes warm, generous reds with red fruit, herbs and spice.
- Vermentino: the main white, crisp and herbal with a salty, almond edge. Vermentino di Gallura, from the granite northeast, is Sardinia's only DOCG.
- Carignano: in the southwest, Carignano del Sulcis comes from old bush vines, some on their own roots in sandy soils, giving deep, dark reds.
- Monica and Bovale: local reds, often soft and easy drinking.
- Vernaccia di Oristano: a rare, oxidative white aged under a layer of yeast, similar to Sherry.

Sardinian wines tend to be warm-climate in style, with ripe fruit and moderate acidity, tempered by sea breezes and altitude.

They're also good value. Outside Italy, many are still little known, so prices are modest.

At the table, Vermentino loves seafood and grilled fish. Cannonau suits roast lamb, pork, pecorino and tomato-based dishes.
