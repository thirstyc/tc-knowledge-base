## 1. Best French wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-french-wine`

There isn't one, and that's the point: France makes more classic styles than anywhere else. The better question is which French region suits what you like.

Bordeaux makes structured reds from Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, plus dry and sweet whites. Burgundy is the home of Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, where single vineyards are mapped in extraordinary detail.

The Rhône Valley splits in two: peppery Syrah in the north, warm Grenache-based blends in the south. The Loire Valley is the place for crisp Sauvignon Blanc, versatile Chenin Blanc and light, savoury Cabernet Franc.

Champagne needs no introduction, and Alsace makes aromatic whites from Riesling, Pinot Gris and Gewürztraminer. Provence is the benchmark for dry rosé, the Languedoc offers great value, and the Jura makes wines that taste like nowhere else.

Where to start, by budget: Côtes du Rhône, Languedoc and Loire whites are affordable and rewarding. Village-level Burgundy and Bordeaux from lesser-known appellations give you the classic experience without the famous-name premium.

One label tip: French wines usually name the place, not the grape. Learning which grape grows where is most of the work.

## 2. Best Italian wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-italian-wine`

Italy makes wine in all 20 of its regions, so the answer depends on what you're eating. Italian wines are built around acidity and structure, which makes them some of the most food-friendly in the world.

Piedmont, in the northwest, makes Barolo and Barbaresco from Nebbiolo, powerful and age-worthy, plus juicier Barbera and Dolcetto.

Tuscany is Sangiovese country: Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano, all tart with cherry and dried herbs.

Veneto gives you Prosecco, crisp Soave and rich Amarone, made from partly dried grapes.

Further south, Campania has firm Aglianico and textured Fiano, Sicily offers juicy Nero d'Avola and mineral Etna wines grown on volcanic soil, and Sardinia is known for Vermentino and Cannonau.

In the north, Alto Adige and Friuli make precise, aromatic whites.

Where to start: Montepulciano d'Abruzzo and Nero d'Avola are great value everyday reds. Chianti Classico is the classic with tomato-based food. For something special, try Barolo or Brunello.

Labels use DOC and DOCG for regulated regions and IGT for wines outside those rules, which doesn't mean lower quality.

## 3. Best Spanish wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-spanish-wine`

Rioja is the most famous, but Spain's range goes far beyond it, and it's one of the best countries in the world for value.

Rioja makes Tempranillo-based reds aged in oak, labelled by ageing time: Crianza, then Reserva, then Gran Reserva. Ribera del Duero uses the same grape for darker, more powerful wines, and Toro is bolder still.

Priorat, in Catalonia, makes intense reds from old Garnacha and Cariñena vines grown on slate.

For whites, Rías Baixas in Galicia makes fresh, saline Albariño, Rueda makes zesty Verdejo, and Galicia's Godello is worth seeking out.

Cava, mostly from Catalonia, is made the same way as Champagne at a fraction of the price.

Jerez, in the south, makes Sherry: bone-dry Fino and Manzanilla, nutty Amontillado and Oloroso, and intensely sweet Pedro Ximénez. It's some of the best value in fine wine.

For everyday value, look to Garnacha from Calatayud and Campo de Borja, Monastrell from Jumilla, and Mencía from Bierzo.

Where to start: a Rioja Crianza with dinner, or an Albariño with seafood.

## 4. Best Australian wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-australian-wine`

Shiraz made Australia famous, but the country's range is much wider, from rich warm-climate reds to some of the world's best dry Rieslings.

Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale in South Australia make the classic rich, spicy Shiraz, often from very old vines, because phylloxera never reached there.

Coonawarra, on its famous strip of red soil, is known for structured Cabernet Sauvignon. Margaret River in Western Australia makes elegant Cabernet and excellent Chardonnay.

For whites, the Clare and Eden Valleys produce bone-dry, lime-scented Riesling that ages beautifully. The Hunter Valley makes Semillon, low in alcohol when young and remarkably complex after a decade.

Cooler regions are a different side of Australia: the Yarra Valley, Mornington Peninsula, Adelaide Hills and Tasmania make restrained Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and sparkling wine.

Rutherglen, in Victoria, makes extraordinary sweet fortified Muscat.

Where to start: a Barossa Shiraz for the classic style, a Clare Valley Riesling to see the other extreme.

Most Australian wine is sealed with a screwcap, which keeps whites fresh and has nothing to do with quality.

## 5. Best New Zealand wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-nz-wine`

Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc is what New Zealand is known for, but the country's Pinot Noir is just as good and often more interesting.

Marlborough, at the top of the South Island, makes the intensely aromatic Sauvignon Blanc that changed the grape's reputation in the 1980s: passion fruit, grapefruit and cut grass, with lively acidity.

Central Otago, far to the south, is one of the world's most southerly wine regions. Its Pinot Noir is rich in dark cherry and spice, with plenty of freshness.

Martinborough, near Wellington, makes more savoury Pinot Noir, and Hawke's Bay on the North Island is the place for Syrah, Bordeaux-style blends and Chardonnay.

New Zealand Chardonnay deserves more attention than it gets: restrained, citrusy and well balanced.

The country's cool, maritime climate is the common thread. Long, gentle growing seasons keep acidity high, which is why the wines taste so fresh.

Where to start: a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc with goat cheese or seafood, or a Central Otago Pinot Noir with duck.

Nearly all New Zealand wine uses screwcaps, so no corkscrew needed.

## 6. Best Chilean wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-chilean-wine`

Chile is one of the best sources of value in the wine world, and Carmenère is its signature grape.

Carmenère originally came from Bordeaux and was thought to be lost after phylloxera. For years Chilean growers believed they were growing Merlot, until the vines were correctly identified in the 1990s. It makes smooth, dark reds with red pepper and spice notes.

The Maipo Valley, near Santiago, is known for structured Cabernet Sauvignon, and Colchagua for rich reds.

Cool coastal areas have transformed the country's whites. Casablanca, San Antonio and Leyda produce crisp Sauvignon Blanc, fine Chardonnay and elegant Pinot Noir, thanks to breezes off the cold Pacific.

Further south, Itata and Bío Bío have old bush vines of País and Cinsault, making light, characterful reds that are very fashionable with natural wine drinkers.

Chile is also one of the few places phylloxera never reached, so many vines grow on their own roots.

Where to start: an inexpensive Carmenère or Cabernet for everyday drinking, or a coastal Sauvignon Blanc with seafood.

Value is the headline: Chilean wine tends to over-deliver at every price.

## 7. Best Argentine wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-argentine-wine`

Malbec from Mendoza is Argentina's calling card: plush, dark and velvety, with plum and violet notes and soft tannins.

Mendoza sits in the foothills of the Andes, and altitude is what makes the wines work. Vineyards are planted high, where strong sunshine ripens the fruit and cold nights keep the acidity fresh.

Within Mendoza, the Uco Valley is the region to know. Its high, cool vineyards make more structured, fresher Malbec than the warmer, riper style from lower sites.

Salta, in the far north, has some of the highest vineyards in the world and is home to Torrontés, a floral, aromatic white that smells sweet but tastes dry.

Patagonia, in the south, is cooler and windier, making more delicate Malbec and Pinot Noir.

Argentine Cabernet Franc has quietly become excellent, and is worth trying if you like herbal, savoury reds.

Where to start: an everyday Mendoza Malbec with steak, then an Uco Valley bottle to taste what altitude does.

Argentina is also good value, though prices for top wines have risen as its reputation has grown.

## 8. Best South African wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-south-african-wine`

Chenin Blanc and Syrah are South Africa's strongest suits, and the country offers some of the best quality for the money anywhere.

Chenin Blanc, locally known as Steen, is the most planted grape. Styles run from simple and refreshing to rich, textured and age-worthy, especially from old bush vines.

Stellenbosch is the best-known region, making structured Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux-style blends.

Swartland has driven the country's recent revival, with old-vine Chenin Blanc and fresh, peppery Syrah made with minimal intervention.

Hemel-en-Aarde, near the coast at Walker Bay, makes fine Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, cooled by ocean air.

Constantia, just outside Cape Town, is one of the oldest wine areas in the New World and is known for Sauvignon Blanc and a historic sweet wine style.

Pinotage is South Africa's own grape, a cross of Pinot Noir and Cinsault created in 1925. Modern versions are far better than its old reputation suggests.

Where to start: an old-vine Chenin Blanc, or a Swartland Syrah if you like peppery reds.

## 9. Best wine for a gift?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-gift`

Pick a wine with a story rather than a famous label, and match it to the person. Champagne or another traditional-method sparkling wine is the safest choice if you don't know their taste.

A few approaches that work:

- Sparkling wine suits almost any occasion, and a Crémant or a good Cava looks generous without costing a lot.
- A magnum feels like an event, and it's more memorable than two ordinary bottles.
- A classic from a famous region is a safe bet if you know they like that style.
- A half bottle of sweet wine, like Sauternes or an ice wine, is an easy luxury.
- Something from a place they love, a region they've travelled to or want to visit, always lands well.

For a wine lover, look for something unusual rather than expensive: a grape or region they may not have tried.

For a beginner, choose something approachable, and add a short note about what to drink it with.

Practical tips: ask a good wine shop for help, keep the receipt, and consider pairing the bottle with glasses or a book.

If you're really unsure, a gift card to a good independent wine shop beats a guess.

## 10. Best wine to learn with?

- **source_doc:** `qa-learning-wine`

Learn with benchmark wines: classic examples of a style, tasted side by side. Comparison teaches you far more than drinking one bottle at a time.

The single most useful exercise is tasting two wines that differ in exactly one way:

- The same grape from two places: Sauvignon Blanc from the Loire and from Marlborough.
- The same region in two styles: oaked and unoaked Chardonnay.
- The same grape at two ages: a young and an older Rioja.

Good benchmark wines to start with include Sauvignon Blanc, Chardonnay, Riesling, Pinot Noir, Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah. Once you know those, the rest of the wine world makes more sense.

Practical tips:

- Write short notes. Even "smells like green apple, made my mouth water" is useful.
- Taste with food, then without, and notice the difference.
- Use half bottles or wine bar flights to try more for less.
- Revisit styles you disliked. Tastes change, and one bad bottle isn't the whole style.
- Learn the structure of a wine, its acidity, tannin, alcohol and body, before worrying about naming aromas.

If you want structure, formal courses exist at several levels, but a curious palate and a notebook will take you a long way.
