## 1. What is Burgundy wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-burgundy`

Burgundy is a region in eastern France that makes red wine from Pinot Noir and white wine from Chardonnay, and it's the place that taught the world to think about wine in terms of individual vineyards.

The region runs from Chablis in the north, through the Côte d'Or, the Côte Chalonnaise and the Mâconnais, down to Beaujolais in the south, which uses Gamay instead.

What sets Burgundy apart is the detail of its map. Vineyards have been divided and classified over centuries, and neighbouring plots with the same grape and winemaker can taste noticeably different. Wines are labelled by place, not grape, and ranked from regional Bourgogne, to village, to Premier Cru, to Grand Cru.

Ownership adds to the complexity. A single famous vineyard is often shared among many growers, each making their own version, so the producer's name matters as much as the vineyard's.

Expect reds that are light in colour but complex, with red cherry, earth and mushroom, and whites that range from steely Chablis to rich Meursault.

Burgundy is expensive at the top, but a regional Bourgogne from a good grower is an affordable way in.

## 2. What is Bordeaux wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-bordeaux`

Bordeaux is a large wine region in southwest France, best known for structured red blends of Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, plus dry and sweet whites.

The Gironde estuary splits the region in two. On the Left Bank, gravel soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon, giving firm, age-worthy wines. On the Right Bank, clay and limestone suit Merlot, giving rounder, softer ones.

Bordeaux is also where the idea of wine as a luxury investment took hold. The 1855 classification ranked Left Bank châteaux into five growths, with the first growths at the top, and those rankings still shape prices today.

But the region is enormous, and most Bordeaux is not expensive. Bottles labelled simply Bordeaux or Bordeaux Supérieur, or from appellations like Fronsac, Castillon or the Médoc, offer the same grapes and style at everyday prices.

Whites come from Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc, made dry in Pessac-Léognan and Entre-Deux-Mers, and sweet in Sauternes, where noble rot concentrates the grapes.

Expect blackcurrant, cedar, graphite and firm tannins in the reds. Serve them with lamb, beef or hard cheese, and give young, serious bottles time or a decanter.

## 3. What is Rioja wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-rioja`

Rioja is Spain's most famous wine region, in the north of the country, making mostly red wine from Tempranillo, usually aged in oak barrels before release.

Its signature comes from that ageing. Traditional Rioja spends years in American oak, which gives vanilla, coconut and dill notes alongside red fruit, leather and tobacco.

The labels tell you how long the wine aged, and the terms are worth learning:

- Genérico or Joven: little or no oak ageing, fresh and fruity.
- Crianza: at least two years, including a year in oak.
- Reserva: at least three years, including a year in oak.
- Gran Reserva: at least five years, including two in oak.

Because the producer does the ageing for you, a Gran Reserva released with a decade of age is one of the best-value mature wines in the world.

Tempranillo leads most blends, often with Garnacha, Graciano or Mazuelo. The region also makes white Rioja, mainly from Viura, in styles from crisp to richly oaked.

A newer generation makes fresher, fruit-forward wines with less oak, so the region now offers both styles.

Try Rioja with roast lamb, grilled meat or Manchego.

## 4. What is Champagne?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-champagne`

Champagne is sparkling wine from the Champagne region in northern France, made by a specific method, and the name is protected: sparkling wine made anywhere else can't be called Champagne.

It's made mostly from three grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. The region is cool, which keeps acidity high, the backbone of any good sparkling wine.

The method matters as much as the place. After the first fermentation, a little yeast and sugar are added and the wine is sealed in the bottle for a second fermentation, which traps carbon dioxide. The wine then rests on the spent yeast, which gives Champagne its toast, brioche and hazelnut character. Non-vintage bottles must age at least 15 months; vintage ones at least three years, and often far longer.

Most Champagne is a blend of grapes, vineyards and years, made to a house style. Vintage Champagne comes from a single good year, and grower Champagne is made by the same people who farm the grapes.

Sweetness is on the label: Brut is dry, Extra Brut and Brut Nature are drier still.

Serve it cold, but not freezing, and in a normal wine glass rather than a narrow flute if you want to smell it properly.

## 5. What is Prosecco?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-prosecco`

Prosecco is Italian sparkling wine from the northeast of the country, made from the Glera grape, with fresh, fruity flavours and soft bubbles.

Its bubbles come from a second fermentation in a large pressurised tank rather than in the bottle. That method is quicker and cheaper, and it preserves the grape's fresh character: pear, green apple, melon and white flowers.

Quality levels are on the label. Prosecco DOC covers a large area and most everyday bottles. Prosecco Superiore DOCG, from the hills of Conegliano Valdobbiadene or Asolo, comes from steeper, better sites and has more depth. Within that, Rive wines come from a single hillside, and Cartizze from a small, prized area.

Sweetness levels can confuse: Brut is driest, Extra Dry is slightly sweeter, and Dry is sweeter still.

You'll also find Prosecco Col Fondo, an old-style, cloudy version bottled with its yeast and left undisgorged.

Prosecco is best young and cold. It's perfect as an aperitif, with fried food or in a spritz, and it costs far less than traditional-method sparkling wine.

Don't expect Champagne's bready complexity: it's a different wine, made for freshness.

## 6. What is Chianti?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-chianti`

Chianti is a red wine from Tuscany, in central Italy, made mostly from Sangiovese. Expect tart cherry, dried herbs, a savoury, almost tomato-like edge, and bright acidity that makes it one of the great food wines.

The name covers a big area, and the label tells you where within it the wine came from. Plain Chianti is the broadest category. Chianti Classico comes from the original hilly heartland between Florence and Siena, and its bottles carry a black rooster seal. Other subzones, like Chianti Rufina, have their own character.

Within Classico there's a quality ladder: the standard wine, then Riserva with longer ageing, then Gran Selezione at the top.

Chianti's reputation suffered in the twentieth century, when rules required white grapes in the blend and cheap bottles in straw baskets flooded export markets. Those rules changed decades ago, and today's wines are mostly Sangiovese, sometimes with a little Merlot or Cabernet.

Its high acidity and moderate tannin make it a natural with tomato-based pasta, pizza, grilled meat and pecorino.

Serve it slightly cool, around 16°C. Good Classico can age for a decade.

## 7. What is Barolo?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-barolo`

Barolo is a red wine from Piedmont in northwestern Italy, made entirely from Nebbiolo, and it's one of the most structured, age-worthy wines in the world.

The wine is famously pale, with a brick-orange rim even when young, which fools people into expecting something light. It isn't: Barolo has very high tannin and high acidity, with aromas often described as tar and roses, plus sour cherry, dried herbs and truffle as it ages.

The rules require at least 38 months of ageing before release, 18 of them in wood. Riserva bottles age at least 62 months. Even so, most Barolo needs another decade to soften.

The wine comes from a small group of villages, including Barolo itself, La Morra, Serralunga d'Alba, Castiglione Falletto and Monforte d'Alba, and single-vineyard bottlings are common.

Styles vary. Traditional producers use long macerations and large old casks; modern ones use shorter macerations and smaller barrels for a rounder, earlier-drinking wine.

Barolo wants rich food: braised beef, game, mushroom or truffle dishes, aged cheese.

Not ready to wait or spend? Langhe Nebbiolo comes from the same area and grape, and drinks well young.

## 8. What is Brunello?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-brunello`

Brunello di Montalcino is a red wine from Tuscany, made entirely from Sangiovese grown around the hilltop town of Montalcino. It's richer and more powerful than Chianti, and it ages for decades.

"Brunello" is the local name for the clone of Sangiovese grown there. Montalcino is warmer and drier than much of Tuscany, so the grapes ripen more fully, giving darker fruit, more body and firmer tannins.

The ageing rules are strict. Brunello must age at least five years before release, including two in oak, and Riserva at least six. That's why it arrives already mature compared with most wines.

Expect black cherry, leather, dried herbs and a savoury, earthy depth, with high acidity and serious tannins.

For a lighter, cheaper alternative from the same place, look for Rosso di Montalcino, made from the same grapes with much shorter ageing. It's often called "baby Brunello" and drinks well young.

Serve Brunello with braised or grilled red meat, game or aged cheese, and give young bottles a decant.

Nearby Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is another Sangiovese-based Tuscan wine worth knowing, sitting between Chianti and Brunello in weight.

## 9. What is natural wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-natural-wine`

Natural wine is wine made with as little added or removed as possible: organically farmed grapes, fermentation with wild yeast, and little or no additives, including very little or no added sulfites.

There's no single legal definition that applies everywhere, which is why the term causes arguments. France has a "Vin Méthode Nature" label, but most producers simply describe their own practices.

In practice, natural wine usually means no added yeast, no acid or sugar adjustments, no fining or filtering, and minimal sulfur. Conventional winemaking allows dozens of additives and processing aids, most of which never appear on a label.

What you notice in the glass: the wines are often cloudy, sometimes lightly fizzy, and taste fresher and more savoury, occasionally with cidery or funky notes.

They're more variable than conventional wine, because there's less protection against oxygen and bacteria. Some bottles are thrilling; some are faulty. Store them cool and drink them fairly young.

Common styles include chillable light reds, skin-contact whites and pét-nat sparkling wines.

Worth knowing: organic and biodynamic refer to farming and are certified; natural refers to the cellar and mostly isn't.

## 10. What is orange wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-orange-wine`

Orange wine is white wine made like red wine: the juice stays in contact with the grape skins during fermentation, which gives it colour, tannin and texture. It's also called skin-contact or amber wine.

Normally white grapes are pressed and the juice is separated from the skins immediately. Leave them together for days, weeks or months, and the wine picks up colour ranging from pale gold to deep amber, plus grippy tannins and flavours of dried apricot, orange peel, tea and nuts.

The style is ancient, not new. It has been made in Georgia for thousands of years, traditionally in buried clay vessels called qvevri, and it survived in Friuli in northeastern Italy and Slovenia, where it was revived in the 1990s.

Because it has tannin, orange wine behaves more like a red at the table. Serve it cool but not cold, and pair it with bold food: curry, roast vegetables, aged cheese, charcuterie or anything with strong spices.

It's often, but not always, made with minimal intervention, so it appears frequently on natural wine lists.

If you're trying it for the first time, start with a short-maceration version, which is fresher and less tannic.
