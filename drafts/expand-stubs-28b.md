## 1. Why is Verdicchio so mineral and dry?

- **source_doc:** `qa-verdicchio-mineral`

Verdicchio keeps high natural acidity even in the warm Marche sun, and it's usually made fully dry, so it tastes crisp, stony and saline rather than fruity or soft.

The grape comes from the Marche, on Italy's Adriatic coast, mainly from two zones:

- Castelli di Jesi, on rolling hills closer to the sea, gives fuller, rounder wines with ripe citrus and almond.
- Matelica, a cooler, higher inland valley, gives leaner, sharper wines with more pronounced acidity.

What creates the "mineral" impression:

- High acidity, which makes the wine taste lean and tense.
- Limestone and clay soils, which many growers associate with a chalky, saline character.
- Subtle, non-aromatic fruit, so savoury notes stand out: lemon, green apple, fennel and a bitter-almond finish.

"Mineral" is a tasting description rather than a literal flavour from the soil, but it's a useful shorthand for this kind of fresh, stony, savoury white.

Verdicchio is also one of Italy's most age-worthy whites. Good examples, especially Riserva, gain honey, nuts and texture over ten years or more.

Serve it with seafood pasta, fish soup, grilled fish and fried calamari.

## 2. Why does Vermentino taste like summer?

- **source_doc:** `qa-vermentino-summer`

Because it's a Mediterranean coastal white: crisp, herbal and slightly salty, with citrus fruit and a light body. It tastes like the places it grows, sunny seaside vineyards surrounded by wild herbs.

Vermentino is grown around the western Mediterranean: Sardinia, Liguria and coastal Tuscany in Italy, Corsica, where it's called Vermentinu, and Provence and the Languedoc, where it's called Rolle.

What gives it that summery character:

- Fresh acidity and moderate alcohol, usually around 12–13%.
- Lime, grapefruit and green apple fruit.
- Herbal notes like fennel, thyme and rosemary, echoing the coastal scrubland.
- A saline edge from sea breezes, and a gently bitter, almond finish.
- Usually made in stainless steel tanks, keeping it clean and crisp.

The best-known example is Vermentino di Gallura from Sardinia's granite northeast, the island's only DOCG, which tends to be fuller and more textured.

It's a natural partner for summer food: grilled fish, seafood pasta, pesto, salads, grilled vegetables and anything with lemon and herbs.

Drink it young and well chilled, at about 8–10°C, ideally outdoors.

## 3. Why does Gewürztraminer smell so floral and exotic?

- **source_doc:** `qa-gewurztraminer-floral`

Because its skins are packed with aromatic compounds, especially terpenes like geraniol and linalool, which smell of roses and flowers, plus other compounds that give lychee and spice. It's one of the most aromatic grapes in the world.

The classic Gewürztraminer aromas:

- Lychee, the signature note.
- Rose petals and orange blossom.
- Ginger, cinnamon and exotic spice ("Gewürz" means spice in German).
- Tropical fruit, like mango and passion fruit.

The grape has pink to reddish skins, which give the wine a deep golden colour and some texture. Winemakers sometimes leave the juice in contact with the skins briefly to draw out more aroma.

Gewürztraminer is also low in acidity and fairly high in alcohol, so it feels full and rich rather than crisp. Many are made slightly off-dry, which amplifies the perfumed, exotic impression.

Alsace is its most famous home, where it makes dry, off-dry and sweet late-harvest wines. It also grows well in Germany, Alto Adige, New Zealand and Oregon.

Its intensity makes it brilliant with strongly flavoured food: Munster cheese, Thai and Indian dishes, Moroccan tagines and foie gras. Serve it cool but not too cold, so the aromas can show.

## 4. Can you age Riesling?

- **source_doc:** `qa-riesling-age`

Yes, and it's one of the best white wines for ageing. Its high acidity preserves it, and good Riesling can improve for ten to thirty years or more, especially sweeter styles.

How long depends on style and quality:

- Everyday dry Riesling: best within three to five years.
- Good dry Riesling from Germany, Austria, Alsace or Australia's Clare and Eden valleys: ten to twenty years.
- German Kabinett and Spätlese: often ten to twenty-five years.
- Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese and Eiswein: decades, sometimes more than fifty years.

How it changes: young Riesling tastes of lime, green apple, peach and flowers. With age, it develops honey, toast, dried apricot and a distinctive note often called "petrol" or kerosene, from a compound called TDN. Many lovers of aged Riesling consider it the grape's most complex expression. Sweetness also seems to fade over time, so an off-dry Riesling can taste drier with age.

Some Rieslings go through a "dumb phase" a few years after release, when they seem closed and quiet, before opening up again.

Store bottles cool, dark and steady. Screwcap-sealed Riesling ages very well too.

## 5. Can Chardonnay age?

- **source_doc:** `qa-chardonnay-age`

Yes, the best Chardonnay can age for ten to twenty years or more, but most is made to drink within a few years of release.

It depends on quality and style:

- Everyday, fruity Chardonnay: drink within two to three years.
- Good Chablis and village white Burgundy: five to ten years.
- Premier Cru and Grand Cru Burgundy: ten to twenty years from good producers and vintages.
- Top Chardonnay from California, Oregon, Australia's Margaret River and New Zealand: often five to fifteen years.
- Vintage Champagne and Blanc de Blancs, made from Chardonnay: often decades.

What helps it age: good acidity, concentration from low yields, careful use of oak, and a cool climate.

How it changes: young Chardonnay shows citrus, apple, pear and, if oaked, vanilla and toast. With age it gains hazelnut, honey, brioche, mushroom and a richer, more savoury texture. Chablis develops a distinctive nutty, mineral complexity.

One warning: some white Burgundy from the mid-1990s to the late 2000s suffered from premature oxidation, or "premox", browning and fading years too early. Producers have since changed techniques, but it's a reason to buy older white Burgundy only from reliable sources.

Store it cool and dark, and serve aged Chardonnay a little warmer than young.

## 6. What pairs with Muscadet?

- **source_doc:** `qa-muscadet-pairing`

Oysters, above all. Muscadet is light, bone-dry, high in acidity and slightly saline, which makes it one of the most food-friendly and seafood-friendly whites in the world.

Muscadet comes from the western end of the Loire Valley, near Nantes and the Atlantic coast, and is made from the Melon de Bourgogne grape. Its subtle flavours of lemon, green apple and sea spray never compete with food; instead, its acidity refreshes the palate like a squeeze of lemon.

Great pairings:

- Raw oysters: the classic, perfect match.
- Mussels, especially moules marinières.
- Clams, prawns and other shellfish.
- Grilled or pan-fried white fish.
- Fish and chips, and other fried seafood.
- Sushi and sashimi.
- Goat cheese, salads and light vegetable dishes.
- Charcuterie and salty snacks.

Look for "sur lie" on the label, meaning the wine was aged on its yeast lees, which adds texture and a subtle bready note. The single-village "cru" Muscadets, aged longer on the lees, are richer and can age for a decade.

Avoid pairing it with heavy, creamy or strongly spiced dishes, which overwhelm its delicacy.

Serve it well chilled, around 8°C.
