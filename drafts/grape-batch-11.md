## 1. Why is Pinot Noir so hard to grow?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pinot-noir-difficult`

Pinot Noir is thin-skinned, early-budding and prone to disease, and it only tastes like itself in a narrow band of climates. Get any of that wrong and the wine is thin, jammy or simply dull.

The thin skins are the root of most problems. They offer little protection against rain, so the grapes split and rot easily, and tight bunches trap moisture, which makes matters worse.

It buds early, so spring frost is a constant threat, and it ripens early, so a hot spell can push it past elegance into stewed fruit.

Climate is unforgiving. Too cool and the wine tastes green and weedy; too warm and it loses its perfume and acidity. The band in between is narrow, which is why the great examples come from a handful of regions: Burgundy, Oregon, the Sonoma Coast, Central Otago, Germany and Tasmania.

Pinot Noir also mutates readily, so growers choose between many clones, each behaving differently.

None of this is forgiving in the cellar either. With so little tannin and colour to hide behind, every decision shows in the glass.

When it works, that's exactly the appeal: a wine that tastes unmistakably of where it grew.

## 2. Why are Rieslings so good?

- **source_doc:** `qa-riesling-quality`

Riesling has an unusual combination: high acidity, low alcohol, intense aroma and the ability to age for decades. Very few grapes do all four.

That acidity is the key. It keeps the wine fresh, balances sweetness when there is any, and preserves it in the bottle for years. A good Riesling at fifteen or twenty years old can taste younger than most whites at five.

It's also one of the most transparent grapes. Riesling is almost never oaked, so nothing hides the vineyard. Slate soils in the Mosel, granite in Alsace and limestone in Austria all show through in the glass.

Its range is another strength. The same grape makes bone-dry wines, gently off-dry ones, and lusciously sweet late-harvest and ice wines, so it fits almost any moment.

At the table it's exceptionally useful, especially the off-dry styles, which handle spicy food, pork and Asian dishes better than almost any other wine.

With age, Riesling develops a distinctive petrol-like note that devotees love. It isn't a fault.

The one hurdle is the label: German Rieslings don't always say how sweet they are. Look for "trocken" if you want dry.

## 3. Why is Cabernet Sauvignon so popular?

- **source_doc:** `qa-cabernet-popularity`

Cabernet Sauvignon grows well almost everywhere warm enough to ripen it, tastes recognisably like itself wherever it's grown, and ages beautifully. That combination made it the world's most planted wine grape.

Its thick skins make it hardy and resistant to disease, and they give the wine deep colour and plenty of tannin. It buds late, avoiding spring frost, and it takes well to oak, which adds vanilla and spice.

The flavour is consistent and easy to recognise: blackcurrant, cedar, graphite and often a minty or herbal note. Whether it comes from Bordeaux, Napa, Chile or Coonawarra, you can usually tell it's Cabernet.

Structure is the other reason. High tannin and acidity mean the best examples improve for decades, which made Cabernet the grape of collectible, investable wine.

It's also a team player: blended with Merlot and Cabernet Franc in Bordeaux, with Shiraz in Australia, and with Sangiovese in Tuscany.

Its parents, incidentally, are Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc, an accidental crossing that DNA testing confirmed in the 1990s.

The one drawback is that its success has pushed out many local grapes around the world.

## 4. Why is Chardonnay so versatile?

- **source_doc:** `qa-chardonnay-versatility`

Chardonnay has a mild flavour of its own, so it takes on the character of where it grows and how it's made. That blank-canvas quality is exactly why it appears in so many styles.

In the vineyard, it adapts to almost any climate. Cool Chablis gives green apple and lemon; warm California and Australia give peach, pineapple and mango.

In the cellar, the winemaker's choices define it. Oak adds vanilla and toast. Malolactic conversion softens sharp acidity and can add a buttery note. Time on the lees brings nuttiness and texture. Skip all three and you get something lean and crisp.

It's also easy to grow: reliable yields, early ripening, and tolerant of different soils.

That's why it's used for still wine, for sparkling wine as one of Champagne's three main grapes, and for wines that range from everyday to the most expensive whites in the world.

The downside is that heavily oaked, overblown versions in the 1980s and 1990s gave the grape a bad name, which lingers.

If you think you dislike Chardonnay, try an unoaked one. You may just dislike oak.

## 5. Why is Sauvignon Blanc so crisp?

- **source_doc:** `qa-sauvignon-blanc-crispness`

Sauvignon Blanc keeps high acidity because it's usually picked early, grown in cooler places, and made without oak or softening techniques.

The grape naturally holds onto acidity as it ripens, and growers pick it before the fruit gets heavy, which keeps that zing intact.

Winemaking protects it too. Most Sauvignon Blanc ferments in stainless steel at cool temperatures, which preserves its aromas, and it usually skips malolactic conversion, the process that turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid. No oak, no lees stirring, nothing to round it off.

Its aromas add to the impression of crispness. Compounds called pyrazines give green, herbaceous notes of grass, green pepper and gooseberry, while thiols give grapefruit and passion fruit. Both read as "fresh" to most people.

Cool-climate regions bring it out best: the Loire Valley, Marlborough, coastal Chile and parts of South Africa.

That freshness is why it works with goat cheese, salads, herbs, asparagus and seafood, and why it's one of the few wines that can handle vinaigrette.

Most Sauvignon Blanc is best drunk within a couple of years, while that acidity and aroma are at their peak.

## 6. Why is Syrah/Shiraz so bold?

- **source_doc:** `qa-syrah-boldness`

Syrah has thick skins, ripens to high sugar levels in warm places, and carries naturally peppery aromatic compounds. That gives dark colour, plenty of tannin, high alcohol and a distinctive spicy punch.

The name signals the style. Syrah, in the northern Rhône, is grown in a cooler climate and tends to be more restrained: black pepper, olive, smoke and firm structure. Shiraz, in Australia's warmer regions, gets riper, giving blackberry, plum, chocolate and a fuller, softer body.

The pepper note comes from a compound called rotundone, which is more pronounced in cooler growing conditions. It's one of the few wine aromas traced to a single molecule.

Warm sites push alcohol up, often to 14.5% or more, which adds to the sense of weight and richness.

Syrah also takes oak well, and new oak adds vanilla and sweet spice on top of the fruit.

It's a great partner for grilled and smoked meat, peppercorn sauce and hearty stews.

If bold isn't what you want, look for cooler-climate Syrah from the northern Rhône, Victoria, the Adelaide Hills or Chile's coastal regions, which is lighter and more savoury.

## 7. Why is Chenin Blanc so versatile?

- **source_doc:** `qa-chenin-versatility`

Chenin Blanc keeps very high acidity even when fully ripe, which means it can be made bone-dry, off-dry, sweet or sparkling and still taste balanced.

That acidity is the grape's superpower. In sweet wines it stops the sugar from cloying; in dry wines it gives tension; in sparkling wine it provides the backbone.

It also ripens unevenly, which is a problem for some grapes and an opportunity here: growers can pick several times, taking riper bunches for sweet wines and fresher ones for dry.

The Loire Valley shows the full range. Savennières is dry and mineral, Vouvray can be dry, off-dry, sweet or sparkling, and Coteaux du Layon is a rich sweet wine made with noble rot.

South Africa, where it's called Steen, is the other heartland, with old bush vines making everything from simple, fresh whites to serious, textured, age-worthy bottles.

Flavours run from quince, apple and chamomile when young to honey, marzipan and beeswax with age. Good Chenin ages for decades.

The one catch is that labels rarely state sweetness, so a Vouvray can surprise you. If you're unsure, ask.

## 8. How long does Cabernet age?

- **source_doc:** `qa-cabernet-ageing`

It depends on the wine. Everyday Cabernet Sauvignon is best within 3–5 years, good regional bottles improve for 10–15, and the top wines from Bordeaux, Napa and similar regions can go 20–50 years or more.

What lets it age is structure: high tannin, good acidity and concentrated fruit. Over time, tannins link up and drop out as sediment, so the wine softens, and the flavours shift from fresh blackcurrant toward cedar, tobacco, leather and dried fruit.

A rough guide:

- Supermarket-level Cabernet: drink now, up to about 5 years.
- Serious regional bottles: 10–15 years.
- Classified Bordeaux and top Napa: 20 years and beyond.

Storage matters as much as the wine. Keep bottles cool (around 12–14°C), dark, and away from temperature swings. A warm cupboard will age a wine much faster, and not in a good way.

Vintage matters too: cooler, structured years usually last longer than very ripe ones.

If you're unsure whether a bottle is ready, buy two. Open one and see.

And remember most wine, including most Cabernet, is made to be drunk young.

## 9. Which wines improve with age?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wines-that-age`

Only a small share of wine improves with age, perhaps one bottle in a hundred. The ones that do share the same traits: high acidity, firm tannin (for reds), concentrated flavour and balance.

Reds that reward patience include Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux blends, Nebbiolo from Barolo and Barbaresco, Sangiovese from Brunello, northern Rhône Syrah, Priorat, Aglianico and traditional Rioja.

Whites can age too, and often longer than people expect: Riesling, white Burgundy and other serious Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc, Hunter Valley Semillon and Assyrtiko.

Sweet and fortified wines are the champions. Sauternes, Tokaji, vintage Port and Madeira can last for decades, and Madeira almost indefinitely.

What ageing does is trade primary fruit for complexity: fresh berries become dried fruit, leather and tobacco; young citrus becomes honey, nuts and toast. Tannins soften, and the wine's texture changes.

What doesn't age: most rosé, most inexpensive whites and reds, Beaujolais Nouveau and most Prosecco. They're made for freshness, and time only takes that away.

Storage is decisive. Cool, dark and stable beats a cellar's prestige every time.

If in doubt, drink it. Far more wine is drunk too late than too early.

## 10. Which grapes have high tannins?

- **source_doc:** `qa-high-tannin-grapes`

Nebbiolo, Tannat, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sagrantino, Syrah, Malbec, Petite Sirah, Aglianico and Mourvèdre are among the most tannic grapes.

Tannin comes mostly from grape skins, seeds and stems, so thick-skinned varieties with small berries give the most. Oak barrels add more.

Nebbiolo is the famous case: pale in colour but ferociously tannic, which is why Barolo needs years to soften. Tannat, from Madiran in France and Uruguay, is so tannic that producers often soften it deliberately. Sagrantino, from Umbria, is among the most tannic of all.

Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah, Malbec, Petite Sirah, Aglianico and Mourvèdre are all firmly structured, though ripeness and winemaking change how harsh that feels.

At the other end, Pinot Noir, Gamay, Grenache, Barbera, Dolcetto, Zinfandel and most lighter reds have gentle tannins.

Tannin isn't a flaw. It gives a wine its shape and lets it age, and it's what makes big reds work so well with fatty, protein-rich food.

If a wine feels too grippy, try it with a steak, decant it, or give it a few more years. All three soften the sensation.
