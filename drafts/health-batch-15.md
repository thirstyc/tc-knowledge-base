## 1. How many calories in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-calories`

Roughly 120–130 calories in a standard 150 ml glass of dry wine at about 13% alcohol. Most of that comes from the alcohol itself, not sugar.

Alcohol carries about 7 calories per gram, nearly as much as fat, so the stronger the wine, the more calories it has. Sugar adds more on top, which is why sweet wines are higher.

Rough figures per 150 ml glass:

- Dry white or red at 12–13%: about 115–130 calories.
- Big red at 15%: about 145–160 calories.
- Brut sparkling wine (a smaller 125 ml pour): about 90–100 calories.
- Off-dry Riesling: about 130–150 calories.
- Sweet dessert wine (a 75 ml pour): about 120–150 calories.
- Port (a 75 ml pour): about 130–150 calories.

Two useful points. A bottle holds five 150 ml glasses, so a dry bottle is roughly 600 calories. And restaurant pours are often 175 ml or more, which adds up faster than people expect.

Lower-alcohol wines are the easiest way to cut the number without giving up a glass.

Figures vary by producer and vintage; treat these as estimates rather than exact counts.

## 2. Is wine healthy?

- **source_doc:** `qa-is-wine-healthy`

Current health guidance is more cautious than it used to be. Older studies suggesting moderate wine drinking protects the heart have been challenged, and major health bodies now emphasise that alcohol carries risks at any level.

The change came from better research methods. Many earlier studies compared drinkers with non-drinkers without accounting for the fact that the non-drinking group often included people who had stopped for health reasons, which made moderate drinkers look healthier than they were.

The World Health Organization has stated that no level of alcohol consumption is safe for health, and several countries have lowered their recommended limits in recent years.

Wine does contain antioxidants such as resveratrol, which is where much of the "red wine is good for you" story began, but the amounts in a glass are small, and no health authority recommends drinking wine to obtain them.

None of this means a glass of wine is dangerous. It means the honest answer is that wine is a pleasure with risks attached, not a health food.

For anything personal, especially if you're pregnant, taking medication or managing a health condition, ask a doctor rather than the internet.

## 3. Does wine have carbs?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-carbs`

Yes, but not many in dry wine: roughly 2–4 grams per 150 ml glass. The carbohydrate in wine is residual sugar, the grape sugar the yeast didn't convert into alcohol.

Rough figures per 150 ml glass:

- Dry red or white: 2–4 grams.
- Off-dry white: 5–10 grams.
- Sweet dessert wine (75 ml): 10–20 grams or more.
- Brut sparkling (125 ml): about 1–3 grams.
- Extra Dry sparkling, which is sweeter than Brut: slightly more.

Brut Nature sparkling wine, with no added sugar, is among the lowest of all.

Things that don't add carbs: alcohol itself is not a carbohydrate, though it does carry calories, and oak ageing adds none.

If you're counting carbs, the useful label clues are the sweetness terms rather than the calorie count, which is rarely printed. Dry styles, Brut or Brut Nature sparkling, and lower-alcohol wines are the leanest choices.

Numbers vary by producer, so treat these as ranges rather than precise values.

## 4. How much sugar in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-sugar`

A dry wine has very little, usually under 4 grams of sugar per litre, which works out to well under a gram per glass. Sweet wines can have 100 grams per litre or more.

Sugar in wine is what's left after fermentation, called residual sugar. Yeast eats the grape sugar and makes alcohol; when it finishes the job, the wine is dry.

Rough ranges per litre:

- Dry wine: under about 4 grams.
- Off-dry: roughly 10–30 grams.
- Medium-sweet: 30–50 grams.
- Sweet dessert wines: 100–200 grams or more.
- Brut sparkling: up to 12 grams.
- Extra Dry sparkling: 12–17 grams, despite the name.
- Demi-Sec sparkling: 32–50 grams.

A common surprise: fruity wine isn't the same as sweet wine. A ripe, tropical-tasting Sauvignon Blanc is usually bone-dry.

Labels rarely state sugar, though some regions and producers now do. If it matters to you, look for "dry", "trocken", "Brut" or "Brut Nature", or ask at the shop.

## 5. Does wine cause hangovers?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-hangovers`

Alcohol causes hangovers, and wine is no exception. The severity depends mostly on how much you drink, though a few other factors play a part.

What's actually going on:

- Dehydration: alcohol makes you urinate more, which is where much of the headache and fatigue comes from.
- Acetaldehyde: your body breaks alcohol down into this compound, which is toxic and causes nausea before it's processed further.
- Poor sleep: alcohol disrupts sleep quality even when it helps you fall asleep.
- Congeners: compounds produced during fermentation and ageing, more plentiful in darker drinks, which some research links to worse hangovers.
- Inflammation: your immune system's response contributes to the general awfulness.

Wine's usual suspects, sulfites and histamines, affect a minority of people and are not the main cause for most.

The strongest predictor remains volume. A big night of anything will do it; two glasses with dinner usually won't.

Individual tolerance varies a lot, including for genetic reasons, so comparing yourself with other people isn't very useful.

## 6. How to avoid wine hangovers?

- **source_doc:** `qa-avoid-hangovers`

Drink less, drink slower, and drink water alongside. Nothing else comes close, and no supplement or trick reliably prevents a hangover.

What genuinely helps:

- Alternate each glass of wine with a glass of water.
- Eat before and while you drink, which slows alcohol absorption.
- Pace yourself: roughly one standard drink an hour is the usual rule of thumb.
- Choose lower-alcohol wines. A 12% wine delivers noticeably less alcohol per glass than a 15% one.
- Know your pour: restaurant glasses are often 175 ml or more, so a "couple of glasses" can be most of a bottle.
- Stop earlier in the evening, so alcohol has cleared before you sleep.
- Drink water before bed.

What doesn't reliably work: "drinking through it", coffee, greasy food afterwards, or most hangover pills. Sleep, water and time are what actually help.

Painkillers can help symptoms, but paracetamol and alcohol are hard on the liver, so read the label and ask a pharmacist if unsure.

If hangovers are frequent or severe, or you find it hard to cut back, that's worth discussing with a doctor.

## 7. Wine and gluten?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-gluten`

Wine is made from grapes, so it contains no gluten. It's considered safe for people with coeliac disease by major coeliac organisations.

Two things occasionally raise concern, and both are minor.

Historically, some barrels were sealed with a wheat-based paste. The practice is now rare, and any gluten that could transfer would be far below the threshold for gluten-free labelling.

Fining agents, used to clarify wine, have historically included a range of substances. Modern fining uses materials like bentonite clay, egg white or casein, and the agents are removed before bottling.

Flavoured wine products are a different matter. Wine coolers, some aromatised drinks and anything with added malt or barley ingredients can contain gluten, so check those labels.

In many countries wine can be labelled gluten-free, and some producers do so.

If you have coeliac disease and are unsure about a specific product, the safest approach is to check with the producer or your own coeliac association, which publish up-to-date guidance.

## 8. How much wine should I drink?

- **source_doc:** `qa-how-much-wine`

There's no single answer, and official guidance varies by country and has been getting stricter. Check what your own health authority recommends.

Some examples of how different the advice can be:

- Canada's guidance describes 1–2 standard drinks a week as low risk, with risk increasing from there.
- The United States has long defined moderate drinking as up to one drink a day for women and two for men.
- The UK advises no more than 14 units a week for everyone, spread over several days.
- The World Health Organization states that no level of alcohol is safe for health.

A standard drink also differs between countries, which is part of why the numbers look so inconsistent. A 750 ml bottle of 13% wine contains roughly seven to ten standard drinks depending on the definition used.

What most guidance agrees on: less is lower risk, drinking should be spread out rather than saved up, and some people should avoid alcohol entirely, including during pregnancy and with certain medications and conditions.

For your own situation, ask a doctor. This is one of the few wine questions where a general answer genuinely isn't good enough.

## 9. Is it OK to drink wine every day?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-every-day`

Health guidance increasingly suggests that frequency matters as much as quantity, and most authorities now recommend having several alcohol-free days each week.

The old idea that a daily glass of red is good for you came from studies whose methods have since been questioned. Current advice is more cautious: risk rises with the total amount consumed, and no health body recommends taking up drinking for health reasons.

Reasons the "every day" habit gets particular attention:

- It's easy for a daily glass to become a daily two or three without noticing.
- Regular drinking builds tolerance, so the same amount has less effect and intake tends to creep up.
- Drinking to relax or sleep can become a habit that's harder to break than an occasional glass.

Practical middle ground, and roughly what many guidelines suggest: keep several days a week alcohol-free, pour a measured glass rather than a generous one, and pay attention if a day without feels difficult.

If you're worried about your drinking, or find cutting back hard, speak to a doctor. That's a far better use of the question than any general rule.

## 10. How to enjoy wine responsibly?

- **source_doc:** `qa-drink-responsibly`

Know how much you're actually drinking, keep water alongside, and never drive after drinking. Most of responsible drinking is simply paying attention.

Useful habits:

- Measure your pour. A standard glass is 150 ml, and a bottle holds five of them. Restaurant and home pours are often much larger.
- Alternate with water throughout the evening.
- Eat while you drink.
- Decide in advance how much you'll have, especially at a long dinner.
- Keep several alcohol-free days a week.
- Plan how you're getting home before you start.

Never drive after drinking. Limits vary by country, and impairment starts below every legal limit.

There's also more choice than there used to be. Good dealcoholised wines, low-alcohol styles like Moscato d'Asti and German Kabinett Riesling, and non-alcoholic aperitifs all let you take part without the alcohol.

Drinking better rather than more is a real strategy: one glass of something you're genuinely interested in beats three of something forgettable.

And if drinking ever feels harder to control than you'd like, talk to a doctor or a local support service. That's not a dramatic step; it's a sensible one.
