## 1. Why is Albariño so drinkable?

- **source_doc:** `qa-albarino-drinkable`

Because it combines bright acidity, ripe peach and citrus fruit and moderate alcohol, so it tastes fresh and lively rather than heavy.

Albariño comes mainly from Rías Baixas in Galicia, Spain's rainy Atlantic corner, and from northern Portugal, where it's called Alvarinho. The cool, damp, sea-influenced climate keeps acidity high, while summer sun ripens the fruit enough to give generous peach, apricot and lemon flavours.

A few things make it especially easy to enjoy:

- Balance: juicy fruit and zesty acidity pull in opposite directions, so it never feels sour or flabby.
- A salty, sea-spray edge that makes you want another sip.
- Moderate alcohol, usually around 12–13%.
- Usually fermented in stainless steel, so the fruit stays clean.
- Thick skins that add a little aromatic intensity and texture.

It's also one of the most food-friendly whites around, especially with seafood. Octopus, grilled fish, oysters, prawns and ceviche are classic partners.

Most Albariño is best young, within two or three years. Some producers make richer versions aged on their lees, which gain texture and can age longer.

Serve it well chilled, at about 7–10°C.

## 2. What is Bordeaux blend?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-bordeaux-blend`

A Bordeaux blend is a wine made from the grapes traditionally grown in Bordeaux, mainly Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, often with Cabernet Franc and smaller amounts of Petit Verdot and Malbec.

In Bordeaux itself, the blend depends on the side of the river:

- Left Bank (Médoc and Graves): Cabernet Sauvignon usually dominates, giving structure, blackcurrant and cedar, with Merlot to soften it.
- Right Bank (Saint-Émilion and Pomerol): Merlot usually leads, giving plush plum and chocolate, often with a good share of Cabernet Franc.

Why blend? Each grape brings something the others lack. Cabernet Sauvignon gives tannin and backbone, Merlot adds flesh and roundness, Cabernet Franc brings perfume, and Petit Verdot adds colour and spice. Blending also protects producers against a bad year for any single grape.

Carmenère was once an important part of the blend too, before it largely disappeared from Bordeaux. It survives today mainly in Chile.

White Bordeaux has its own blend: Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, sometimes with Muscadelle.

The style has been copied worldwide, in Napa, Washington, Margaret River, Stellenbosch, Chile and Tuscany. In the US, some are labelled Meritage.

## 3. What is Rhone blend?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-rhone-blend`

A Rhône blend is a wine made from the grapes of France's southern Rhône Valley, most famously Grenache, Syrah and Mourvèdre, often shortened to GSM.

Each grape plays a role:

- Grenache: red fruit, warmth and a soft, generous body. It usually leads the blend.
- Syrah: colour, dark fruit, black pepper and structure.
- Mourvèdre: savoury, meaty depth, tannin and ageing potential.

Other grapes often join them, including Cinsault for freshness and Carignan for rustic depth. Châteauneuf-du-Pape famously allows thirteen varieties, red and white.

Classic examples include Côtes du Rhône, Côtes du Rhône Villages, Gigondas, Vacqueyras and Châteauneuf-du-Pape. In the northern Rhône, by contrast, reds are made mostly from Syrah alone.

White Rhône blends exist too, based on Grenache Blanc, Marsanne, Roussanne and Viognier.

The style has travelled well. Australia's Barossa and McLaren Vale make rich GSM blends, and California's Paso Robles and Santa Barbara are known for Rhône-style wines. Spain, South Africa and Washington State make excellent versions too.

Expect ripe red and black fruit, spice, herbs and a warm, rounded texture. Rhône blends are great with grilled meat, lamb, sausages and hearty stews.

## 4. What is Meritage?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-meritage`

Meritage is a term used mainly in the United States for wines made from a blend of the traditional Bordeaux grapes. It rhymes with "heritage", not with the French-sounding "garage".

The term was created in the late 1980s by a group of California wineries. US labelling rules require at least 75% of a single grape for a wine to be named after it, so many high-quality Bordeaux-style blends could only be called "red table wine". Meritage gave them a quality name of their own.

To use it, a wine must be made from at least two of the approved Bordeaux varieties:

- Red Meritage: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot, Malbec, and the rarer Carmenère, St. Macaire and Gros Verdot. No single grape may make up more than 90%.
- White Meritage: Sauvignon Blanc, Sémillon and Sauvignon Vert (Muscadelle).

Wineries must be licensed members of the association that owns the term.

In practice, many producers of Bordeaux-style blends never use the word, preferring a proprietary name. Meritage is common in California, Washington, Virginia and Canada.

Expect the same style as a Bordeaux blend: structured, with blackcurrant, plum and cedar.

## 5. Why is Tempranillo underrated?

- **source_doc:** `qa-tempranillo-underrated`

Because it often hides behind place names. Tempranillo makes some of the world's great reds, but labels usually say Rioja, Ribera del Duero or Toro rather than the grape, so many people don't realise how much they already drink it.

It's Spain's most important red grape, and it's also grown in Portugal, where it's called Tinta Roriz in the Douro and Aragonez in the Alentejo.

Other reasons it's overlooked:

- It's a chameleon. Young, unoaked Tempranillo is juicy and cherry-fruited; long-aged Gran Reserva is savoury, with leather, tobacco and dried fig. People often don't connect the two.
- Oak can dominate. Traditional American oak gives strong vanilla and dill, which some drinkers attribute to the winemaking rather than the grape.
- It's great value, which ironically lowers its prestige. Aged Rioja is often cheaper than younger wines from more fashionable regions.

What it offers: medium to full body, red and black cherry, plum, leather and spice, with moderate acidity and firm but smooth tannins. It ages beautifully.

Outside Spain, good examples come from Portugal, Argentina, Australia, Texas and Oregon.

Try a Crianza and a Gran Reserva side by side to see its full range.

## 6. How long does Pinot Noir age?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pinot-noir-age`

Most Pinot Noir is best within two to five years. Good-quality examples age well for five to fifteen years, and the best Burgundy can improve for twenty years or more.

Pinot Noir has thin skins and relatively low tannin, so it relies on acidity and fruit concentration to age. That's why its ageing potential varies so much by origin and quality.

A rough guide:

- Everyday Pinot Noir from warm regions: drink within two to four years, while the fruit is fresh.
- Good New World Pinot from Oregon, New Zealand, California or Australia: five to ten years, sometimes more.
- Village Burgundy: five to ten years.
- Premier Cru and Grand Cru Burgundy: ten to twenty-plus years from a good vintage.

How it changes: young Pinot tastes of fresh red cherry, raspberry and flowers. With age, it develops earthy, savoury notes of mushroom, forest floor, leather and dried roses, while the colour turns from ruby to brick-orange. Many collectors think this "sous-bois" character is Pinot at its most magical.

Signs to drink up: fading fruit, a brown rim and a dry, thin finish.

Storage matters more than anything. Keep Pinot cool, dark and steady, around 12–14°C.

## 7. How to age wine at home?

- **source_doc:** `qa-age-wine-at-home`

Keep bottles cool, dark, still and at a steady temperature, lying on their sides if they have corks, and choose wines that are actually built to age.

The ideal conditions:

- Temperature: around 12–14°C. Consistency matters more than the exact number; frequent swings do more damage than a steady 16°C.
- Darkness: light, especially sunlight, can damage wine quickly.
- Humidity: around 60–70% stops corks from drying out.
- Stillness: keep bottles away from vibration, such as washing machines.
- Position: store cork-sealed bottles on their side to keep the cork moist.

Where to do it:

- A wine fridge is the easiest reliable option.
- A cool basement or interior closet can work if the temperature stays steady.
- Avoid the kitchen, the top of the fridge, garages and anywhere near heating.

Choose wines that improve with age: structured reds like Bordeaux, Barolo, northern Rhône Syrah and good Rioja, plus Riesling, Chenin Blanc, white Burgundy, vintage Champagne and vintage Port. Most everyday wine is made to drink young.

Buy several bottles of the same wine and open one every few years. It's the best way to learn when it tastes best to you.

Keep a simple record of what you have and when to drink it.

## 8. Which grapes are high in acidity?

- **source_doc:** `qa-high-acid-grapes`

Among whites, Riesling, Chenin Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño, Assyrtiko, Furmint and Grüner Veltliner are among the most acidic. Among reds, Nebbiolo, Sangiovese, Barbera, Pinot Noir and Gamay stand out.

High-acid white grapes:

- Riesling: famously tangy, from dry to sweet.
- Chenin Blanc: bright acidity that lets it age for decades.
- Sauvignon Blanc: zesty and green.
- Albariño: fresh and saline.
- Assyrtiko: bracing acidity even in hot Santorini.
- Furmint: high acid behind Tokaji's sweetness.
- Picpoul: its name means "lip stinger".
- Muscadet (Melon de Bourgogne) and Chablis (Chardonnay from a cool climate).

High-acid red grapes:

- Barbera: very high acid, low tannin.
- Sangiovese: tart cherry and savoury.
- Nebbiolo: high acid and high tannin.
- Pinot Noir and Gamay: fresh and bright.
- Blaufränkisch and Aglianico.

Climate matters as much as the grape. Cool climates, high altitude and early picking preserve acidity; heat lowers it. The same grape can taste much more tart from a cool region.

High-acid wines are the most food-friendly, since acidity refreshes the palate and cuts through fat and salt. They also tend to age better.

## 9. What does "off-dry" mean?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-off-dry`

Off-dry means a wine has a small amount of noticeable sweetness, somewhere between fully dry and properly sweet.

Sweetness comes from sugar left over when fermentation stops before the yeast has converted it all into alcohol. Winemakers can stop fermentation deliberately, by chilling the wine or filtering out the yeast, to keep a touch of sweetness.

As a rough guide:

- Dry: up to about 4 grams of sugar per litre, with no perceptible sweetness.
- Off-dry, or medium-dry: roughly 4 to 12 grams per litre, sometimes more.
- Medium-sweet and sweet: above that.

Acidity changes how sweet a wine tastes. A high-acid Riesling with 15 grams of sugar can taste only slightly sweet, while a low-acid wine with less sugar can seem sweeter.

Common off-dry wines:

- German Riesling Kabinett, or labelled "feinherb".
- Vouvray labelled "demi-sec" or "tendre".
- Many Alsace Gewürztraminer and Pinot Gris.
- Some rosés and New World Rieslings.
- Lambrusco labelled "amabile".

Off-dry wines are brilliant with spicy food, since a little sweetness tames chili heat. They also suit pork, salty cheese and dishes with a sweet element, like glazes and fruit sauces.

## 10. What are Corsican wines?

- **source_doc:** `qa-corsican-wines`

Corsica, the mountainous French island in the Mediterranean, makes distinctive wines from grapes shared with Italy, especially its own signature reds, Nielluccio and Sciaccarello, and the white Vermentino.

The island's history under Genoese rule before becoming French shows in its grapes:

- Nielluccio: the island's main red, closely related to Sangiovese. It makes structured, savoury reds and rosés, especially in Patrimonio in the north.
- Sciaccarello: a Corsican specialty with a pale colour, peppery spice and red fruit, especially around Ajaccio on the west coast.
- Vermentino, called Vermentinu locally: crisp, herbal, saline whites.

Other important areas include Figari and Sartène in the south, and Cap Corse, known for the sweet Muscat du Cap Corse.

Rosé is a big part of production, much of it pale, dry and Provence-like.

The island's mountains, granite and schist soils, and sea breezes keep the wines fresh despite the hot sun. Many vineyards are small, family-run and farmed organically.

Corsican wines rarely leave France in large quantities, so they're still a discovery for most drinkers, and often good value.

At the table: Vermentinu with seafood, Sciaccarello with charcuterie and grilled fish, Nielluccio with lamb and wild boar.
