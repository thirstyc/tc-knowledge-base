## 1. Cava vs Champagne?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-cava-champagne`

Both are made the same way, with a second fermentation in the bottle, but they come from different countries, use different grapes, and Cava usually costs far less.

Champagne comes from northern France and is made mostly from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. Its cool climate gives high acidity, and long ageing on the spent yeast builds toast and brioche notes. A non-vintage Champagne must age at least 15 months before release.

Cava is mostly made in Catalonia, in northeastern Spain, from local grapes: Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada, sometimes with Chardonnay or Pinot Noir. The warmer climate gives riper fruit, and the wines often have a distinctive earthy, slightly bitter almond note. Basic Cava must age at least nine months.

Longer-aged Cava is labelled Reserva or Gran Reserva and gets closer to Champagne in complexity, usually for a lot less money.

With food, both work as aperitifs; Cava's fruitiness suits tapas, fried food and paella particularly well.

Which to choose? Champagne for a special occasion and more depth. Cava when you want traditional-method bubbles without the price, or for cocktails and parties.

## 2. What is Crémant?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-cremant`

Crémant is French sparkling wine made the same way as Champagne, but from outside the Champagne region. It's usually the best value in French bubbles.

Like Champagne, Crémant gets its bubbles from a second fermentation inside the bottle, followed by ageing on the spent yeast, which gives it fine bubbles and bready, nutty notes. The rules require at least nine months on the lees.

There are several Crémant appellations across France, and each uses its local grapes:

- Crémant d'Alsace: mostly Pinot Blanc, often Riesling or Pinot Gris.
- Crémant de Bourgogne: Chardonnay and Pinot Noir, closest in spirit to Champagne.
- Crémant de Loire: Chenin Blanc and Cabernet Franc.
- Crémant de Limoux: from the south, where French sparkling wine may have started.
- Crémant du Jura, de Bordeaux, de Die and de Savoie round out the family.

Prices are typically a fraction of Champagne's, because the grapes and land cost less, not because the method is simpler.

Use it anywhere you'd use Champagne: as an aperitif, with fried food or oysters, or for a toast without the premium.

## 3. Brut vs Extra Dry Champagne?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-brut-extra-dry`

Brut is drier than Extra Dry. The names are counterintuitive, and they trip up almost everyone.

Sparkling wine gets a small amount of sweetness added before the bottle is sealed, called the dosage, and the label terms describe how much sugar ends up in the wine:

- Brut Nature or Zero Dosage: essentially no added sugar, bone-dry.
- Extra Brut: very dry.
- Brut: dry. This is the most common style and what most people expect.
- Extra Dry (or Extra Sec): slightly off-dry, a touch sweeter than Brut.
- Sec: noticeably sweeter, despite "sec" meaning dry in French.
- Demi-Sec: sweet, good with dessert.
- Doux: very sweet and now rare.

So the order from driest to sweetest runs Brut Nature, Extra Brut, Brut, Extra Dry, Sec, Demi-Sec, Doux.

Why the confusion? The terms date from a time when sparkling wine was much sweeter overall, and the names stuck even as tastes changed.

In practice: choose Brut as an aperitif or with savoury food, Extra Dry if you like a hint of sweetness, and Demi-Sec with cake and fruit desserts.

## 4. Blanc de Blancs vs Blanc de Noirs?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-blanc-de-blancs-noirs`

Blanc de Blancs is white wine made from white grapes. Blanc de Noirs is white wine made from black grapes. Both terms are most often used for sparkling wine.

Black grapes can make white wine because their juice is clear. The colour lives in the skins, so if the grapes are pressed gently and the juice is taken off the skins immediately, the wine stays pale.

In Champagne, Blanc de Blancs means 100% Chardonnay. It tastes lean and precise, with citrus, green apple and chalky minerality, and it often ages beautifully, gaining toast and hazelnut.

Blanc de Noirs in Champagne means Pinot Noir, Meunier, or both. It's fuller and rounder, with red-apple and small red-fruit notes and more weight on the palate.

Most Champagne is a blend of all three grapes, so these labels tell you the producer made a deliberate choice.

With food, Blanc de Blancs is perfect with oysters, sushi and delicate seafood. Blanc de Noirs can handle richer dishes: roast chicken, charcuterie, mushroom tart.

The terms appear outside Champagne too, on Crémant, English sparkling wine and sparkling wines from around the world.

## 5. Port vs Sherry?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-port-sherry`

Both are fortified wines, meaning grape spirit is added, but the timing of that addition changes everything. Port is usually sweet; most Sherry is bone-dry.

Port comes from the Douro Valley in northern Portugal. The spirit goes in during fermentation, which stops the yeast before it has eaten all the sugar. The result is sweet, rich and usually around 20% alcohol.

Port styles include Ruby, fruity and young; Tawny, aged in barrel until nutty and caramelly; Late Bottled Vintage; Vintage Port, made only in the best years and built to age for decades; and White Port, often served with tonic.

Sherry comes from Jerez in southern Spain. The spirit goes in after fermentation has finished, so the base wine is already dry.

Sherry styles run from Fino and Manzanilla, pale and bracing, aged under a film of yeast called flor, to Amontillado and Oloroso, which are darker and nuttier, to sweet Pedro Ximénez, which tastes of raisins and figs. Sherry is aged in a solera, a system that gradually blends younger wine into older.

With food: Fino with olives, almonds and fried fish; Oloroso with stew; Port with blue cheese, chocolate and dessert.

## 6. Madeira vs Port?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-madeira-port`

Both are Portuguese fortified wines, but Madeira is deliberately heated and oxidised, which makes it taste different and last far longer once opened.

Madeira comes from a small Atlantic island of the same name. The wine is gently heated, either in tanks or slowly in warm lofts over years, which caramelises it and makes it remarkably stable. Its signature is high acidity with flavours of caramel, walnut, dried fruit and a tangy, almost salty finish.

Madeira styles are named after grapes, running from dry to sweet: Sercial, Verdelho, Bual and Malmsey.

Port, from the Douro Valley on the mainland, isn't heated. It's fortified during fermentation to keep the sweetness, and it tastes richer and fruitier, with styles from young Ruby to long-aged Tawny and Vintage.

The most practical difference is what happens after opening. Madeira has already been exposed to heat and air, so an open bottle keeps for months. Port keeps for days or a few weeks, depending on the style.

With food: Madeira suits soups, nuts and caramel desserts, and it's superb with aged cheese. Port shines with blue cheese and chocolate.

## 7. Vermouth vs Sherry?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-vermouth-sherry`

Sherry is a fortified wine. Vermouth is an aromatised wine: fortified and then flavoured with botanicals, always including wormwood.

Sherry comes only from Jerez in southern Spain, made mainly from the Palomino grape, and its character comes from how it's aged: under a layer of yeast called flor for the palest styles, or in contact with air for the darker ones.

Vermouth can be made anywhere, though Italy and France are the classic homes. Producers start with a fairly neutral base wine, fortify it, then infuse it with herbs, roots, spices and citrus peel, and often add sugar. Styles range from pale and dry to sweet red.

They're used differently too. Vermouth is the backbone of cocktails like the Martini and Negroni, though it's lovely on its own over ice with a slice of orange. Sherry is usually drunk as a wine, with food.

Both need refrigeration after opening, since both lose their freshness once air gets in. Drink an open bottle of either within a few weeks.

Try Fino Sherry with olives and almonds, and vermouth on the rocks as an aperitif.

## 8. What grape makes Burgundy wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-burgundy-grapes`

Pinot Noir for the reds and Chardonnay for the whites. Burgundy labels almost never mention the grape, because the name on the bottle is the place.

That's the key to reading Burgundy: Chablis, Meursault and Puligny-Montrachet are Chardonnay. Gevrey-Chambertin, Vosne-Romanée and Volnay are Pinot Noir.

Two other grapes appear in smaller amounts. Aligoté makes a crisp, lemony white, sold as Bourgogne Aligoté. Gamay is the grape of Beaujolais, at Burgundy's southern end, and can be blended with Pinot Noir in a wine called Bourgogne Passe-Tout-Grains.

Burgundy's labels are also arranged by a quality ladder. Regional wines say Bourgogne. Village wines name the village. Premier Cru names the village and the vineyard. Grand Cru, the top tier, names only the vineyard.

Because one grape is used for each colour, Burgundy is the best place in the world to taste what "terroir" means. Two neighbouring vineyards, same grape, same winemaker, can taste noticeably different.

Starting out? A regional Bourgogne Rouge or Blanc from a good grower shows the style without the famous-name price.

## 9. Which grapes make Bordeaux?

- **source_doc:** `qa-bordeaux-grapes`

Bordeaux reds are blends, mostly Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, with Cabernet Franc, Petit Verdot and Malbec in supporting roles. The whites are mostly Sémillon and Sauvignon Blanc.

Which grape leads depends on which side of the Gironde estuary the vineyard sits.

On the Left Bank, including the Médoc, Pauillac and Saint-Julien, gravel soils suit Cabernet Sauvignon, so the wines are firmer, more structured and built to age.

On the Right Bank, including Pomerol and Saint-Émilion, clay and limestone suit Merlot, and often Cabernet Franc, so the wines are rounder and softer.

Blending is the point. Merlot brings flesh and plummy fruit, Cabernet Sauvignon adds tannin and structure, Cabernet Franc adds perfume and herbal lift, and Petit Verdot contributes colour and spice in small doses.

For whites, Sémillon gives body and waxy texture while Sauvignon Blanc adds freshness. The same pair, affected by noble rot, makes the sweet wines of Sauternes.

Bordeaux has also approved a handful of new varieties for the warming climate, though you'll rarely see them on a label yet.

Worth knowing: "Bordeaux blend" elsewhere in the world means wine made from these same grapes.

## 10. What are noble wine grapes?

- **source_doc:** `qa-noble-grapes`

"Noble grapes" usually means the handful of varieties that travelled from Europe and became the international standard: Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir, Syrah, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling.

The term isn't official, and no authority maintains the list. It was popularised to describe grapes that make high-quality wine in many different places, hold their character across climates, and are recognised worldwide.

There's also a regional use of the word. In Alsace, four grapes are officially designated noble and may be used for the top wines: Riesling, Gewurztraminer, Pinot Gris and Muscat.

The idea has fair criticism. Plenty of "non-noble" grapes make extraordinary wine: Nebbiolo, Sangiovese, Tempranillo, Chenin Blanc, Grenache and Assyrtiko among them. The list also reflects which grapes were planted by colonial and commercial expansion, not simply which taste best.

One thing the noble grapes are genuinely useful for is learning. Because they grow everywhere, they're the easiest way to taste how climate and winemaking change a wine.

Don't confuse the term with noble rot, which is a beneficial fungus that concentrates grapes for sweet wines like Sauternes.
