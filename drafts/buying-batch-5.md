## 1. Best red wine for beginners?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-red-beginners`

Start with a fruity, soft red with gentle tannins: Pinot Noir, Beaujolais, Merlot or a Côtes du Rhône. These wines are easy to enjoy without food and don't dry out your mouth the way bolder reds can.

Pinot Noir is light, silky and full of red fruit like cherry and raspberry. Affordable bottles from Chile, New Zealand and California are a friendly introduction.

Beaujolais, made from Gamay, is juicy and fresh, and it tastes great slightly chilled.

Merlot is soft and plummy, with more body than Pinot Noir but still smooth tannins.

Côtes du Rhône and other Grenache-based wines are warm, fruity and generous, and often great value.

Once you're comfortable, move up to fuller wines like Malbec or Zinfandel, which are rich and ripe but still approachable. Save big, tannic reds like young Cabernet Sauvignon, Nebbiolo or Tannat for later.

A few tips:

- Serve reds slightly cool, around 15–16°C. Warm red wine tastes more alcoholic.
- Try wines with food. Tannin feels much softer alongside a meal.
- Ask at the shop for "fruity and smooth". It's a perfectly good description.

The best way to learn is to taste a few side by side and notice what you like.

## 2. Best white wine for beginners?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-white-beginners`

Start with a fresh, fruity white that's easy to like: Pinot Grigio, Sauvignon Blanc, unoaked Chardonnay or an off-dry Riesling.

Pinot Grigio is light, crisp and gentle, with flavours of lemon, apple and pear. It's hard to dislike and easy to find.

Sauvignon Blanc is zesty and aromatic. New Zealand versions are bursting with passion fruit and citrus; bottles from the Loire Valley are more restrained and mineral.

Unoaked Chardonnay, including Chablis, is clean and fresh, with apple and lemon. It's a good way to learn what Chardonnay tastes like before oak changes it.

Off-dry Riesling, like a German Kabinett, has a touch of sweetness balanced by lively acidity, and it's often low in alcohol. It's a great choice if you think you don't like dry wine.

Albariño is another good option: fresh, a little salty, and perfect with seafood.

If you like sweeter wine, Moscato d'Asti is lightly sparkling, low in alcohol and full of peach and orange blossom.

Once you're comfortable, try oaked Chardonnay, Viognier or Chenin Blanc for richer, fuller styles.

Serve whites chilled but not ice-cold, around 8–10°C. Too cold, and the flavour disappears.

## 3. Best light red wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-light-red`

Pinot Noir and Beaujolais are the go-to light reds. They're low in tannin, bright in acidity and full of red fruit, and both taste great slightly chilled.

Pinot Noir is the benchmark. Look to Burgundy for elegance, or to Oregon, New Zealand and Germany, where it's called Spätburgunder.

Beaujolais, made from Gamay, is juicy and fun. The crus, like Fleurie and Morgon, add more depth without losing freshness.

A few lighter reds worth discovering:

- Frappato from Sicily: floral and strawberry-scented.
- Valpolicella from northern Italy: cherry and almond, easy-drinking.
- Schiava from Alto Adige: pale, delicate and aromatic.
- Poulsard from the Jura: so light it can pass for rosé.
- Zweigelt from Austria: juicy and peppery.
- Cinsault: soft and perfumed, especially from South Africa and southern France.
- Lambrusco: a lightly sparkling red from Emilia-Romagna, great with food.

Light reds are especially good with salmon, roast chicken, charcuterie and pizza, and they're a smart choice for warm weather.

Serve them cooler than bigger reds, around 12–14°C. Twenty minutes in the fridge is usually enough.

## 4. Best full-bodied red wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-full-bodied-red`

Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz, Malbec and Zinfandel are the most popular full-bodied reds. They're rich, ripe and powerful, with more alcohol and flavour than lighter reds.

Body is how heavy a wine feels in your mouth. It comes mostly from alcohol, ripe fruit and extract, the flavour and colour drawn from the grape skins, and often from tannin too.

Cabernet Sauvignon from Napa Valley, Bordeaux or Coonawarra is structured, with blackcurrant, cedar and firm tannins.

Australian Shiraz, especially from the Barossa Valley, is rich and generous, with blackberry, chocolate and spice.

Argentine Malbec is plush and velvety, with plum and violet notes.

Zinfandel from California is jammy, spicy and often high in alcohol.

For something bolder or more unusual, try:

- Amarone della Valpolicella: made from partly dried grapes, rich and raisiny.
- Petite Sirah: inky and very tannic.
- Tannat from Madiran or Uruguay: dark and powerful.
- Priorat and Châteauneuf-du-Pape: warm, concentrated Grenache-based blends.
- Aglianico from southern Italy: firm and age-worthy.

Full-bodied reds are best with rich food like steak, lamb, braises and aged cheese. Many also improve with a few years in the bottle or some time in a decanter.

## 5. Best crisp white wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-crisp-white`

Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño, Muscadet, Chablis and dry Riesling are classic crisp whites. They're high in acidity, unoaked and refreshing, usually from cooler climates.

"Crisp" describes a wine with lively acidity that makes your mouth water, and no heavy oak or richness to soften it.

Sauvignon Blanc from Sancerre is flinty and citrusy; from Marlborough, it's zesty and tropical.

Albariño from Rías Baixas is fresh and slightly salty.

Muscadet from the Loire is light, lemony and made for oysters.

Chablis is Chardonnay without oak: green apple, lemon and a stony, mineral edge.

Dry Riesling from Germany, Austria, Alsace or Australia's Clare and Eden Valleys is steely and citrusy.

More crisp whites to try:

- Grüner Veltliner from Austria: peppery and fresh.
- Assyrtiko from Santorini: bone-dry, saline and intense.
- Vinho Verde from Portugal: light and sometimes slightly spritzy.
- Picpoul de Pinet from southern France: lemony and great value.
- Verdejo from Rueda: herbal and zesty.
- Vermentino from Sardinia and Liguria: citrusy with a slightly bitter finish.

Crisp whites are best with seafood, salads, goat cheese and anything with lemon or herbs. Serve them well chilled.

## 6. Best dry wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-dry-wine`

Most wine is dry. "Dry" simply means the yeast has turned almost all the grape sugar into alcohol, leaving very little residual sugar. If you want a reliably dry wine, look for Muscadet, Chablis, Sancerre, Provence rosé, Brut Champagne or most red Bordeaux and Chianti.

The most common confusion: fruity is not the same as sweet. A ripe New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc or an Australian Shiraz can smell like tropical fruit or jam and still be completely dry. High alcohol can also give a sense of sweetness.

Some of the driest styles:

- Whites: Muscadet, Chablis, Sancerre, Assyrtiko, Albariño.
- Reds: Bordeaux, Chianti, Nebbiolo, Rioja.
- Rosé: Provence rosé.
- Sparkling: Brut Nature, Extra Brut or Brut Champagne and Cava.
- Fortified: Fino and Manzanilla Sherry, which are bone-dry.

Label words that help:

- "Trocken" on German wines means dry.
- "Brut" on sparkling wine means dry, and "Brut Nature" or "Zero Dosage" means the driest.
- Watch out: on Champagne, "Sec" and "Extra Dry" are actually slightly sweet.

If a Riesling doesn't say "trocken", ask. Many are off-dry or sweet.

## 7. Best sweet wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-sweet-wine`

Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú, late-harvest Riesling, ice wine, Port and Moscato d'Asti are some of the best sweet wines. The great ones balance their sweetness with fresh acidity, so they taste rich rather than sugary.

Sweet wines are made in a few different ways.

Noble rot: a beneficial fungus called Botrytis shrivels the grapes and concentrates their sugar. Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú and German Beerenauslese and Trockenbeerenauslese are made this way, with notes of honey, apricot and marmalade.

Late harvest: grapes are left on the vine longer to build sugar. Late-harvest Riesling and Gewürztraminer are good examples.

Ice wine: grapes freeze on the vine and are pressed frozen, concentrating the sugar. Canada, especially Niagara, and Germany, where it's called Eiswein, are famous for it.

Dried grapes: grapes are dried after picking. Italy's Vin Santo, Recioto and passito wines, and Spain's Pedro Ximénez Sherry are made this way.

Fortified: spirit is added to stop fermentation and keep the sugar. Port, Banyuls, sweet Madeira and Australia's Rutherglen Muscat fall into this group.

Sparkling: Moscato d'Asti and Brachetto d'Acqui are light, fizzy and sweet.

Many sweet wines come in half bottles, which is plenty for four to six people with dessert.

## 8. Best value wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-value-wine`

The best value usually comes from lesser-known regions and grapes, not famous names. Look to Portugal, southern France, Spain, southern Italy, Chile and South Africa for great wine at modest prices.

Famous regions like Burgundy, Bordeaux and Champagne charge a premium for their reputation. Similar styles from nearby or less famous places often cost much less.

Places and wines that regularly over-deliver:

- Portugal: reds from the Douro, Dão and Alentejo, and Vinho Verde for whites.
- Southern France: Languedoc, Côtes du Rhône, Côtes de Gascogne and Picpoul de Pinet.
- Spain: Garnacha from Calatayud and Campo de Borja, Monastrell from Jumilla, Verdejo from Rueda.
- Southern Italy: Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, Nero d'Avola, Primitivo.
- The Loire: Muscadet, Chinon and Saumur.
- Beaujolais: the crus give Burgundy-like pleasure for much less.
- South Africa: Chenin Blanc.
- Chile: Carmenère and Cabernet Sauvignon.

Swap Champagne for Crémant, English sparkling wine or a good Cava.

Other tips:

- Look for "second wines" from top Bordeaux châteaux.
- Try a great producer's entry-level wine, like their regional Bourgogne.
- Buy by the case; many shops offer a discount.
- Build a relationship with a good local wine shop.

## 9. Best wine under $20?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-under-20`

You can find excellent wine under $20 if you skip the famous names and look for regions and grapes known for value. Prices vary a lot by country and tax, but the same places tend to deliver.

For reds, look for:

- Côtes du Rhône and other southern French blends.
- Malbec from Mendoza.
- Portuguese reds from the Douro or Alentejo.
- Montepulciano d'Abruzzo and Nero d'Avola.
- Beaujolais-Villages.
- Spanish Garnacha and Monastrell.
- Chilean Carmenère.

For whites, look for:

- Muscadet, Picpoul de Pinet and Côtes de Gascogne.
- Vinho Verde.
- Verdejo from Rueda.
- South African Chenin Blanc.
- Sauvignon Blanc from Chile or New Zealand.

For sparkling, Cava, Prosecco and many Crémants fit the budget.

What to be careful with: the cheapest bottles from famous, expensive regions. Budget Champagne, Burgundy and Bordeaux often disappoint, because you're paying for the name rather than the quality.

A few tips:

- The difference between a $10 and a $20 bottle is often bigger than between $20 and $40.
- Ask shop staff for their favourite under your budget. They taste a lot.
- Screwcaps are not a sign of cheap wine.

## 10. Best low-alcohol wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-buying-low-alcohol`

German Riesling Kabinett, Moscato d'Asti, Vinho Verde and Lambrusco are some of the best naturally low-alcohol wines, often between 5% and 11%.

Alcohol comes from sugar, so wines from cool climates, or wines that keep some sweetness, usually have less of it.

Good low-alcohol choices:

- Moscato d'Asti: around 5–6%, sweet and lightly sparkling.
- German Riesling Kabinett: often 7.5–10%, with a touch of sweetness and bright acidity.
- Vinho Verde: usually 9–11.5%, light and fresh.
- Lambrusco: often 8–11%, a lightly sparkling red.
- Txakoli from the Spanish Basque Country: around 10.5–11.5%, crisp and slightly spritzy.
- Muscadet: usually around 12% or less.
- Light reds like Beaujolais and some Loire reds: often 12–13%.

For comparison, many full-bodied reds are 14–15% or more.

Always check the ABV on the label, since it can vary a lot between producers and vintages, even for the same style.

If you want to avoid alcohol entirely, look for dealcoholised wine instead. It's made like regular wine, then the alcohol is removed.

Lower alcohol doesn't mean less flavour. Many of these wines are full of aroma and freshness, and they're great for long lunches or warm evenings.
