## 1. Best wine for summer?

- **source_doc:** `qa-season-summer`

Light, fresh and chillable: crisp whites, dry rosé, sparkling wine and light reds served cool. Heat makes heavy, high-alcohol wines feel sluggish, so summer favours freshness.

Crisp whites are the backbone: Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño, Vinho Verde, Picpoul de Pinet, Muscadet and dry Riesling. They're refreshing on their own and great with salads, seafood and grilled fish.

Dry rosé, especially from Provence, is the classic summer wine. It works with almost anything eaten outdoors.

Sparkling wine is never wrong in summer, from Prosecco and Cava to pét-nat.

Red wine still has a place, as long as it's light and cool. Beaujolais, Gamay, Frappato, Pinot Noir and Lambrusco are all great after 20–30 minutes in the fridge.

For barbecues, reach for a juicy red like Zinfandel or Grenache, served slightly chilled, or a fuller rosé.

Practical summer tips:

- Keep everything colder than you think. Warm wine tastes flabby and alcoholic.
- Use an ice bucket with water, not just ice.
- Screwcaps and cans are handy outdoors.
- Keep an eye on alcohol levels on hot days, and drink plenty of water.

## 2. Best wine for winter?

- **source_doc:** `qa-season-winter`

Fuller, warmer wines that suit heavier food: structured reds, rich whites and fortified wines. Winter cooking is slow and hearty, and the wines can match it.

Reds come into their own. Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and Shiraz, Malbec, Rioja Reserva, Barolo, Châteauneuf-du-Pape and Amarone all suit stews, braises and roasts.

Rich whites work too, especially with creamy dishes, roast chicken or fish: oaked Chardonnay, white Burgundy, Viognier, white Rioja and textured Chenin Blanc.

Fortified wines are made for cold evenings. Tawny Port, Oloroso Sherry and Madeira are perfect after dinner, and Port with Stilton is a winter classic.

Winter food pairings to try:

- Beef stew or bourguignon with Burgundy or Rhône reds.
- Roast lamb with Rioja or Bordeaux.
- Cheese fondue with a crisp Savoie or Swiss white.
- Mushroom risotto with Nebbiolo or Pinot Noir.

For holiday gatherings, sparkling wine still belongs on the table, and mulled wine is a good use for inexpensive red.

Serve reds at around 16–18°C, not beside the fire. Even in winter, warm red wine tastes more alcoholic.

## 3. Best wine for fall/autumn?

- **source_doc:** `qa-season-autumn`

Autumn calls for wines with a little more weight and earthiness than summer's: medium-bodied reds, textured whites and anything that loves mushrooms, squash and roast meat.

Pinot Noir is the quintessential autumn red. Its earthy, forest-floor notes suit mushrooms, game birds and roast chicken. Beaujolais crus and Nebbiolo work beautifully too.

Harvest season also suits Rhône blends, Grenache and Cabernet Franc, with their spice and savoury edge.

For whites, move toward texture: oaked Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc, Viognier and Alsace Pinot Gris pair well with squash, pumpkin and roast pork.

Autumn food pairings:

- Squash or pumpkin soup with Chenin Blanc or Viognier.
- Mushroom dishes with Pinot Noir or Nebbiolo.
- Roast pork and apples with Riesling or Chenin Blanc.
- Duck or game with Pinot Noir or Syrah.
- Apple desserts with a sweet Chenin Blanc or Tokaji.

It's also a great time to visit wine regions during harvest, when many wineries hold events and tastings.

Orange wines, with their nutty, dried-fruit character and grippy texture, also feel right as the weather cools.

## 4. Best wine for spring?

- **source_doc:** `qa-season-spring`

Fresh, bright and green-leaning: Sauvignon Blanc, Grüner Veltliner, dry rosé and light reds. Spring food is lighter, full of new vegetables and herbs, and the wines should follow.

Sauvignon Blanc and Grüner Veltliner are ideal with spring vegetables like asparagus, peas and artichokes, which are notoriously hard to pair. Their green, herbal notes echo the food.

Dry Riesling, Albariño and Vermentino suit seafood and salads.

Dry rosé comes back out as the weather warms, and it's great with spring lunches.

Light reds, like Beaujolais, Pinot Noir and Gamay, work well with lamb, a spring classic, and are best served slightly chilled.

Spring food pairings:

- Asparagus with Sauvignon Blanc or Grüner Veltliner.
- Roast spring lamb with Pinot Noir, Rioja or Bordeaux.
- Pea and mint dishes with Sauvignon Blanc.
- Goat cheese salads with Sancerre.
- Brunch with sparkling wine.

Spring is also when many producers release their fresh whites and rosés from the previous harvest, so it's a good time to try new vintages.

## 5. Wine for Super Bowl Sunday?

- **source_doc:** `qa-super-bowl-wine`

Crowd-pleasing, food-friendly wines that handle wings, chips and chili: juicy reds, off-dry whites and plenty of bubbles.

Game-day food is salty, spicy, fried and rich, so the wine needs fruit and freshness rather than finesse.

What works:

- Buffalo wings: off-dry Riesling or a fruity rosé. The sweetness balances the heat.
- Chili: Zinfandel, Malbec or a Grenache blend.
- Burgers and sliders: Zinfandel, Malbec, Shiraz or Tempranillo.
- Nachos and guacamole: Sauvignon Blanc, Albariño or a dry rosé.
- Fried snacks: sparkling wine. Bubbles cut through grease better than almost anything.
- Pizza: Barbera, Chianti or Lambrusco.
- Barbecue ribs and pulled pork: Zinfandel or Shiraz.

Practical tips:

- Go for magnums or several bottles of a couple of styles rather than lots of different ones.
- Screwcaps save time.
- Keep a few lower-alcohol options for a long afternoon.
- Put reds in the fridge for 15–20 minutes. Big reds taste better slightly cool, especially with spicy food.

And keep water and non-alcoholic options on hand, especially for anyone driving home.

## 6. Wine for Mother's Day?

- **source_doc:** `qa-mothers-day-wine`

Something that feels like a treat and suits a spring brunch or lunch: Champagne or another good sparkling wine, a dry rosé, or a bottle from a region she loves.

Sparkling wine is the natural choice for a celebration, and it pairs with almost any brunch dish, including eggs, which trip up most wines. A Crémant or English sparkling wine feels special without costing as much as Champagne.

Dry rosé suits spring lunches, salads and seafood, and it's easy to enjoy.

For a gift, personal beats expensive:

- A wine from a place she's visited or wants to visit.
- A bottle she enjoyed once and mentioned.
- A half bottle of a dessert wine like Sauternes or Moscato d'Asti, paired with her favourite dessert.
- A wine with a note about why you chose it.

If you're cooking, match the wine to the meal: sparkling wine for brunch, Pinot Noir for salmon or lamb, a crisp white for fish.

If she doesn't drink much, a good non-alcoholic sparkling wine or a single glass of something special can mean more than a full bottle.

## 7. Wine for Father's Day?

- **source_doc:** `qa-fathers-day-wine`

Match it to the day: a bold red if there's a barbecue, a special bottle if you're giving a gift, or something from a place that means something to him.

Father's Day often means grilling, so reds that love smoke and char are a safe bet:

- Steak: Cabernet Sauvignon, Malbec or Syrah.
- Burgers and ribs: Zinfandel or Shiraz.
- Sausages: Côtes du Rhône or Grenache.
- Grilled fish: a fuller rosé or Chardonnay.

For a gift, think about what he actually likes rather than what's expensive:

- A classic from a region he enjoys, such as a good Rioja, Bordeaux or Barolo.
- A wine from a trip you took together.
- A bottle to age, if he likes to cellar, with a note to open it on a specific future date.
- A bottle of Port or Madeira for sipping, since both keep for a while after opening.

If he's curious about wine, a mixed case of different regions or a tasting evening together can be a better gift than a single trophy bottle.

And if he prefers beer or spirits, don't force it. A great gift is one he'll enjoy.

## 8. Wine for a book club?

- **source_doc:** `qa-book-club-wine`

Easy, crowd-pleasing wines that don't need food and won't overwhelm conversation: a crisp white, a dry rosé and a smooth, fruity red.

Book clubs usually mean snacks, not a full meal, and long, chatty evenings, so choose wines that are pleasant on their own and not too high in alcohol.

Reliable picks:

- White: Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Albariño or an unoaked Chardonnay.
- Rosé: a dry Provence or Spanish rosé.
- Red: Pinot Noir, Beaujolais, Merlot or a Côtes du Rhône, which are soft and fruity with gentle tannins.
- Sparkling: Prosecco or Cava for a special meeting.

A fun twist: pair the wine with the book. Reading a novel set in Italy? Pour Chianti or Prosecco. Spain? Rioja or Albariño. France? Something from the region where it's set.

Practical tips:

- Plan roughly half a bottle per person for a long evening, and include a non-alcoholic option.
- Choose lower-alcohol wines, around 12–13%, for a long conversation.
- Put out simple snacks like cheese, olives and bread.
- Rotate who brings the wine and let them tell the group why they chose it.

## 9. Wine for a casual night in?

- **source_doc:** `qa-night-in-wine`

Whatever you'd genuinely enjoy with what you're eating. A casual night in is the best time to open an easy, reliable bottle you like, or to try something new without pressure.

Great everyday choices:

- Red: Côtes du Rhône, Beaujolais, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, a Chilean Carmenère or a Portuguese red, all soft, fruity and affordable.
- White: Sauvignon Blanc, Picpoul de Pinet, Pinot Grigio or an unoaked Chardonnay.
- Rosé: a dry rosé for warmer nights.
- Sparkling: a Crémant or Cava, because Tuesday deserves bubbles too.

Match it loosely to dinner:

- Pizza or pasta: Barbera, Chianti or Montepulciano d'Abruzzo.
- Takeout curry or Thai: off-dry Riesling.
- Burgers: Zinfandel or Malbec.
- Sushi: Sauvignon Blanc or sparkling wine.
- Just snacks and a film: something light and fruity.

A few habits that make weeknight wine better:

- Keep a couple of everyday bottles on hand.
- Re-cork and refrigerate what's left; most wine keeps three to five days.
- Pour a measured glass rather than a big one.

It's also the perfect time to open a half bottle, or a style you've been curious about.

## 10. Wine for a business dinner?

- **source_doc:** `qa-business-dinner-wine`

Choose something reliable, food-friendly and moderately priced, and keep the focus on the conversation rather than the wine.

The goal is a bottle nobody has to think about: pleasant, versatile and appropriate. Very cheap can look careless; very expensive can look like showing off.

Safe, versatile choices:

- Red: Pinot Noir, Rioja Crianza or Reserva, Chianti Classico or a Bordeaux from a lesser-known appellation.
- White: Chablis, Sancerre, Albariño or a lightly oaked Chardonnay.
- Sparkling: a glass to start if there's something to celebrate.

How to handle the list:

- If you're hosting, ask the sommelier for a recommendation in a price range. Pointing at a price on the list is normal and discreet.
- Ask guests what they prefer, or choose one red and one white to cover the table.
- Avoid extremes of style, like very oaky, very tannic or very sweet wines.

Etiquette points:

- Pace yourself, and match the table rather than setting the pace.
- It's perfectly acceptable to decline wine or choose a non-alcoholic option.
- Know your company's rules on expenses and alcohol.

The best business-dinner wine is the one nobody remembers, because the meeting went well.
