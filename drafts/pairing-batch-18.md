## 1. Wine with pulled pork?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-pulled-pork`

A juicy, ripe red with soft tannins, like Zinfandel, Shiraz or Grenache, or an off-dry Riesling if the sauce is sweet and spicy. Pulled pork is rich, smoky and usually sweet, so it needs fruit more than structure.

Zinfandel is the classic. Its jammy fruit and peppery spice match smoky, sweet barbecue sauce, and its soft tannins won't fight the meat.

Australian Shiraz, Côtes du Rhône and Spanish Garnacha work for the same reason: ripe fruit, a little spice, nothing too austere.

The sauce decides a lot. Sweet, tomato-based sauces want fruity reds. Tangy, vinegar-based Carolina sauces are lighter and sharper, and a chilled Beaujolais or a dry rosé handles them better than a big red.

If there's real heat, switch to an off-dry Riesling or a fruity rosé. Lower alcohol and a touch of sweetness cool the spice rather than amplifying it.

Pulled pork sandwiches with slaw are a great match for sparkling wine or a crisp rosé, especially outdoors.

What to avoid: highly tannic, oaky reds like young Cabernet Sauvignon. The sweet sauce makes them taste harsh and bitter.

## 2. Wine with ham?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-ham`

An off-dry white, a fruity light red or a dry rosé: Riesling, Beaujolais, Pinot Noir or Provence rosé. Ham is salty and often glazed with something sweet, so the wine needs fruit and freshness rather than tannin.

Glazed ham, with honey, brown sugar or pineapple, pairs best with an off-dry Riesling or a Gewürztraminer. Their touch of sweetness matches the glaze, and their acidity handles the salt.

Beaujolais and Pinot Noir are the best reds for ham. They're light and fruity, with low tannin that won't clash with the salt.

Dry rosé is a crowd-pleaser, especially for a big spread with lots of sides.

Cured hams, like prosciutto, jamón serrano or jamón ibérico, want something dry and fresh. Fino or Manzanilla Sherry is the classic Spanish match, and a crisp sparkling wine like Cava or Prosecco works too.

Smoked ham can take a slightly fuller red, like Zinfandel or a Grenache blend.

Ham and cheese, whether in a sandwich or a croque monsieur, is great with Chardonnay or a crisp white Burgundy.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds. Salt makes tannin taste harsher and more bitter.

## 3. Wine pairing with tomato sauce?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-tomato-sauce`

A red with high acidity, like Sangiovese, Barbera or Montepulciano d'Abruzzo. Tomatoes are acidic, and a wine with less acidity than the sauce tastes flat and flabby next to it.

Italian reds are the natural fit, because they grew up alongside tomato sauce. Chianti and other Sangiovese-based wines have bright cherry and herbal notes that sit perfectly with tomato. Barbera is juicy and low in tannin, great for everyday pasta and pizza. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo is softer and very good value.

Match the sauce's weight too:

- A light, fresh marinara suits a lighter red or even a dry rosé.
- A long-cooked meat sauce or ragù can take Chianti Classico, Langhe Nebbiolo or Primitivo.
- Spicy arrabbiata wants a fruity, lower-alcohol red, so the heat doesn't build.

Beyond pasta, the same logic works for pizza, shakshuka, tomato-based stews and eggplant parmigiana.

Prefer white? Choose one with lots of acidity, like Vermentino, Verdicchio or Sauvignon Blanc. They work especially well with tomato and seafood dishes.

What to avoid: low-acid, oaky wines, which the tomato makes taste dull and heavy.

## 4. Wine with Indian food?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-indian-food`

Aromatic, off-dry whites like Riesling and Gewürztraminer handle most Indian dishes, because a touch of sweetness balances chile heat and their perfume matches the spices.

Indian cooking is too varied for a single answer, so think about heat, richness and spice.

Mild, creamy dishes like korma, butter chicken and malai kofta suit a fuller white: Viognier, Pinot Gris or an off-dry Chenin Blanc.

Tomato-based dishes like tikka masala and rogan josh can take a dry rosé or a soft, fruity red with low tannin, such as Grenache or Gamay.

Hot dishes like vindaloo and chicken 65 need low alcohol and a little sweetness. Off-dry Riesling is the safest choice.

Tandoori and grilled dishes are smoky and less saucy, and pair well with a dry rosé, Grenache or even a light Syrah if the spice is moderate.

Vegetable dishes and dals are earthier and often work with Grüner Veltliner or a dry rosé.

Sparkling wine is a good all-rounder: bubbles and acidity refresh the palate between spicy bites.

What to avoid: high-alcohol, oaky or very tannic wines. Alcohol makes chile burn more, and tannin turns bitter against spice.

## 5. Wine with Asian cuisine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-asian-food`

Off-dry Riesling is the most versatile choice, but "Asian food" covers so many cuisines that the best wine depends on the dish: sweetness, heat, umami and freshness all matter.

Some reliable starting points:

- Chinese: Cantonese dishes suit Riesling, Champagne or a light Pinot Noir; Sichuan heat wants off-dry Riesling or Gewürztraminer; Peking duck loves Pinot Noir.
- Japanese: sushi and sashimi pair with Champagne, Grüner Veltliner or Chablis; teriyaki and yakitori with Pinot Noir or Gamay.
- Thai: off-dry Riesling or Gewürztraminer balances sweet, sour, salty and spicy flavours at once.
- Vietnamese: fresh herbs and lime work with Sauvignon Blanc, Grüner Veltliner or dry rosé.
- Korean: barbecue and gochujang-based dishes suit a juicy red like Zinfandel or Grenache, or an off-dry Riesling for spicier dishes.

A few general rules help. Umami from soy sauce and fish sauce makes tannic reds taste bitter, so keep reds light. Chile heat is amplified by alcohol, so choose lower-alcohol wines for spicy food. Sweet sauces call for a wine with at least a little sweetness.

Sparkling wine is a safe choice for a shared meal with lots of different dishes.

## 6. Wine with mozzarella?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-mozzarella`

A crisp, fresh white like Pinot Grigio, Vermentino or Falanghina, or a dry rosé. Mozzarella is mild, milky and delicate, so the wine should be light and refreshing, not overpowering.

Italian whites from the south are the natural match. Falanghina and Fiano come from Campania, the home of mozzarella di bufala, and their citrus and floral notes suit it perfectly. Greco di Tufo and Vermentino work too.

Caprese salad, with tomato, basil and olive oil, needs a wine with enough acidity for the tomato: Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino or a dry rosé.

Burrata, richer and creamier, can take a white with a little more texture, like a Soave, a lightly oaked Chardonnay or Champagne.

Pizza with mozzarella and tomato sauce shifts the pairing toward light reds with good acidity: Barbera, Chianti, Montepulciano d'Abruzzo or a chilled Lambrusco.

Fried mozzarella and mozzarella sticks love sparkling wine, whose bubbles cut through the fried coating.

What to avoid: heavy, oaky whites and tannic reds. They overwhelm the cheese's gentle flavour.

## 7. Wine with feta?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-feta`

A crisp, high-acid white like Assyrtiko, Sauvignon Blanc or Picpoul, or a dry rosé. Feta is salty and tangy, and it needs a wine with bright acidity to match its sharpness.

Greek wines are the obvious starting point. Assyrtiko from Santorini is bone-dry, saline and mineral, a natural partner for feta. Moschofilero is lighter and more aromatic, and Malagousia is another good option.

Sauvignon Blanc from the Loire or New Zealand works well, as do Picpoul de Pinet and Vermentino.

Greek salad, with tomato, olives, cucumber and onion, is fresh and acidic, so a crisp white or a dry rosé is best.

Baked feta, often with honey, herbs or tomatoes, is richer and can take an off-dry white or a fuller rosé.

Spanakopita and other pastries with feta and spinach suit a crisp white with a little texture, like Assyrtiko or Chablis.

Feta with watermelon is a summer favourite that pairs beautifully with a dry rosé.

Prefer red? Pick a light, fresh one with low tannin, like Xinomavro rosé or a chilled Gamay, since salt makes tannin taste harsh.

## 8. Wine with ice cream?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-ice-cream`

Honestly, it's tricky. Cold numbs your palate, and ice cream is very sweet, so most wines taste thin or sour next to it. The ones that work are sweet, rich and full of flavour.

Pedro Ximénez Sherry is the classic answer. It's intensely sweet, with raisin, fig and molasses flavours, and it's traditionally poured straight over vanilla ice cream in Spain.

Other good matches:

- Vanilla ice cream: Pedro Ximénez, Tawny Port or sweet Madeira.
- Chocolate ice cream: Ruby Port, Banyuls or a Late Bottled Vintage Port.
- Coffee or caramel ice cream: Tawny Port or a sweet Oloroso Sherry.
- Fruit sorbets: Moscato d'Asti, Brachetto d'Acqui or a late-harvest Riesling.
- Berry ice cream: Brachetto d'Acqui, whose red-fruit flavours match it well.

A simple trick is to pour the wine over the ice cream rather than drinking it alongside. PX and Tawny Port are especially good this way.

What to avoid: dry wines of any kind, including dry sparkling wine. The sugar makes them taste harsh and sour.

Served very cold, sweet wines work better with frozen desserts, so chill them well.

## 9. Wine for a celebration?

- **source_doc:** `qa-celebration-wine`

Sparkling wine, almost always. Champagne if the occasion calls for it, but Crémant, Cava, English sparkling wine and Franciacorta can be just as good for much less.

Why bubbles? They feel festive, they work as an aperitif and with most food, and they suit a crowd because almost everyone enjoys a glass.

Choose the level to match the moment:

- Big party: Prosecco or Cava, easy and affordable.
- Dinner or special toast: Crémant or a good English sparkling wine.
- Milestone: Champagne, maybe a vintage bottle or a magnum.

Magnums make any celebration feel bigger, and sparkling wine arguably ages better in them.

Beyond bubbles, think about the meal. A celebratory dinner might move from Champagne to a special red, like a good Burgundy or a Barolo with some age, and finish with a sweet wine.

Practical tips:

- Chill sparkling wine well, around 6–8°C.
- Open it slowly, with a gentle sigh rather than a loud pop, to keep the bubbles.
- Pour into a normal wine glass rather than a narrow flute to smell it properly.
- Plan on about five glasses per bottle.

The best celebration wine is the one people remember drinking together.

## 10. Wine for Easter?

- **source_doc:** `qa-easter-wine`

It depends on the main dish, and Easter usually means lamb, ham or fish. Pinot Noir is the most flexible choice, since it works with all three.

For roast lamb, choose a savoury red with some structure: Bordeaux, Rioja, a Côtes du Rhône or a fuller Pinot Noir. Herbs like rosemary and garlic point toward southern French reds.

For glazed ham, pick an off-dry Riesling, a Beaujolais or a dry rosé. They handle the salt and the sweet glaze better than big reds.

For salmon or other fish, go with Pinot Noir, a dry rosé or a lightly oaked Chardonnay.

For a vegetarian Easter, with spring vegetables, asparagus and eggs, Sauvignon Blanc and Grüner Veltliner are ideal.

Brunch is a great excuse for sparkling wine, which also works with eggs, a notoriously tricky food for wine.

For dessert, whether chocolate eggs or hot cross buns, pour a Tawny Port, a Moscato d'Asti or, with chocolate, a Banyuls.

Easter falls in spring, so fresh, lighter styles often feel right: rosé, light reds and crisp whites.

Hosting a crowd? Pick one versatile red, one white and a sparkling wine, and you'll cover most of the table.
