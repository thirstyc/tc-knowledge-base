## 1. What is sulfites in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-are-sulfites`

Sulfites are sulfur dioxide and related compounds used in winemaking as a preservative. They stop wine from oxidising and protect it from unwanted bacteria and yeast.

Every wine contains some, because fermentation produces sulfites naturally, usually around 10 milligrams per litre. Winemakers then add more, at the crush, during ageing or at bottling.

How much varies by style. Reds generally need less, because their tannins also protect the wine. Whites need more, and sweet wines the most, since residual sugar is an invitation to microbes.

Legal limits differ by country and category, and organic and biodynamic certifications set stricter ones. Wines labelled "no added sulfites" still contain the small amount fermentation creates.

Labels in many countries must say "contains sulfites" above a low threshold, which is why the phrase appears on almost every bottle, including ones with very little.

For context, dried fruit typically contains far more sulfites per gram than wine does.

Low-sulfite wines can be wonderful, but they're more fragile: store them cool and drink them younger.

## 2. Do sulfites cause headaches?

- **source_doc:** `qa-sulfites-headaches`

Probably not, for most people. Sulfites are a common scapegoat for wine headaches, but the evidence doesn't support it, and there are more likely explanations.

Sulfite sensitivity is real but uncommon, and it mainly affects a minority of people with asthma, where it triggers breathing symptoms rather than headaches.

The clearest argument against the sulfite theory is comparison. White and sweet wines contain more sulfites than reds, yet red wine is the one people most often blame for headaches. Dried apricots contain far more sulfites than any wine and rarely get blamed.

More likely culprits include alcohol itself, which dilates blood vessels and dehydrates you; histamines and tyramine, found in higher amounts in red wine; tannins, which some research links to headaches; and simply drinking more than you meant to.

Practical steps: drink water alongside, eat something, go slower, and see whether a particular style or producer is the problem rather than wine in general.

If wine reliably gives you headaches or other symptoms, that's worth raising with a doctor rather than self-diagnosing a sulfite allergy.

## 3. What is fermentation in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-fermentation`

Fermentation is how grape juice becomes wine: yeast eats the sugar in the juice and produces alcohol, carbon dioxide and heat.

The equation is simple, but the choices around it define the wine.

Yeast is the first. Some winemakers use ambient yeasts already present on the grapes and in the cellar, which gives less predictable, often more complex results. Others add selected cultured yeast for a reliable ferment.

Temperature is the second. Cool fermentation, common for whites, preserves delicate fruit and floral aromas. Warmer fermentation, common for reds, extracts more colour, tannin and flavour from the skins.

Colour comes from contact, not the juice. Almost all grape juice is clear, so red wine gets its colour by fermenting with the skins, while white wine is usually pressed first.

Fermentation stops when the yeast runs out of sugar, giving a dry wine, or when it's halted deliberately by chilling, filtering or adding spirit, which leaves sweetness behind.

A ferment that stops early by accident is called stuck, and it's every winemaker's least favourite phone call.

Most wines then undergo a second, different process: malolactic conversion.

## 4. What is oak aging?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-oak-ageing`

Oak ageing means keeping wine in oak barrels, which adds flavour and texture and lets in a tiny amount of oxygen that softens the wine over time.

Oak gives vanilla, baking spice, toast, smoke and sometimes coconut or dill. It also adds a little tannin, and the slow, controlled oxygen exchange through the wood rounds the wine's edges.

The main variables:

- New versus used: new barrels give the most flavour; older ones mostly provide gentle oxygen exchange.
- French versus American: French oak tends toward subtle spice and fine tannin; American oak gives sweeter vanilla, coconut and dill.
- Size: small barrels, like the 225-litre barrique, give more oak character than large casks, which is why traditional Barolo and Rioja producers favour big old ones.
- Toast level: barrels are charred to different degrees, which changes how smoky or sweet they taste.

Cheaper alternatives exist. Oak chips, staves or powder give flavour without the cost of barrels, and many inexpensive wines use them.

Oak is seasoning, not an ingredient. Used well you barely notice it; used heavily it buries the fruit, which is why "unoaked" became a selling point.

## 5. What is malolactic fermentation?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-malolactic`

Malolactic conversion is a second process after alcoholic fermentation, in which bacteria turn sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid. It makes wine rounder and less tart.

Think of the difference between a green apple, which is full of malic acid, and milk, which contains lactic acid. That's the shift in the glass.

It's technically not a fermentation at all, since bacteria rather than yeast do the work and no alcohol is produced, which is why many people now call it malolactic conversion.

Nearly all red wines go through it, both to soften them and to make them microbially stable. For whites it's a style decision: many Chardonnays, especially in Burgundy, use it for a rounder texture, while crisp styles like Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and most Pinot Grigio block it to keep their zip.

One by-product is diacetyl, the compound behind the buttery aroma in some Chardonnays. That famous butter note comes from here, not from oak.

Winemakers can encourage it by warming the cellar or adding bacteria, or prevent it with sulfur, cold and filtration.

## 6. What is terroir?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-terroir`

Terroir is the idea that a wine tastes like where it comes from. It covers soil, climate, topography and the local traditions of the people who farm there.

The four parts, roughly:

- Soil: drainage, heat retention and nutrients, which shape how the vine grows.
- Climate: temperature, rainfall and sunshine over the season.
- Topography: slope, altitude and which way the vineyard faces, which change how much sun and air it gets.
- Human practice: which grapes are planted, how vines are pruned, when the grapes are picked.

Burgundy is the classic demonstration. One grape, one winemaker, two neighbouring plots, and two noticeably different wines.

One common misunderstanding: vines don't absorb flavour from rocks. A wine grown on slate doesn't taste of slate because it drank slate. Soil affects wine mostly through drainage, temperature and vine vigour, and "minerality" is a description of a sensation, not a chemical transfer.

Terroir is also why European labels name places rather than grapes: the place is understood to be the more important information.

It's a real, observable effect, and also a marketing term. Both things are true.

## 7. How does climate affect wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-climate-and-wine`

Climate decides how much sugar and acidity grapes have at harvest, which in turn sets the wine's alcohol, freshness and flavour.

In cool climates, grapes ripen slowly and keep more acidity, so wines are lighter, fresher and lower in alcohol, with green apple, citrus and red-fruit flavours and herbal notes. Think Chablis, Mosel Riesling and Champagne.

In warm climates, grapes ripen fully and build sugar, giving fuller, riper wines with higher alcohol and flavours of tropical fruit, black fruit and jam. Think Barossa Shiraz or Napa Cabernet.

The difference between day and night temperatures matters too. Big swings, common at altitude, let grapes ripen while keeping acidity, which is why high-altitude Argentine Malbec tastes fresh despite the sunshine.

Maritime climates, moderated by the sea, have mild, even seasons. Continental ones, inland, have hot summers and cold winters, and more variation between vintages.

Which is why vintage matters more in cool regions: in a marginal climate, one wet or cool summer changes everything. In consistently warm regions, vintages vary much less.

Warming is now changing this map, pushing some classic regions toward riper styles and making cooler places, like England and Tasmania, newly viable.

## 8. What is appellation control?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-appellation`

An appellation is a legally defined wine region, and appellation control is the set of rules that go with it: where the grapes come from, which varieties are allowed, and often how the wine is made.

France's system, the AOC or AOP, was built in the early twentieth century largely to stop fraud, and the model spread. Italy has DOC and DOCG, Spain DO and DOCa, Portugal DOC, and the EU groups them under PDO.

Typical rules cover the boundary of the region, permitted grapes, maximum yields, minimum alcohol, and sometimes ageing requirements, like Rioja's Crianza and Reserva categories.

Most systems also have a looser tier, such as IGP in France or IGT in Italy, which allows grapes or methods the strict appellation forbids. Some superb wines sit in these categories by choice, the Super Tuscans being the famous example.

The United States uses AVAs, which define geographic boundaries but say almost nothing about grapes or methods.

The key thing to understand: an appellation guarantees origin and compliance with rules, not quality. There are dull wines inside famous appellations and brilliant ones outside them.

## 9. How do I know if my wine is ready to drink?

- **source_doc:** `qa-when-to-drink-wine`

Assume it's ready. The vast majority of wine is made to be drunk on release, and only a small fraction improves with age.

If it's a wine that can age, a few signals help. Colour: young reds are purple at the rim, older ones brick or orange; whites deepen from pale straw to gold. Aroma: fresh fruit gives way to dried fruit, leather, mushroom and nuts. Texture: tannins that felt grippy soften over time.

Practical approaches:

- Check what the producer says. Many list a drinking window on their website or back label.
- Buy several bottles and open one every year or two. It's the only way to learn a wine's curve.
- Use vintage charts as a rough guide, not gospel.
- Trust the structure: high acidity and firm tannin mean it can wait; soft, fruity and low-acid means drink up.

Storage decides as much as time. A bottle kept warm ages fast and badly.

If a wine seems closed or clumsy, decant it and give it an hour.

And remember that far more good bottles are opened too late than too early.

## 10. Why do vintage years matter?

- **source_doc:** `qa-why-vintage-matters`

The vintage is the year the grapes were picked, and because weather changes every year, so does the wine. In some regions that difference is dramatic; in others it barely registers.

Weather shapes everything: a cool, wet summer gives lighter, higher-acid, sometimes underripe wines; a hot, dry one gives riper, fuller, higher-alcohol wines; rain at harvest dilutes the fruit; hail or frost can wipe out much of a crop.

Vintage matters most in marginal, cool climates like Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne, Germany and the Loire, where ripening is never guaranteed. It matters least in consistently warm regions like much of California, Chile, Australia and southern Spain.

It also affects how long a wine will keep. Structured vintages age longer; soft, ripe ones drink well earlier.

A caution about vintage charts: they average a whole region, and a good producer in a difficult year usually makes better wine than a weak one in a celebrated year. Producer first, vintage second.

Non-vintage wine, common in Champagne, blends several years deliberately to keep a house style consistent.

If you're buying to drink soon, vintage matters far less than most people think.
