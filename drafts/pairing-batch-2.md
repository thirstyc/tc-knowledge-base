## 1. What wine pairs with lamb?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-lamb`

A structured red with some savoury character: Bordeaux, Rioja, Syrah or a Grenache blend. Lamb is rich and a little gamey, so it wants a wine with enough tannin and flavour to match.

Roast leg or rack of lamb is a classic partner for red Bordeaux, especially from Pauillac, where Cabernet's cedar and blackcurrant meet the meat's richness. A Rioja Reserva works just as well and often costs less.

Herbs change the picture. Rosemary, thyme and garlic point toward the southern Rhône: Côtes du Rhône, Gigondas or Châteauneuf-du-Pape, where Grenache and Syrah carry the same garrigue notes.

Slow-cooked shoulder and tagines with cumin, cinnamon and dried fruit suit riper, spicier reds like Grenache, Malbec or a Barossa Shiraz.

Lamb chops and kebabs off the grill love Syrah. The char brings out its smoky, peppery side.

Pink, delicately cooked lamb can take a lighter red too: a fuller Pinot Noir from Burgundy or Central Otago is lovely here.

Mint sauce is the tricky one. Its sugar and vinegar flatten most reds, so go light on it, or pick a Cabernet that already has a minty edge, like one from Coonawarra.

## 2. Best wine for duck?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-duck`

Pinot Noir is the classic answer. Its acidity cuts through duck's rich fat, and its red-cherry, earthy flavours suit the slightly gamey meat.

Roast or pan-seared duck breast is where red Burgundy shines. Oregon and New Zealand Pinot Noir work too, with a little more fruit.

Duck with a fruit sauce, like cherry or plum, likes a riper red with matching fruit: a New World Pinot, a Beaujolais cru like Morgon, or even a Zinfandel.

Duck confit and cassoulet come from southwest France, and so do their best partners. Cahors, made from Malbec, and Madiran, made from Tannat, have the structure to stand up to all that fat and beans.

Duck à l'orange and Peking duck bring sweetness and spice. An off-dry Riesling or an Alsace Gewürztraminer handles those flavours better than most reds, and a juicy Pinot Noir is a good red option.

Smoked duck pairs well with Syrah or a northern Rhône red, whose smoky, peppery notes echo the meat.

What to avoid: very oaky, high-alcohol reds, which can make duck feel heavy, and delicate whites, which disappear next to it.

## 3. Wine with chicken?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-chicken`

Chicken goes with almost anything, so pick the wine for the sauce and the cooking method, not the bird.

A simple roast chicken is one of the best dishes for wine. White Burgundy or another lightly oaked Chardonnay suits the crispy skin and juices, and a Pinot Noir or Beaujolais works if you prefer red.

Lemon, herbs and garlic call for something fresh and zesty: Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino or a dry Riesling.

Creamy sauces and mushrooms want a fuller white with some texture, like oaked Chardonnay or Chenin Blanc from Vouvray or South Africa.

Coq au vin is traditionally made with red Burgundy, so drink the same.

Fried chicken and sparkling wine are a genuinely great match. The bubbles and acidity cut through the crispy coating and grease.

Barbecued, jerk or smoky grilled chicken can take a juicy red like Zinfandel or Grenache, or an off-dry Riesling if there's heat.

Spiced dishes like chicken tikka masala suit an aromatic, off-dry white such as Gewürztraminer, or a dry rosé.

Grilled chicken salad? Keep it light: a dry rosé or a crisp white.

## 4. What wine goes with salmon?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-salmon`

Pinot Noir. Salmon is one of the few fish rich enough for red wine, and Pinot's low tannin and bright acidity won't make it taste metallic.

Grilled, roasted or pan-seared salmon is the classic match for Pinot Noir from Oregon, Burgundy or New Zealand. A dry rosé works just as well, especially in summer.

Richer preparations, like salmon in butter or cream sauce, pair well with a fuller white: lightly oaked Chardonnay or a white Burgundy such as Meursault.

Smoked salmon wants bubbles or bright acidity. Champagne, especially a Blanc de Blancs, is a classic, and a dry Riesling or Sauvignon Blanc also works.

Raw salmon in sushi, sashimi or tartare is delicate and clean. Choose a crisp, unoaked white like Chablis, Albariño or Grüner Veltliner.

Teriyaki or miso-glazed salmon brings sweetness and umami. A juicy Pinot Noir or an off-dry Riesling handles both.

Spicy salmon dishes suit an off-dry Riesling too.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds like young Cabernet Sauvignon. The tannin clashes with the fish oils and leaves a metallic taste.

## 5. Wine with oysters?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-oysters`

Muscadet, Chablis or Champagne. Oysters are briny, cold and delicate, so they need a dry, crisp white with plenty of acidity and a salty, mineral edge.

Muscadet is the classic. It comes from near the Atlantic coast at the mouth of the Loire, it's light and lemony, and it's cheap enough to order by the bottle. Look for "sur lie" on the label, which means extra time on the yeast and a little more texture.

Chablis is the other classic. Many of its vineyards sit on Kimmeridgian soil, a limestone and clay packed with fossilised oyster shells. Whether or not that explains it, the pairing works.

Champagne and other dry sparkling wines add bubbles to cut through the brininess. Blanc de Blancs, made only from Chardonnay, is especially good.

Other great choices: Picpoul de Pinet from the Mediterranean coast, where oysters are farmed nearby, Albariño from Galicia, and Sancerre.

Cooked oysters, like fried oysters or oysters Rockefeller, can take a slightly fuller white or a sparkling wine.

What to skip: oaky whites and red wine, which overwhelm the delicate, salty flavour.

## 6. Wine with pork?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-pork`

Pork is one of the most wine-friendly meats. It works with both whites and light reds, so match the wine to the cut and the sauce.

Roast pork loin or tenderloin is lean and mild. Pinot Noir, Beaujolais or a medium-bodied white like Chenin Blanc or dry Riesling are all good choices.

Pork with apples or other fruit calls for a white with some fruit and acidity, such as Riesling, Chenin Blanc or an Alsace Pinot Gris.

Pork belly and other fatty cuts want acidity to cut the richness. Off-dry Riesling, Alsace Pinot Gris or a Grenache-based red all work.

Sausages, especially spicy or herby ones, pair well with Syrah, Côtes du Rhône or Zinfandel.

Pork chops, grilled or pan-fried, suit a juicy red like Pinot Noir, Barbera or Grenache.

Asian pork dishes, like char siu or sweet and sour pork, have sweetness and spice. Off-dry Riesling, Gewürztraminer or a fruity Pinot Noir will handle them.

Barbecued or pulled pork with a sweet, smoky sauce wants a riper red: Zinfandel or Shiraz.

What to avoid: very tannic, heavy reds with lean cuts. They overwhelm the meat's mild flavour.

## 7. What wine goes with turkey?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-turkey`

A fruity, medium-bodied red like Pinot Noir or Beaujolais, or a rich white like Chardonnay. Turkey itself is lean and mild, so the wine mostly needs to handle everything else on the plate.

A turkey dinner usually comes with stuffing, gravy, cranberry sauce and a dozen sides. That's why flexible wines with good acidity and plenty of fruit work best.

Pinot Noir is the go-to red. It's light enough for the white meat, and its red-berry flavours match cranberry sauce.

Beaujolais crus like Morgon or Fleurie are juicy, low in tannin and easy to share, and they don't cost much, which helps with a big table.

Zinfandel is a traditional choice in North America. Its ripe fruit and spice suit dark meat, stuffing and rich gravy.

For white, a Chardonnay with some oak or a dry to off-dry Riesling works well, especially with sweet sides like squash or sweet potatoes.

Sparkling wine is a smart starter and keeps working through the meal.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds. They can make the turkey taste dry and clash with cranberry sauce.

Buying for a crowd? Pick two bottles, one red and one white, and let people choose.

## 8. Wine with pasta?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-pasta`

Match the wine to the sauce, not the pasta. A good rule: if the dish is Italian, an Italian wine from the same region is often the best partner.

Tomato sauces, like marinara or arrabbiata, are acidic, so they need a wine with plenty of acidity. Sangiovese, including Chianti, Barbera and Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, are ideal.

Meat sauces like Bolognese or ragù can take a slightly bigger red: Chianti Classico, Barbera, Langhe Nebbiolo or Primitivo.

Cream-based sauces, like Alfredo, suit a fuller white such as lightly oaked Chardonnay, Soave or Fiano.

Carbonara and cacio e pepe are rich and salty. A crisp Roman white like Frascati, a Verdicchio or a light red like Barbera cuts through them.

Pesto is herby and garlicky. Vermentino, from the same part of Italy as the sauce, is a natural match, as is Sauvignon Blanc.

Seafood pasta, like spaghetti alle vongole, wants something light and fresh: Vermentino, Verdicchio, Pinot Grigio or Albariño.

Mushroom or truffle pasta pairs beautifully with Nebbiolo or Pinot Noir.

What to avoid: heavy, oaky reds with delicate or creamy sauces. They drown the dish.

## 9. Best wine for mushroom risotto?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-mushroom-risotto`

Pinot Noir or a Piedmont red like Barbera or Nebbiolo. Mushrooms are earthy and savoury, and these wines share those flavours without overwhelming the creamy rice.

Pinot Noir is the classic choice. Its red fruit and forest-floor notes, especially in red Burgundy, sit perfectly alongside mushrooms.

Mushroom risotto is at home in northern Italy, so Piedmont wines make sense too. Barbera is juicy and fresh, with enough acidity to cut through the butter and Parmesan. Langhe Nebbiolo adds more structure.

If there's truffle, reach for Nebbiolo, ideally a Barolo or Barbaresco with some age. Truffle and aged Nebbiolo are a famous combination.

Prefer white? Pick one with some body and texture, like an oaked Chardonnay or white Burgundy, which matches the creaminess.

For a more adventurous pairing, try a dry Sherry like Fino or Amontillado. Its nutty, savoury flavour and umami echo the mushrooms.

What to avoid: very tannic reds like young Cabernet Sauvignon. Mushrooms are rich in umami, which makes tannin taste harsher and more bitter.

## 10. Wine with sushi?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-sushi`

A crisp, dry white or a dry sparkling wine: Champagne, Grüner Veltliner, Albariño or dry Riesling. Sushi is delicate, and it comes with soy sauce, wasabi and pickled ginger, so the wine needs to be fresh and not too heavy.

Sparkling wine is the easy answer. Its acidity and bubbles refresh the palate between bites and handle the saltiness of soy sauce.

Grüner Veltliner has a peppery note that works with wasabi. Albariño, Chablis and Muscadet bring a salty, mineral freshness that suits raw fish.

Dry or off-dry Riesling is especially good with spicy rolls or sweet sauces, since a touch of sweetness balances heat.

Match the fish. Lean white fish like sea bream or flounder suits the lightest wines. Fattier fish like salmon and tuna can take a dry rosé, or even a light, chilled red like Pinot Noir or Gamay.

Eel with sweet sauce (unagi) pairs well with off-dry Riesling or a fruity Pinot Noir.

What to avoid: oaky whites and tannic reds. Oak overwhelms the fish, and tannin can clash with it, leaving a metallic taste.

Don't forget sake. It's made for this food and worth exploring alongside wine.
