## 1. Pinot Noir vs Cabernet Sauvignon?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-pinot-cabernet`

They sit at opposite ends of the red wine spectrum. Pinot Noir is light, silky and perfumed; Cabernet Sauvignon is dark, firm and structured.

It starts with the grape skins. Pinot Noir has thin skins, so it makes pale wines with gentle tannins. Cabernet has thick skins, giving deep colour and plenty of tannin.

Flavours differ too. Pinot Noir tastes of red cherry, raspberry and, with age, mushroom and forest floor. Cabernet leans black: blackcurrant, cedar, graphite and often a minty or herbal note.

In the vineyard, Pinot Noir is fussy and needs a cool climate; Cabernet ripens late and prefers warmth, which is why it thrives in Bordeaux, Napa and Coonawarra.

At the table, Pinot Noir is the more flexible of the two. It works with salmon, chicken, duck and mushrooms. Cabernet wants fat and protein: steak, lamb, aged cheese.

Both age well, but differently. Cabernet softens over a decade or more; Pinot Noir trades fruit for savoury complexity.

Which to choose? If you want elegance and food-friendliness, Pinot Noir. If you want power and structure, Cabernet Sauvignon.

## 2. Cabernet vs Shiraz?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-cabernet-shiraz`

Both are full-bodied reds, but Cabernet Sauvignon is structured and savoury while Shiraz is richer, rounder and spicier.

Cabernet tastes of blackcurrant, cedar and graphite, with firm, grippy tannins and a tighter, more linear shape.

Shiraz, the Australian name for Syrah, gives blackberry, plum, chocolate and pepper, with softer tannins and a plusher, more generous feel. Cooler-climate Syrah is more peppery and restrained; warm-climate Shiraz is fuller and riper.

Australia is the easiest place to compare them: Coonawarra and Margaret River for Cabernet, Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale for Shiraz. The two are often blended there, a classic Australian combination that balances Cabernet's structure with Shiraz's flesh.

With food, Cabernet suits steak, lamb and aged cheese, where fat softens tannin. Shiraz handles barbecue, sausages, braised beef and smoky, spiced dishes.

Both can age, though Cabernet usually rewards patience more.

Which to choose? If you like a firm, dry finish, Cabernet. If you prefer something rounder and more fruit-forward, Shiraz.

## 3. Syrah vs Grenache?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-syrah-grenache`

They're the two pillars of the Rhône Valley and they play opposite roles: Syrah brings colour, structure and pepper; Grenache brings ripe fruit, warmth and alcohol.

Syrah is dark and firm, with blackberry, black pepper, olive and smoke. In the northern Rhône it makes serious, age-worthy wines.

Grenache is paler with softer tannins, tasting of strawberry, raspberry and dried herbs. It ripens late and builds a lot of sugar, so its wines are often 14.5% alcohol or more.

That contrast is why they're blended in the southern Rhône, in wines like Châteauneuf-du-Pape and Côtes du Rhône, often with Mourvèdre. Grenache fills the middle, Syrah adds backbone and colour.

With food, Syrah suits grilled meat, peppercorn sauce and smoky dishes. Grenache is the friendlier one, good with lamb, roast vegetables, charcuterie and spiced stews.

Grenache also makes some of the best rosé, especially in Provence and the southern Rhône, and sweet fortified wines in Roussillon.

Which to choose? Syrah for structure and spice; Grenache for warmth and easy drinking.

## 4. Tempranillo vs Garnacha?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-tempranillo-garnacha`

Spain's two great red grapes. Tempranillo is savoury, structured and often oak-aged; Garnacha is fruity, warm and softer.

Tempranillo tastes of red cherry, dried fig, leather and tobacco. It has moderate acidity and firm tannins, and it takes oak well, which is why Rioja and Ribera del Duero age their wines for years in barrel. The name comes from "temprano", meaning early, because it ripens ahead of other grapes.

Garnacha, the same grape as French Grenache, is riper and rounder, with strawberry and raspberry fruit, soft tannins and higher alcohol. It shows best in old vineyards in places like Priorat, Calatayud and Campo de Borja.

In Rioja the two are often blended, with Tempranillo leading and Garnacha adding flesh and fruit.

At the table, Tempranillo suits roast lamb, grilled meat and hard cheeses like Manchego. Garnacha is more flexible, good with roast vegetables, charcuterie and spiced dishes.

Value note: old-vine Garnacha from lesser-known regions is some of the best-value red wine in the world.

Which to choose? Tempranillo for savoury, oak-aged classics; Garnacha for generous fruit.

## 5. Gamay vs Pinot Noir?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-gamay-pinot`

Both are light, low-tannin reds from Burgundy's neighbourhood, but Gamay is juicier and more playful, while Pinot Noir is more perfumed and serious.

Gamay is the grape of Beaujolais. It tastes of red cherry, raspberry and banana or bubblegum in some styles, a result of the carbonic maceration often used there, where whole grapes ferment in a sealed tank.

Pinot Noir, from Burgundy just to the north, is more delicate and aromatic, with red cherry and earthy, savoury notes that deepen with age.

Gamay is usually cheaper. The Beaujolais crus, like Morgon, Fleurie and Moulin-à-Vent, make more structured wines that can age for several years, and they cost a fraction of village Burgundy.

Both love the same food: roast chicken, charcuterie, mushrooms, salmon and soft cheese. Both are best served slightly chilled.

There's history here too. In 1395 the Duke of Burgundy banned Gamay from the Côte d'Or in favour of Pinot Noir, which is part of why the two grapes ended up in different places.

Which to choose? Gamay for value and easy pleasure; Pinot Noir when you want complexity.

## 6. Chardonnay vs Sauvignon Blanc?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-chardonnay-sauvignon`

Chardonnay is shaped by the winemaker; Sauvignon Blanc is shaped by the grape. That's the core difference between the world's two most popular white grapes.

Chardonnay has a fairly neutral flavour of its own, so oak, malolactic conversion and lees ageing define its style. It ranges from lean and mineral in Chablis to buttery and tropical in warm climates.

Sauvignon Blanc is intensely aromatic no matter where it grows: cut grass, green pepper, grapefruit and passion fruit, with high acidity. It's usually fermented in steel to protect those aromas and drunk young.

Body is the other difference. Chardonnay is medium to full-bodied with softer acidity; Sauvignon Blanc is light to medium with sharper acidity.

With food, Chardonnay suits richer dishes: roast chicken, creamy sauces, lobster. Sauvignon Blanc goes with goat cheese, salads, asparagus, herbs and seafood, and it's one of the few wines that handles vinaigrette.

Chardonnay can age, especially from Burgundy. Most Sauvignon Blanc is best within a couple of years.

Which to choose? Chardonnay for texture and richness; Sauvignon Blanc for freshness and aroma.

## 7. Riesling vs Sauvignon Blanc?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-riesling-sauvignon`

Both are high-acid, aromatic whites, but Riesling smells of flowers and stone fruit and can be sweet or dry, while Sauvignon Blanc is herbaceous, citrusy and almost always dry.

Riesling gives lime, green apple, peach and floral notes, plus a distinctive petrol-like aroma as it ages that fans love. It's usually unoaked, often lower in alcohol, and made in every style from bone-dry to lusciously sweet.

Sauvignon Blanc is greener and zestier: grass, gooseberry, green pepper and grapefruit. Those notes come from compounds called pyrazines, which are stronger in cooler climates.

Ageing is the big divide. Great Riesling can improve for decades, developing honey and toast. Most Sauvignon Blanc is meant for drinking young, though oaked white Bordeaux is an exception.

With food, Riesling, especially off-dry, is the better choice for spicy food, pork and Asian dishes. Sauvignon Blanc shines with goat cheese, salads, herbs and seafood.

One label tip: German Riesling isn't always dry. Look for "trocken" if that's what you want.

Which to choose? Riesling for versatility and ageing; Sauvignon Blanc for immediate, zesty refreshment.

## 8. Pinot Grigio vs Chardonnay?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-pinot-grigio-chardonnay`

Pinot Grigio is light, crisp and simple; Chardonnay is fuller, rounder and far more variable.

Pinot Grigio, in its common Italian style, is picked early to keep it fresh. Expect lemon, green apple and pear, light body, high acidity and no oak. It's an easy, refreshing wine that rarely demands attention.

Chardonnay covers much more ground. Unoaked versions like Chablis are crisp and mineral, not far from Pinot Grigio in spirit. Oaked versions add vanilla, toast and a creamy texture, and can be full-bodied and rich.

Note the naming: the same grape as Pinot Grigio is called Pinot Gris in France, where Alsace makes a richer, spicier and sometimes off-dry style closer in weight to Chardonnay.

With food, Pinot Grigio suits light seafood, salads and antipasti. Chardonnay handles richer dishes: roast chicken, creamy pasta, lobster.

Price and ageing differ too. Most Pinot Grigio is inexpensive and best young; Chardonnay runs from cheap to some of the most expensive white wine in the world, and the best can age for decades.

Which to choose? Pinot Grigio for easy refreshment; Chardonnay when you want texture and depth.

## 9. Chablis vs Chardonnay?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-chablis-chardonnay`

Chablis is Chardonnay. It's a place in northern Burgundy, and by law its wines are made only from that grape, so the question is really Chablis versus other styles of Chardonnay.

What sets Chablis apart is its cool climate and its soil. Its vineyards sit on Kimmeridgian limestone and clay, full of ancient marine fossils, and the wines taste taut and stony as a result: green apple, lemon, wet stone and a saline finish.

Most Chablis sees little or no new oak, especially at village level, which keeps it lean and precise. Premier Cru and Grand Cru wines have more depth and can age for a decade or more.

Warmer regions make a very different Chardonnay. California, Australia and southern France give riper fruit, peach and tropical notes, often with oak adding vanilla and a creamy texture.

With food, Chablis is the classic partner for oysters and raw seafood. Richer Chardonnay suits lobster, roast chicken and cream sauces.

So: if someone says they don't like Chardonnay but love Chablis, they don't dislike the grape. They dislike oak.

## 10. Champagne vs Prosecco?

- **source_doc:** `qa-compare-champagne-prosecco`

They're made differently, from different grapes, in different countries, and they taste it. Champagne is complex and bready; Prosecco is fresh and fruity.

Champagne comes from the Champagne region of France, made mostly from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. Its second fermentation happens inside the bottle, and the wine then rests on the spent yeast for at least 15 months, often much longer. That's where the toast, brioche and fine, persistent bubbles come from.

Prosecco comes from northeastern Italy, made from the Glera grape. Its second fermentation happens in a large pressurised tank, which is quicker and cheaper and keeps the wine's fresh, floral fruit: pear, green apple and white flowers. The bubbles are softer and frothier.

Price follows from the method. Prosecco is usually much cheaper, which makes it ideal for parties and cocktails like the spritz.

Sweetness differs too. Most Champagne sold is Brut and tastes dry; much Prosecco is labelled Extra Dry, which is actually a little sweeter than Brut.

For quality Prosecco, look for Conegliano Valdobbiadene on the label.

Which to choose? Champagne for depth and occasion; Prosecco for everyday freshness.
