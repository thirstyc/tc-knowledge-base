## 1. How do tannins age?

- **source_doc:** `qa-how-tannins-age`

Over time, tannin molecules slowly link together into longer chains, which feel softer and smoother in the mouth. Some grow large enough to fall out of the wine as sediment.

Tannins come mainly from grape skins, seeds and stems, and from oak barrels. In a young red, they feel grippy and drying, like strong tea.

What happens as the wine ages:

- Polymerisation: small, astringent tannin molecules combine with each other and with colour pigments into larger ones, which taste less harsh.
- Sedimentation: the largest chains become insoluble and drop out, forming the deposit in old bottles.
- Colour change: as pigments bind with tannins and settle, reds shift from purple to ruby, then to brick and tawny.

The result is a smoother, rounder texture, and room for more complex aromas to show.

This happens slowly in the bottle, over years. Tiny amounts of oxygen through the cork play a part, which is why storage matters.

Not all tannic wines get better. The fruit, acidity and alcohol need to be in balance, or the fruit can fade before the tannins soften, leaving a dry, hollow wine.

Decanting gives young, tannic wines a gentler preview of this effect by exposing them to air.

## 2. What are wine notes/tasting notes?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-are-tasting-notes`

Tasting notes are written descriptions of how a wine looks, smells and tastes. They help people remember wines, compare them and decide what to buy.

A typical tasting note covers:

- Appearance: colour and intensity, like "pale lemon" or "deep ruby".
- Nose: the aromas, like "black cherry, violets and a hint of vanilla".
- Palate: sweetness, acidity, tannin, alcohol and body, plus the flavours.
- Finish: how long the flavour lasts after swallowing.
- Conclusion: quality, readiness to drink and ageing potential.

Aroma descriptions compare the wine to familiar things. Nobody adds blackcurrant to a red wine that smells of it; the same aromatic compounds occur naturally in the grape and the fruit. Descriptors fall into families:

- Primary: from the grape, like fruit, flowers and herbs.
- Secondary: from winemaking, like butter, bread and vanilla from oak.
- Tertiary: from ageing, like leather, mushroom, nuts and honey.

Professional notes can be flowery, but they're only useful if they help you. Your own notes can be simple: what you tasted, whether you liked it, and what you ate with it.

Keeping short notes, or even photos of labels with a word or two, is the fastest way to learn what you enjoy.

## 3. How to describe wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-how-to-describe-wine`

Start with structure, then flavours. Describing how a wine feels (sweet or dry, light or full, crisp or soft, smooth or grippy) is easier and often more useful than naming specific fruits.

A simple framework:

- Sweetness: dry, off-dry or sweet.
- Acidity: crisp and mouth-watering, or soft and round.
- Tannin, for reds: smooth, or drying and grippy like strong tea.
- Body: light like skim milk, medium, or full like cream.
- Alcohol: whether it feels warm in the throat.
- Finish: short, or long and lingering.

Then add flavour families, getting more specific if you can:

- Fruit: citrus, green, stone or tropical fruit in whites; red, black or dried fruit in reds.
- Floral and herbal: flowers, grass, mint, pepper.
- Oak and winemaking: vanilla, toast, butter, smoke.
- Earthy and aged: mushroom, leather, forest floor, honey, nuts.

Useful everyday words: "fruity", "savoury", "earthy", "crisp", "rich", "elegant", "juicy".

There are no wrong answers. If a wine reminds you of something, that's a valid description. Comparing two wines side by side makes differences much easier to describe.

When asking for recommendations, describing a wine you liked helps more than knowing its name.

## 4. How does altitude affect wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-altitude-wine`

Higher vineyards are cooler, especially at night, which keeps acidity fresh while grapes still ripen in strong sunshine. The result is often wine with bright acidity, intense aromas and deep colour.

Temperature drops by roughly 0.6°C for every 100 metres of altitude, so a high vineyard can have a much cooler climate than a nearby valley floor.

The main effects:

- Big day-night temperature swings: warm days build sugar and flavour, cool nights preserve acidity and aroma.
- Stronger sunlight, especially ultraviolet, which thickens grape skins, adding colour and tannin.
- A longer, slower ripening season, giving more complexity.
- Less disease pressure from humidity, in many places.

Famous high-altitude regions:

- Salta, in northern Argentina, with some of the world's highest vineyards, over 3,000 metres.
- Mendoza's Uco Valley, at 900–1,500 metres.
- Mount Etna in Sicily, with vineyards up to about 1,000 metres.
- Alto Adige in northern Italy.
- Parts of Spain's central plateau, like Ribera del Duero.

Altitude is also how warm countries make fresh wine. Planting higher is one of the main ways growers are responding to climate change.

The trade-offs are frost risk, shorter seasons and harder work on steep slopes.

## 5. How does aspect (sun exposure) affect wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-aspect-wine`

Aspect is the direction a vineyard slope faces, and it controls how much sun and warmth the vines receive. In cool regions, the right aspect can decide whether grapes ripen at all.

In the Northern Hemisphere:

- South-facing slopes get the most sun and warmth, ideal in cool climates.
- East-facing slopes get gentle morning sun and dry off dew early, reducing disease, while avoiding the hottest afternoon heat.
- West-facing slopes get the hot afternoon sun, useful in cool places but risky in warm ones.
- North-facing slopes are coolest, sometimes used in hot regions to keep freshness.

In the Southern Hemisphere, north-facing slopes are the warmest.

Slope steepness matters too. A steep slope facing the sun receives light more directly, drains cold air and water away, and warms faster.

Famous examples:

- Germany's Mosel, where the best Riesling grows on very steep, south-facing slate slopes above the river.
- Burgundy's Côte d'Or, where the top vineyards sit on east and southeast-facing slopes.
- The Northern Rhône's Côte-Rôtie, meaning "roasted slope".

In warmer regions, growers increasingly choose cooler aspects to preserve acidity and avoid overripe, high-alcohol wines.

Aspect is one reason neighbouring vineyards can make very different wines.

## 6. What temperature to serve Champagne?

- **source_doc:** `qa-serving-temp-champagne`

Around 8–10°C for most Champagne. Serve simpler non-vintage bottles a little colder, around 6–8°C, and serve vintage and prestige Champagne a little warmer, around 10–12°C, so their complexity can show.

Too cold, and the aromas and flavours are muted; you lose what you paid for. Too warm, and the bubbles become aggressive, the wine foams when poured, and it feels heavy.

How to get there:

- Fridge: about three hours in a standard fridge.
- Ice bucket: 20–30 minutes in a bucket of ice and water, which chills much faster than ice alone. Add a handful of salt to speed it up further.
- Freezer: only in an emergency, and no longer than 15 minutes. Set a timer; a forgotten bottle can freeze and burst.

Serving tips:

- Open it gently, holding the cork and twisting the bottle, so it sighs rather than pops.
- Pour slowly, tilting the glass, to keep the bubbles.
- Use a white wine glass or a tulip-shaped flute rather than a narrow flute or a wide coupe, to capture the aromas.

Between pours, keep the bottle in the ice bucket. It warms quickly on the table.

## 7. What temperature to serve rosé?

- **source_doc:** `qa-serving-temp-rose`

Around 8–12°C. Light, pale rosés taste best at the colder end, while fuller, darker rosés show more flavour a little warmer.

By style:

- Pale, delicate rosé, like Provence: 8–10°C.
- Fruity, medium-bodied rosé: 10–12°C.
- Fuller, more structured rosé, like Tavel or Bandol: 12–13°C.
- Sparkling rosé: 6–10°C.

Too cold, and even a good rosé tastes of nothing but cold. Too warm, and it feels flabby and alcoholic.

Practical tips:

- Two to three hours in the fridge is about right for most rosé.
- If it's straight from the fridge at 4–5°C, let it sit for 10 minutes before pouring.
- Twenty minutes in an ice bucket with water chills a room-temperature bottle quickly.
- Outdoors in summer, keep the bottle in an ice bucket between pours, but let glasses warm slightly in your hand.

Ice cubes in rosé are common in the south of France on hot days. They dilute the wine, but for a simple, refreshing rosé by the pool, nobody minds.

## 8. How long before wine is ready to drink after opening?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-breathing-time`

Most wine is ready to drink as soon as it's opened. Only young, tannic reds and some complex whites really benefit from time in the air, usually 30 minutes to a couple of hours in a decanter.

Simply pulling the cork does very little, since only the small surface in the neck touches the air. To let a wine breathe, pour it into a decanter or into glasses.

A rough guide:

- Everyday whites, rosés, sparkling and light reds: drink immediately.
- Medium-bodied reds: 15–30 minutes in a decanter or glass can help.
- Young, powerful reds like Barolo, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah or Bordeaux: one to two hours, sometimes more.
- Young, closed whites like top white Burgundy or Chenin Blanc: 20–30 minutes.
- Old, fragile wines: decant only to remove sediment, then drink soon, since they fade quickly.

The best test is to taste. Pour a little when you open it, then again every 20–30 minutes. When it tastes more open and fragrant, it's ready.

Swirling the wine in your glass gives a quick burst of air too.

If a wine smells faintly of sulfur or eggs when first opened, a few minutes of air, or vigorous swirling, usually clears it.

## 9. How long does wine last in a wine fridge?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-fridge-how-long`

Unopened wine stored properly in a wine fridge lasts as long as the wine itself is built to last, from a year or two for everyday bottles to decades for age-worthy wines. The fridge preserves it; it doesn't change how long the wine can age.

A wine fridge provides the conditions wine needs: steady temperature, darkness, low vibration, and often some humidity. Set a single-zone fridge to around 12–14°C for long-term storage.

How long different wines last unopened:

- Everyday whites, rosés and light reds: one to three years.
- Most everyday reds: two to five years.
- Quality whites like white Burgundy and Riesling: five to twenty years.
- Age-worthy reds like Bordeaux, Barolo and northern Rhône: ten to thirty years or more.

Opened wine is different. Recorked, it lasts about three to five days for most still wines, and one to three days for sparkling wine with a stopper. Keep opened bottles in a regular fridge, which is colder and slows oxidation more.

A wine fridge is not the same as a kitchen fridge. Kitchen fridges are too cold and dry for long-term storage, and their vibration and door opening disturb the wine.

## 10. Why orange wine is trendy?

- **source_doc:** `qa-orange-wine-trendy`

Because it's new to most drinkers but thousands of years old, it pairs brilliantly with food, and it grew up alongside the natural wine movement and a new generation of wine bars.

Orange wine is white wine made like red: the juice ferments with the grape skins, giving an amber colour, grippy texture and flavours of dried fruit, tea, nuts and spice.

Why it caught on:

- An ancient story: it began in Georgia, where amber wines have been fermented in clay qvevri for about 8,000 years. UNESCO recognised the qvevri method as intangible cultural heritage in 2013.
- A modern revival: winemakers in Friuli and Slovenia brought the style back from the late 1990s.
- The natural wine scene: many orange wines are made with minimal intervention, and natural wine bars made them a staple.
- Food versatility: their texture and tannin handle dishes that defeat many wines, like spiced food, fermented flavours, and Middle Eastern and Asian cooking.
- Something different: adventurous drinkers enjoy a style that doesn't fit the usual red, white or rosé.
- Social media: the colour is striking.

It's not a passing fad. Orange wine is now an established category, often called "skin-contact" or "amber" wine, made by serious producers around the world.
