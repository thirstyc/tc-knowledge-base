# Part 1 — Guides

## Cabernet Franc As Food Wine (`comparison-cabernet-franc-as-food-wine`)

### id 536 — Loire Franc > Expensive Bordeaux — Concept

Loire Franc > Expensive Bordeaux — Concept

The idea: put a Loire Cabernet Franc next to a famous, expensive Bordeaux and see which one you actually want with dinner. Our pairing is Bernard Baudry, a Chinon producer whose bottles usually sit around the $20–30 mark, against a Bordeaux first growth, where prices start in the hundreds and climb fast.

These wines are related. Cabernet Franc is one of Cabernet Sauvignon's parents, and it grows in Bordeaux too, especially on the Right Bank. But the Left Bank first growths are built mostly on Cabernet Sauvignon, made to be powerful, structured and patient. They are often at their best after a decade or two.

Chinon plays a different game. It's grown in the cooler Loire Valley, so the wine comes out lighter, fresher and more savoury: red fruit, a streak of graphite, sometimes a green, leafy edge that people either love or learn to love. It's ready much sooner and it doesn't need a special occasion.

The point isn't that one is better. It's that price tells you about scarcity, reputation and ageing potential, not about what will taste best with Tuesday's roast chicken.

### id 537 — Loire Franc > Expensive Bordeaux — Teaching

Loire Franc > Expensive Bordeaux — Teaching

Which is better for dinner? Most nights, the Loire bottle.

A young first growth is dense, tannic and built for the cellar. Opened too early, it can dominate a plate and make everything around it taste flat. Chinon has lower tannin, higher acidity and less oak, which is exactly what food wants. The acidity cuts through fat, the moderate tannin can handle a steak without drowning a piece of fish, and the herbal notes pick up anything with thyme, rosemary or grilled vegetables.

Try it with roast chicken, pork chops, mushroom dishes, charcuterie or goat's cheese. Serve it slightly cool, around 15–16°C, which makes the fruit taste brighter.

Save the Bordeaux for a night when it can be the main event, ideally after it has spent years in a cellar. Spend the difference on another good bottle.

## Pinot Noir Across Regions (`comparison-pinot-noir-across-regions`)

### id 538 — Burgundy vs New Zealand Pinot — Concept

Burgundy vs New Zealand Pinot — Concept

Same grape, two very different places. Pinot Noir is famously sensitive to where it grows, which makes it the best grape for learning what "terroir" actually tastes like.

Burgundy is the benchmark. The Côte d'Or's limestone and clay slopes, a continental climate and centuries of mapping individual vineyards produce wines that lean savoury: red cherry, earth, mushroom and a mineral, almost chalky finish. Fruit is part of the picture, not the whole of it.

New Zealand is younger and brighter. Regions like Central Otago, Marlborough and Martinborough have cool nights and lots of sunshine, so the grapes keep their acidity while the fruit gets vivid: dark cherry, plum, sometimes a spicy, herbal lift. Most bottles are made to be enjoyed young and are sealed under screwcap.

Neither is the "right" Pinot. Tasting them side by side shows how much climate and soil can reshape one grape.

### id 539 — Burgundy vs New Zealand Pinot — Teaching

Burgundy vs New Zealand Pinot — Teaching

Pour both, then taste in this order: colour, smell, then the finish.

The New Zealand wine will usually be a shade darker and smell more openly of fruit. The Burgundy is often paler and quieter at first, with more earth and spice showing as it opens up. On the palate, look for where the wine lands: New Zealand tends to finish on juicy fruit, Burgundy on acidity and a slightly dusty, savoury grip.

Neither is objectively better. Ask yourself which one you'd want with dinner. The Burgundy loves roast chicken, mushrooms and duck. The New Zealand bottle handles salmon, lamb and anything with a bit of char.

Keep price in mind. Village-level Burgundy and good Central Otago often cost about the same, which makes this a fair fight.

## Sangiovese Old Vs New (`comparison-sangiovese-old-vs-new`)

### id 540 — Old World vs New World Sangiovese — Concept

Old World vs New World Sangiovese — Concept

Sangiovese is Tuscany's grape, the backbone of Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile. It's high in acidity, firm in tannin and tastes of sour cherry, dried herbs and a little tomato leaf.

This comparison is about style more than geography. Our pairing is two Montalcino producers, Altesino and Argiano, one representing a more traditional approach and one a more modern one.

Traditional Sangiovese is often aged in large, old oak casks that add little flavour, so the wine stays lean, savoury and tart, and needs time. Modern styles tend to use riper fruit and smaller, newer barrels, which add vanilla, spice and a rounder, more polished texture. Outside Italy, in California or Australia, Sangiovese usually leans further toward ripe fruit and softer acidity.

Both approaches can be excellent. The difference is what the winemaker wants you to notice first: the place or the fruit.

### id 541 — Old World vs New World Sangiovese — Teaching

Old World vs New World Sangiovese — Teaching

Same grape, different philosophy. Tasting them together makes the winemaker's choices obvious.

Start with smell. The traditional bottle should lead with cherry, leather, dried herbs and earth. The modern one will often add vanilla, sweet spice and darker, riper fruit.

Then feel the texture. Traditional Sangiovese can seem sharp and grippy on its own and completely transform with food. The modern style is usually smoother and easier to drink without a plate.

Now add food. Tomato sauce, pizza and pecorino are Sangiovese's natural partners because the wine's acidity matches the tomato's. Notice which bottle gets better with a bite and which one gets louder.

If you like what the traditional style is doing, look for Chianti Classico and Brunello from producers known for large-cask ageing. If you prefer the modern one, you have plenty of company.

## Tempranillo Evolution (`comparison-tempranillo-evolution`)

### id 542 — Oak-Heavy vs Modern Rioja — Concept

Oak-Heavy vs Modern Rioja — Concept

Rioja is the easiest place to taste what oak does to wine, because for more than a century it defined the region's style.

Traditional Rioja is Tempranillo, often blended with Garnacha and a little Graciano or Mazuelo, aged for long periods in American oak barrels. That oak gives the classic Rioja notes: vanilla, coconut, dill and sweet spice. The longer the ageing, the higher the label: Crianza, then Reserva, then Gran Reserva, each with more time in barrel and bottle before release. Our traditional example is Marqués de Riscal, one of the region's historic houses.

A newer generation uses oak with a lighter hand, often favouring French oak or larger vessels, and focuses more on vineyard and fruit. Our example is Palacios Remondo, from the warmer eastern part of Rioja, known for a fresher, fruit-forward style.

Same region, same grapes. The biggest variable is the barrel.

### id 543 — Oak-Heavy vs Modern Rioja — Teaching

Oak-Heavy vs Modern Rioja — Teaching

Oak is the variable. Here's how to hear it.

Smell the traditional bottle first. Vanilla, coconut, cedar and dill are oak talking. With age, the fruit turns from fresh red berries to dried fruit, leather and tobacco. It's mellow, savoury and often surprisingly light on its feet.

Now the modern one. The fruit is louder: red and black cherry, plum, sometimes a floral note. The oak is there but in the background, and the texture feels juicier.

Neither is a mistake. Many people fall for Rioja because of that old-school oak, and a good Gran Reserva is one of the best-value aged wines in the world.

Food helps settle it. Roast lamb and grilled chorizo love the traditional style. The fruit-forward style is easier with tapas, pizza or a burger.

## Food Pairing Masterclass (`guide-food-pairing-masterclass`)

### id 528 — Wine + Food Pairing — The real rule

Wine + Food Pairing — The real rule

Acidity and tannin in wine cut fat in food. If you remember one thing, make it that.

Fat coats your mouth. Acidity cleans it off and makes the next bite taste fresh, which is why Champagne works with fried chicken and Sauvignon Blanc works with a creamy goat's cheese. Tannin does something similar with protein and fat: a rich steak softens a tannic red, and the red makes the steak taste less heavy.

A few more rules worth knowing:

- Match weight. Delicate food wants a lighter wine; rich, heavy food can handle a bigger one.
- Sweet food makes dry wine taste sour. The wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert.
- Chile heat is amplified by alcohol and tannin. Reach for something off-dry, low in alcohol and fruity.
- Salty food loves acidity and a little sweetness.
- Umami, from mushrooms or aged cheese, can make tannic reds taste bitter. Softer reds cope better.

Everything else is preference.

### id 529 — Wine + Food Pairing — Why Cabernet Franc wins

Wine + Food Pairing — Why Cabernet Franc wins

Cabernet Franc is the most useful red at a mixed table, and the reason is balance.

It has just enough tannin to cut through cream and stand up to red meat, but not so much that it bullies a piece of fish. Its acidity is high enough to refresh the palate between bites. And its flavours, red fruit, pencil shavings and a green, herbal note, echo whatever is on the plate: roasted peppers, herbs, tomatoes, mushrooms.

That makes it a safe bet when one person orders salmon and another orders steak. A Loire Cabernet Franc from Chinon, Bourgueil or Saumur-Champigny, served slightly cool, can handle both.

It's also good with food that usually trips reds up. Goat's cheese, roast vegetables, charcuterie and even a creamy mushroom pasta all work.

If a restaurant list has one, it is rarely the wrong choice.

### id 530 — Wine + Food Pairing — Case studies

Wine + Food Pairing — Case studies

Three pairings that show the rules in action.

1. Sangiovese + tomato pasta = acidity matches acidity. Tomatoes are acidic, and a low-acid wine next to them tastes flat and flabby. Sangiovese has the acidity to keep up, and its herbal, cherry flavours sit right alongside the sauce.
2. Pinot Noir + salmon = silk + richness. Salmon is oily enough to handle a red, and Pinot's low tannin won't turn the fish metallic the way a big, tannic red can. Its acidity cuts the richness.
3. Beaujolais + light lunch = harmony. Gamay is light, juicy and low in tannin, so it fits salads, charcuterie, roast chicken and sandwiches without weighing anything down. Serve it cool.

The pattern: acidity for fat and acidic food, low tannin for fish and delicate dishes, and matching weight for everything else.

## How To Taste Wine (`guide-how-to-taste-wine`)

### id 531 — Wine Tasting Framework — Overview

Wine Tasting Framework — Overview

Tasting well isn't a talent. It's a routine. Four steps, in this order:

1. **Look** — color tells age/oxidation. Tilt the glass against something white. Whites darken toward gold and amber with age; reds fade from purple toward brick and orange at the rim.
2. **Smell** — primary (grape), secondary (fermentation), tertiary (age). Primary aromas come from the grape: fruit, flowers, herbs. Secondary aromas come from winemaking: butter, bread, toast, vanilla from oak. Tertiary aromas come from time: dried fruit, leather, mushroom, nuts.
3. **Taste** — attack, mid-palate, finish. Notice what hits first, how the flavour develops, and how long it lingers after you swallow.
4. **Structure** — acidity, tannins, alcohol, body. Acidity makes your mouth water. Tannin dries your gums. Alcohol warms the back of your throat. Body is how heavy the wine feels, like skim milk versus cream.

Then make a call: do you like it, and would you buy it again?

### id 532 — Wine Tasting Framework — Practice

Wine Tasting Framework — Practice

Taste the same wine three times. Once when you open it, once after it has sat in the glass for twenty minutes, and once with food. It will change each time, and noticing how it changes is the skill.

Conditions matter more than people think. Serve whites cool, not ice-cold, and reds slightly below room temperature; too cold hides aroma, too warm makes alcohol stand out. The glass and what you just ate or drank can also change what you perceive, so keep them consistent when you're comparing.

Say what you smell out loud, even if it sounds silly. "Strawberry jam" and "wet rocks" are real tasting notes.

The fastest way to learn is side by side. Open two bottles that differ in one way, same grape from two regions, or the same wine from two vintages, and the difference becomes obvious.

## Natural Wine Intro (`guide-natural-wine-intro`)

### id 533 — Natural Wine — Overview

Natural Wine — Overview

Minimal intervention: grapes grown without synthetic chemicals, fermented with the wild yeasts on their skins, and very little added or taken away in the cellar. That usually means no fining, no filtration, and little or no added sulfites.

There is no single legal definition that applies everywhere. France introduced a "Vin Méthode Nature" label, but most producers simply describe their own methods, so the term covers a wide range of wines.

What you might notice in the glass: cloudiness, a bit of fizz, flavours that are juicy and fresh, and sometimes funky, cidery or savoury notes you wouldn't find in conventional wine. Many natural wines are light, chillable reds, skin-contact "orange" wines or sparkling pét-nats.

Because there's less protection against oxygen and bacteria, bottles can vary more from one to the next. Store them cool and drink them fairly soon.

### id 534 — Natural Wine — Reality

Natural Wine — Reality

Natural wine is a spectrum. Some incredible, some flawed. Taste first, judge later.

At its best, natural wine tastes vivid and alive, with a sense of place that heavily managed wine can lose. At its worst, the "funk" is simply a fault: mousiness (a lingering, stale-cracker aftertaste), excessive volatile acidity that smells like nail-polish remover, or brettanomyces that turns every wine into a farmyard.

The label doesn't guarantee quality either way. Plenty of conventional producers farm organically and intervene very little without calling themselves natural, and some natural wines are brilliant only on the day they're opened.

A good approach: buy from a shop or bar that tastes what it sells, ask what a bottle is like before you commit, and don't be afraid to decide a wine isn't for you. That's not snobbery. It's taste.

### id 535 — Natural Wine — Start here

Natural Wine — Start here

Start with regions where low-intervention winemaking has deep roots and the wines are usually clean and approachable.

- Beaujolais: Gamay made with minimal intervention has been a benchmark since Marcel Lapierre and his neighbours in Morgon championed it in the 1980s. Jean-Paul Brun is another producer to look for.
- Loire Valley: smaller producers making Chenin Blanc, Cabernet Franc and light reds like Gamay and Pineau d'Aunis.
- Languedoc: warm, generous reds and skin-contact whites, often at friendly prices.
- Jura: oxidative whites and pale, perfumed reds, the region natural wine fans treat as home.

Begin with a chilled light red or a pét-nat. They're forgiving, fun and a good test of whether the style is for you.

# Part 2 — Grape Sections

## Chardonnay (`grape-chardonnay`)

**section_title:** Chardonnay — What To Know

Chardonnay — What To Know

Chardonnay doesn't have a loud personality of its own, which is exactly why it's so interesting. It takes on the flavour of where it's grown and how it's made more than almost any other white.

Climate sets the fruit. Cool places like Chablis give green apple, lemon and a stony, mineral edge. Warmer places like California and much of Australia push it toward peach, pineapple and mango.

The cellar sets the style. Fermenting or ageing in oak adds vanilla, toast and a creamy texture. Malolactic conversion turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and can add that famous buttery note. Time on the lees adds nuttiness and weight. Skip all of that and you get a lean, crisp wine.

Burgundy is where Chardonnay is taken most seriously, from Chablis in the north to Meursault and Puligny-Montrachet in the Côte de Beaune. It's also a key grape in Champagne, where it makes Blanc de Blancs.

If you think you don't like Chardonnay, you probably don't like heavy oak. Try Chablis or an unoaked bottle before giving up on it.

## Sauvignon Blanc (`grape-sauvignon-blanc`)

**section_title:** Sauvignon Blanc — What To Know

Sauvignon Blanc — What To Know

Sauvignon Blanc is about aroma and acidity, and it's almost always made to drink young.

It has two classic personalities. In the Loire Valley, in Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, it's restrained: citrus, green apple, cut grass and a flinty, mineral edge. In New Zealand, especially Marlborough, it's louder: passion fruit, grapefruit, gooseberry and a sharp green-pepper note. That explosive style made Marlborough famous in the 1980s and has been copied ever since.

Those green, herbal notes come from compounds called pyrazines, which are stronger in cooler conditions. More sun and ripeness shift the wine toward tropical fruit.

Most Sauvignon Blanc is fermented in stainless steel to protect those aromas. In Bordeaux it's often blended with Sémillon and sometimes aged in oak, which gives a rounder, more textured wine. Robert Mondavi's oaked California version was sold as "Fumé Blanc", and the name stuck.

Pair it with goat's cheese, salads, asparagus, seafood and anything with fresh herbs. It's one of the few wines that handles vinaigrette.

## Merlot (`grape-merlot`)

**section_title:** Merlot — What To Know

Merlot — What To Know

Merlot is the most planted grape in Bordeaux, even though Cabernet Sauvignon gets more attention.

It ripens earlier than Cabernet and has thinner skins, which means softer tannins, a rounder feel and flavours of plum, black cherry and chocolate. That's why it's so often blended: Merlot fills in the middle, Cabernet adds the backbone.

On Bordeaux's Right Bank, in Pomerol and Saint-Émilion, Merlot is the lead grape, and the clay soils there suit it perfectly. Some of the world's most sought-after wines come from these vineyards, which says a lot about how serious "easy" Merlot can be.

Elsewhere, Merlot does well in Washington State, Napa Valley, Tuscany and Chile. Cooler sites keep it fresher and more herbal; warmer ones make it plush and jammy.

Its reputation took a hit in the 2000s, partly because of a lot of cheap, dull bottles. Good Merlot is still one of the most food-friendly reds around. Try it with roast pork, duck, mushroom dishes and pasta with meat sauce.

## Zinfandel (`grape-zinfandel`)

**section_title:** Zinfandel — What To Know

Zinfandel — What To Know

Zinfandel is California's signature red, but it isn't originally from California. DNA research showed it's the same grape as Italy's Primitivo, and both trace back to a Croatian variety called Crljenak Kaštelanski.

The grape ripens unevenly, so a single bunch can hold raisined berries next to green ones. That's partly why Zinfandel can reach high alcohol levels and taste of jammy raspberry, blackberry, black pepper and baking spice all at once.

Some of California's oldest vineyards are Zinfandel, with vines over a hundred years old in places like Sonoma's Dry Creek Valley, Lodi and Amador County. Old vines give smaller crops and more concentrated, complex wines, and "old vine" on a label is worth noticing.

Styles range widely, from lighter, peppery versions to rich, heady ones. And yes, "White Zinfandel" is the same grape, made as a sweet pink wine that outsold the red for years.

Drink it with barbecue, burgers, pizza and spicy sausages, but go easy on chile heat: the alcohol amplifies it.

## Pinot Grigio (`grape-pinot-grigio`)

**section_title:** Pinot Grigio — What To Know

Pinot Grigio — What To Know

Pinot Grigio and Pinot Gris are the same grape, a pink-skinned mutation of Pinot Noir. The name you see often tells you the style.

Pinot Grigio, the Italian name, usually means a light, crisp, dry white picked early to keep it fresh: lemon, green apple, pear and not much else. It became one of the best-selling whites in the world because it's easy, affordable and hard to dislike. The best Italian examples, from Friuli and Alto Adige, have more texture and a clean, almond-like finish.

Pinot Gris, the French name, usually means a richer style. In Alsace it's picked riper and can be full-bodied, spicy and sometimes off-dry, with notes of ripe pear, honey and smoke. Oregon makes excellent versions somewhere between the two.

Those pink skins also mean that with a little skin contact the wine turns coppery. That's "ramato", a traditional Friulian style worth trying.

Serve Pinot Grigio with light seafood, salads and antipasti. Save Alsace Pinot Gris for richer dishes like roast pork or mild curries.

## Viognier (`grape-viognier`)

**section_title:** Viognier — What To Know

Viognier — What To Know

Viognier nearly disappeared. By the 1960s only a few hectares were left, almost all in Condrieu in the northern Rhône. It has since spread around the world, but Condrieu is still the reference point.

It's an unusual white: full-bodied and fairly high in alcohol, with low acidity and a huge perfume of apricot, peach, honeysuckle and orange blossom. Picked too early it tastes of very little; picked too late it gets heavy and oily. Getting that balance right is the whole challenge.

Most Viognier is best young, while the aromas are at their peak.

It has a second job in the Rhône. In Côte-Rôtie, growers may co-ferment a small amount of Viognier with Syrah, which helps stabilise colour and adds a floral lift to the red.

Outside France, look for Viognier from California, Australia's Eden Valley and Virginia, where it has become a local favourite.

Because of its weight and perfume, it pairs well with dishes that have some richness and spice: roast chicken, pork with apricots, mild curries and Moroccan tagines.

## Shiraz (`grape-shiraz`)

**section_title:** Shiraz — What To Know

Shiraz — What To Know

Shiraz and Syrah are the same grape. The name usually signals the style: Syrah for the cooler, peppery Rhône approach, Shiraz for the riper, fuller Australian one.

Australia's Shiraz story runs deep. Vines were brought over in the 1830s, and some of the oldest producing Shiraz vines in the world grow in South Australia, particularly in the Barossa Valley, where phylloxera never arrived.

Barossa and McLaren Vale make the classic style: blackberry, plum, chocolate, sweet spice and a warm, generous body. Cooler regions like the Adelaide Hills, the Yarra Valley and parts of Victoria make leaner, more peppery wines that are closer to the Rhône.

Australian winemakers often blend Shiraz with Cabernet Sauvignon, and some add a splash of Viognier, following the Côte-Rôtie tradition.

Shiraz also led Australia's biggest icon wine, Penfolds Grange, first made in the 1950s.

Serve it with barbecue, lamb, braised beef and hard cheeses. A slight chill, around 16°C, keeps the big versions from feeling too heavy.

## Albariño (`grape-albarino`)

**section_title:** Albariño — What To Know

Albariño — What To Know

Albariño comes from Galicia in the far northwest of Spain, and Rías Baixas is its home. It's a green, rainy corner of the country facing the Atlantic, and the wine tastes like it: fresh, salty and bright.

Expect lemon, lime, peach, apricot and a saline edge, with high acidity and moderate alcohol. Many producers age the wine on its lees for a few months, which adds texture without dulling the freshness.

To handle the humidity, vines are often trained high on granite posts in pergolas, which keeps the grapes away from damp ground and lets air flow through.

Across the border in northern Portugal the same grape is called Alvarinho. It's a key variety in Vinho Verde, especially around Monção and Melgaço, where it makes fuller, more serious wines than typical Vinho Verde.

Albariño is built for seafood: oysters, grilled fish, octopus, shellfish and ceviche. It also works well with light, herb-driven dishes and sushi.

Most are best within a couple of years, although the best bottles can develop honeyed notes with some age.

## Grüner Veltliner (`grape-gruner-veltliner`)

**section_title:** Grüner Veltliner — What To Know

Grüner Veltliner — What To Know

Grüner Veltliner is Austria's most planted grape, grown mostly in Lower Austria along the Danube in regions like the Wachau, Kamptal, Kremstal and the Weinviertel.

Its signature is a peppery note, often described as white pepper, alongside green apple, citrus and fresh herbs. Acidity is lively and the texture can range from light and zippy to rich and full.

The style depends a lot on where and how it's grown. The Weinviertel makes a lot of the light, peppery style, perfect for everyday drinking. The Wachau has its own ripeness categories: Steinfeder for the lightest wines, Federspiel for the middle, and Smaragd for the ripest and fullest. A good Smaragd can age for a decade or more.

That versatility makes Grüner one of the most food-friendly whites around. It handles foods that are famously hard on wine, like asparagus and artichokes, and it's great with schnitzel, salads, sushi and Asian dishes with herbs and a little heat.

If you like Sauvignon Blanc but want something a bit more savoury, start here.

## Pinot Noir (`grape-pinot-noir`)

**section_title:** Pinot Noir — What To Know

Pinot Noir — What To Know

Pinot Noir is thin-skinned, early-ripening and notoriously fussy. It needs a cool climate to keep its freshness, but it rots easily in the damp and struggles in the heat. When it works, it makes some of the most elegant and expensive wines in the world.

Thin skins mean pale colour and soft tannins, with flavours of red cherry, raspberry and strawberry. With age, Pinot develops earthy, savoury notes: mushroom, forest floor and spice.

Burgundy is the reference, where growers have spent centuries mapping how small differences in soil and slope change the wine. Other great sources include Oregon's Willamette Valley, California's Sonoma Coast, New Zealand's Central Otago and Germany, where it's called Spätburgunder.

It's also one of the main grapes in Champagne, where it adds body and red-fruit depth to the blend.

Because it's light and acidic, Pinot Noir is one of the most flexible reds for food: salmon, duck, roast chicken, pork and mushrooms all work well.

Serve it slightly cool, around 14–16°C, in a wide glass.
