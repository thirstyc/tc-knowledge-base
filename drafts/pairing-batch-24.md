## 1. Wine with broccoli?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-broccoli`

A crisp, green-edged white: Sauvignon Blanc, Grüner Veltliner or Verdejo. Broccoli's bitter, sulfurous notes can make oaky or tannic wines taste harsh, so freshness is the goal.

Like asparagus and Brussels sprouts, broccoli belongs to a family of vegetables that clash with many wines. The trick is to echo its green flavour rather than fight it.

What works:

- Steamed or boiled broccoli: Sauvignon Blanc, Grüner Veltliner or a dry Riesling.
- Roasted or charred broccoli: the sweetness and char suit a fuller white like Chenin Blanc, or a light red like Pinot Noir or Gamay.
- Broccoli with cheese sauce: an unoaked or lightly oaked Chardonnay.
- Broccoli with garlic and chili, or in a stir-fry: off-dry Riesling or Grüner Veltliner.
- Broccoli pasta with anchovy and Parmesan: Vermentino or Verdicchio.

What to avoid: big, tannic reds and heavily oaked whites, which the vegetable's bitterness makes taste metallic.

Most of the time, the sauce and cooking method matter more than the broccoli itself. Butter, cheese, lemon or roasting all soften its edges and open up more options.

## 2. Wine with spinach?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-spinach`

A crisp, dry white with good acidity: Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Albariño or a light Chardonnay. Spinach has an iron, slightly bitter edge that can make tannic reds taste metallic.

Spinach rarely stands alone, so match the dish:

- Spinach salad with goat cheese or vinaigrette: Sauvignon Blanc or Sancerre.
- Creamed spinach: an oaked Chardonnay or white Burgundy, whose richness matches the cream.
- Spinach and ricotta pasta or cannelloni: Pinot Grigio, Vermentino or Verdicchio.
- Spanakopita, with feta and pastry: Assyrtiko or a crisp Greek white.
- Saag and spinach curries: off-dry Riesling or Gewürztraminer.
- Spinach with eggs, like eggs Florentine: sparkling wine.
- Sautéed spinach with garlic and lemon: Albariño or Grüner Veltliner.

If you want a red, choose something light and low in tannin, like Pinot Noir, Gamay or Barbera, especially when the spinach is served alongside meat.

Lemon, butter, cream and cheese all soften spinach's metallic note, so a well-seasoned dish is far easier to pair than plain wilted leaves.

## 3. Wine with ramen?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-ramen`

It depends on the broth. Rich, porky ramen suits a crisp sparkling wine or a light, chilled red; lighter broths suit dry Riesling or Grüner Veltliner.

Ramen is salty, savoury and full of umami, so the wine needs acidity and freshness rather than weight or tannin.

By broth style:

- Tonkotsu, the rich pork-bone broth: sparkling wine, whose bubbles and acidity cut through the fat, or a chilled Gamay.
- Shoyu, soy-based: dry Riesling, Grüner Veltliner or a light Pinot Noir.
- Shio, salt-based and lighter: Albariño, Muscadet or a dry sparkling wine.
- Miso: an oxidative style like dry Sherry, or an orange wine with some texture.
- Spicy ramen with chili oil: off-dry Riesling. A touch of sweetness calms the heat.

What to avoid: big, oaky or tannic reds. Salt and umami make tannin taste bitter, and high alcohol makes spice burn more.

Toppings matter too. Chashu pork leans toward a light red, seafood toward a crisp white, and a soft egg toward sparkling wine.

Sake is the classic partner, but a well-chosen wine works just as well.

## 4. Wine with butter sauce?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-butter-sauce`

A white with enough acidity to cut the richness, or enough body to match it: Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc, white Burgundy or Champagne.

Butter sauces are rich but delicate, so the wine shouldn't overwhelm them. There are two good approaches:

- Match the richness: a lightly oaked Chardonnay or white Burgundy echoes the buttery texture. This is the classic partner for lobster or scallops in butter.
- Cut through it: a high-acid white like Chablis, Chenin Blanc, Muscadet or sparkling wine refreshes the palate between bites.

By sauce:

- Beurre blanc, with shallots, vinegar and white wine: Muscadet, Sancerre or Chablis. It was created in the Loire, and Loire whites are the natural match.
- Hollandaise and béarnaise: Chardonnay, or Champagne with eggs Benedict. With steak béarnaise, a medium-bodied red like Pinot Noir or a Bordeaux works.
- Brown butter and sage, with pasta or gnocchi: Chardonnay, Pinot Gris or a light Pinot Noir.
- Lemon butter with fish: Chablis, Albariño or Sauvignon Blanc.

Avoid tannic reds, which clash with butter and fish, and very aromatic whites, which can smother a subtle sauce.

## 5. Wine with caramel?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-caramel`

A sweet wine with nutty, toffee or dried-fruit flavours: fortified wines like Tawny Port, sweet Madeira and Pedro Ximénez Sherry, or Sauternes and late-harvest wines. The wine must be at least as sweet as the dessert.

That's the key rule with any dessert. If the dessert is sweeter than the wine, the wine tastes thin, sour and bitter, so dry wines don't work with caramel.

Good matches:

- Tawny Port: its caramel, nut and dried-fruit notes echo the dessert perfectly.
- Sweet Madeira, like Bual or Malmsey: rich, tangy and nutty, with acidity that keeps it fresh.
- Sauternes or Barsac: honeyed and apricot-scented, ideal with crème brûlée and caramel custards.
- Pedro Ximénez Sherry: very sweet and raisiny, for intense sticky toffee pudding or caramel ice cream. Some people pour it straight over the ice cream.
- Tokaji Aszú: sweet but with bright acidity, lovely with caramelised apple or tarte Tatin.
- Vin Santo: nutty and dried-fruit sweet, good with caramel and nut tarts.

Salted caramel is especially good with Tawny Port or sweet Madeira, since salt and sweetness play off each other.

Serve dessert wines slightly chilled, and in small glasses. A little goes a long way.

## 6. Wine with nuts?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-nuts`

Dry Sherry, the fortified wine of southern Spain, is the classic match, especially Fino, Manzanilla or Amontillado. Its nutty, savoury flavours echo the nuts, and its freshness handles the salt.

Salted nuts are one of the most wine-friendly snacks, since salt makes wine taste smoother and fruitier.

By type:

- Salted almonds: Fino or Manzanilla Sherry, a Spanish classic, or a dry sparkling wine.
- Marcona almonds and Spanish tapas: Amontillado or a crisp Albariño.
- Roasted peanuts and mixed nuts: sparkling wine, a dry rosé or a juicy, low-tannin red.
- Walnuts: Tawny Port, especially with blue cheese and pears.
- Pecans and hazelnuts: an oaked Chardonnay or Amontillado, which share their toasted notes.
- Pistachios: a crisp white like Sauvignon Blanc or Vermentino.
- Spiced or honey-roasted nuts: a demi-sec Champagne or another off-dry sparkling wine.

For nut desserts, like pecan pie, walnut cake or praline, choose sweet wines with nutty flavours of their own: Tawny Port, sweet Madeira, Vin Santo or Oloroso Sherry.

Aged wines often develop nutty notes too, so nuts are a good snack to serve with older Champagne or mature Chardonnay.

## 7. Wine with pie?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-pie`

It depends on the filling. Sweet fruit pies want a dessert wine at least as sweet as the pie; savoury pies want a wine matched to the meat or vegetables inside.

Sweet pies:

- Apple pie: late-harvest Riesling, sweet Chenin Blanc from the Loire, or Tokaji. Their apple and honey flavours and bright acidity suit the fruit.
- Pecan pie: Tawny Port, sweet Madeira or Pedro Ximénez Sherry.
- Pumpkin pie: Tawny Port, Vin Santo or a late-harvest white with spice.
- Cherry or berry pie: Ruby Port, Brachetto d'Acqui or a sweet sparkling red.
- Lemon meringue: Moscato d'Asti or a sweet Riesling, with acidity to match the lemon.
- Chocolate cream pie: Ruby Port or Banyuls.

Savoury pies:

- Steak and ale or beef pie: a hearty red like Syrah, Malbec or a Rhône blend.
- Chicken pie in cream sauce: Chardonnay or a light Pinot Noir.
- Pork pie: Beaujolais or a dry Riesling.
- Fish pie: white Burgundy, Chablis or Chenin Blanc.
- Tourtière, the spiced meat pie: Pinot Noir, Gamay or a Côtes du Rhône.

Pastry adds richness, so a wine with good acidity keeps each bite fresh.

## 8. Wine with olive oil?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-olive-oil`

A crisp Mediterranean white, like Vermentino, Verdicchio, Assyrtiko or Albariño, or a light, savoury red from the same places the olive oil comes from.

Olive oil is rarely eaten alone, but a dish dressed generously with it shares its flavours with the wine. The fruitiness, bitterness and peppery bite of good extra virgin oil all matter.

Matching the oil's character:

- Delicate, buttery oils: softer whites like Pinot Grigio, Soave or Pinot Blanc.
- Grassy, green oils: Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino or Verdejo, which echo the herbal notes.
- Peppery, bitter oils, like early-harvest Tuscan oil: savoury reds with good acidity, like Chianti or other Sangiovese.

Regional pairings work beautifully: a Tuscan oil with Chianti, a Greek oil with Assyrtiko, a Spanish oil with Albariño or Rioja, a Provençal oil with dry rosé.

For bread dipped in olive oil, try a dry rosé or a crisp sparkling wine, which refresh the palate between bites.

Avoid heavily oaked whites and big, tannic reds, which can make fresh olive oil taste bitter and flat.

## 9. Wine with garlic?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-garlic`

It depends on how the garlic is cooked. Raw and sharp garlic suits crisp, herbal whites; roasted, mellow garlic suits fuller whites and medium-bodied reds.

Garlic changes dramatically with cooking, and so does the best wine.

- Raw garlic, in aioli, pesto or gazpacho: Sauvignon Blanc, Vermentino, Albariño or a dry Provence rosé.
- Sautéed garlic with shrimp or clams: Picpoul de Pinet, Albariño or Verdicchio.
- Roasted garlic, sweet and soft: oaked Chardonnay, Viognier or a light red like Pinot Noir.
- Garlic bread: a crisp white, Barbera or Chianti.
- Garlic-heavy meat dishes, like roast lamb with garlic and rosemary: Rioja, Côtes du Rhône, Syrah or Grenache.
- Chicken with forty cloves of garlic: white Burgundy or a Rhône white.
- Garlic with chili and soy, in stir-fries: off-dry Riesling or Grüner Veltliner.

Regional instincts are a good guide. The cuisines that use the most garlic, like Provence, Spain, southern Italy and Greece, all make wines that suit it: fresh, herbal whites, dry rosés and savoury reds.

Avoid very delicate or aged wines, whose subtle flavours strong garlic will overwhelm.

## 10. Wine with teriyaki?

- **source_doc:** `qa-pairing-teriyaki`

A fruity, low-tannin wine that can handle the sauce's sweetness and salt: off-dry Riesling, Pinot Noir, Gamay or a juicy Zinfandel.

Teriyaki glaze is sweet, salty and savoury, made from soy, sugar and mirin. That sweetness is the key challenge, since it makes dry, tannic wines taste thin and bitter.

By protein:

- Teriyaki chicken: off-dry Riesling, Gewürztraminer or a light Pinot Noir.
- Teriyaki salmon: Pinot Noir is the classic match. Its bright fruit and gentle tannin suit the rich fish and glaze. A dry rosé also works.
- Teriyaki beef: a fruit-forward red like Zinfandel, Grenache, Merlot or a ripe Shiraz.
- Teriyaki tofu or vegetables: off-dry Riesling or Chenin Blanc.

Why off-dry works: a touch of sweetness in the wine matches the glaze, and the high acidity keeps things fresh and balances the salt.

What to avoid: bone-dry, tannic reds like young Cabernet Sauvignon or Nebbiolo, which turn bitter against sugar and soy, and heavily oaked whites.

If the dish is grilled with smoky char, a slightly chilled fruity red is especially good.
