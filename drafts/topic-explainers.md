## enology-fermentation

### What fermentation does

Fermentation is how grape juice becomes wine. Yeast eats the natural sugars in the juice (glucose and fructose) and turns them into alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving off heat as it works.

Roughly 17 grams of sugar per litre become 1% alcohol, so riper, sweeter grapes make stronger wine. Fermentation stops when:

- the sugar runs out, leaving a dry wine;
- the alcohol reaches around 15–16%, which most yeast can't survive;
- or the winemaker stops it on purpose, by chilling, filtering or adding grape spirit, to keep some sweetness.

Yeast also makes small amounts of other compounds, like glycerol and fruity esters, which add texture and aroma. A lot of what we call a wine's "fruit" is built during fermentation.

### Wild or cultured yeast

Almost all wine is fermented by one species, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, the same family used for bread and beer. The choice is where it comes from.

- **Cultured yeast**: selected strains bought by the winemaker. They start quickly, finish reliably, and some are chosen to bring out particular aromas.
- **Wild or native yeast**: the yeasts already living on the grapes and in the cellar. Several species start the job before *Saccharomyces* takes over.

Wild fermentations can add complexity and a sense of place, but they are less predictable: they can stall before all the sugar is used, or produce off-aromas. Neither choice is better in itself; many excellent wines are made both ways.

### Temperature and time

Temperature is one of the winemaker's main controls, because fermentation makes its own heat.

- **Whites and rosés** are usually fermented cool, around 12–18°C, over two to four weeks. Cool fermentation keeps fresh, fruity and floral aromas.
- **Reds** are fermented warmer, around 20–30°C, usually for one to three weeks, on their skins. The heat helps extract colour, tannin and flavour.

Too hot and the yeast struggles and aromas cook off; too cold and fermentation can stop. That's why modern cellars use temperature-controlled tanks.

### The second fermentation

After the main fermentation, many wines go through **malolactic conversion**. This isn't done by yeast but by bacteria, which turn sharp malic acid (the acid of green apples) into softer lactic acid (the acid of milk).

- Almost all red wines go through it, which makes them rounder.
- Some whites do, especially Chardonnay, where it can add a buttery note.
- Crisp whites like Riesling and Sauvignon Blanc usually don't, to keep their freshness.

Sparkling wine has a different second fermentation: a second *alcoholic* fermentation in a sealed bottle or tank, which traps the carbon dioxide as bubbles.

## enology-sulphites

### What sulphites are and why they're used

Sulphites are compounds of sulphur dioxide (SO₂), and they are the most widely used preservative in wine. They do two jobs:

- **Antioxidant**: they protect the wine's colour and fresh aromas from oxygen.
- **Antimicrobial**: they stop spoilage yeasts and bacteria that would turn wine vinegary or smelly.

Winemakers have used sulphur for centuries, originally by burning it inside barrels to clean them.

Yeast also makes a little sulphur dioxide on its own during fermentation, usually under 10 milligrams per litre. So even a wine with nothing added contains some sulphites.

### How much is in wine

Legal limits are much higher than what most wines contain.

- **European Union**: up to 150 mg/L for dry reds and 200 mg/L for dry whites and rosés, with higher limits for sweet wines.
- **United States**: up to 350 mg/L.
- **Labels**: in the EU, the US and Canada, wine with more than 10 mg/L must say "contains sulphites".

Some general patterns:

- Reds usually contain less than whites, because their tannins also protect them.
- Sweet wines contain the most, because sugar needs extra protection.
- Organic and biodynamic rules set lower limits, and natural wines often have little or none added.

For comparison, dried fruit like apricots often contains ten times more sulphites than a glass of wine.

### Do sulphites cause headaches?

Probably not, for most people. True sulphite sensitivity is real but uncommon, affecting roughly 1% of people, more often some people with asthma. When it happens, the usual symptoms are breathing problems like wheezing, not headaches.

The strongest clue: red wine has fewer sulphites than white wine, yet red wine is the one most often blamed for headaches. More likely causes include:

- the alcohol itself, and dehydration;
- histamine, which is higher in some red wines;
- other grape compounds from the skins, which researchers are still studying.

If you think sulphites affect you, a doctor or allergist can help confirm it.

### "No sulphites added" and "sulphite-free"

No wine is completely free of sulphites, because yeast makes a small amount during fermentation. "No sulphites added" means the winemaker didn't add any, not that there are none.

These wines can be excellent, but they are more fragile:

- Store them somewhere cool and dark.
- Drink them fairly young.
- Expect more variation from bottle to bottle.

A wine with a small, careful addition of sulphites isn't a lesser wine. The amounts used in good winemaking are low, and they are what lets most wine travel and age safely.

## enology-biodynamic

### What biodynamic farming involves

Biodynamic farming starts with organic rules (no synthetic pesticides, herbicides or fertilisers) and adds its own practices. It comes from a series of lectures on agriculture given by the Austrian philosopher Rudolf Steiner in 1924.

The main ideas:

- **The farm as one living system**: growers aim to produce their own compost, keep animals and encourage biodiversity rather than buying inputs.
- **Preparations**: nine specific mixtures, made from manure, silica and plants such as yarrow, chamomile, nettle and valerian, used in tiny doses on the soil, the vines or the compost. The best known is cow manure buried in a cow horn over winter, then stirred into water and sprayed.
- **A calendar**: work such as planting, pruning and harvesting is timed to the phases of the moon and the positions of the planets.

### What the evidence says

The honest answer is mixed.

- Studies comparing biodynamic and organic vineyards generally find similar results for soil health, vine health and grape quality.
- There is little scientific evidence that the preparations or the calendar have the specific effects claimed for them.
- A 2017 study in New Zealand found that the calendar's "fruit days" and "root days" made no difference to how experts rated the same wines.

What most people agree on: biodynamic growers watch their vineyards very closely, work by hand and build living, diverse soils. That attention is good farming, and it often shows in the grapes, whatever you think of the cosmology.

### Certification and labels

"Biodynamic" is a protected term only when a wine is certified. The main certifiers are:

- **Demeter**, the international biodynamic organisation, founded in 1928;
- **Biodyvin**, a French growers' association.

Their rules cover the cellar too, not just the vineyard: fewer permitted additives, and lower sulphite limits than conventional or even organic wine.

Many respected producers farm biodynamically without certifying, because it's expensive and bureaucratic, so the absence of a logo doesn't always mean much. Ask your shop.

### What it means for you

A biodynamic label tells you a producer farms without synthetic chemicals and with unusual care. It doesn't guarantee a better-tasting wine, and it isn't a health claim.

Biodynamic wines are often more expensive, mainly because the work is slow and done by hand.

The best approach is the same as with organic wine: treat the label as useful information about how the grapes were grown, then judge the wine by how it tastes. Many famous estates in Burgundy, the Loire, Alsace, the Rhône and Champagne farm this way, so good examples are easy to find.
