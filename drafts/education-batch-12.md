## 1. How to taste wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-how-to-taste-wine`

Look, smell, taste, then decide. Those four steps are all a professional tasting note really is, and they take about a minute.

Look: tilt the glass against something white. Colour hints at age. Whites deepen toward gold with time; reds fade from purple to brick at the rim.

Smell: swirl, then take a short sniff. Try to name two or three things. Fruit first (citrus, cherry, blackberry), then anything else (herbs, vanilla, smoke, earth). Wine aromas are grouped as primary, from the grape; secondary, from fermentation and oak; and tertiary, from age.

Taste: take a decent sip and let it move around your mouth. Notice what hits first, what develops, and how long the flavour lasts after you swallow. A long finish is usually a good sign.

Structure: this is the part that improves quickly with practice. Acidity makes you salivate. Tannin dries your gums. Alcohol warms your throat. Body is how heavy the wine feels.

Decide: do you like it, and would you buy it again? That's the only part that really matters.

Tasting two wines side by side teaches you more than any single bottle can.

## 2. How to read a wine label?

- **source_doc:** `qa-read-wine-label`

Find four things: who made it, where it's from, what year, and how strong it is. Everything else is detail.

New World labels usually name the grape, which makes them easy. Old World labels usually name the place, and you're expected to know which grape grows there: Chablis is Chardonnay, Sancerre is Sauvignon Blanc, Chianti is Sangiovese.

The place also tells you how tightly the wine is regulated. France uses AOC or AOP, Italy DOC and DOCG, Spain DO and DOCa, and each has broader categories, like IGP or IGT, with looser rules. Tighter rules mean more specific origin, not automatically better wine.

The vintage is the year the grapes were picked. Non-vintage, common in Champagne, means a blend of years.

Alcohol by volume is a useful style clue: 12% suggests something light, 15% something rich and warm.

Other terms worth knowing: Reserva and Riserva mean extra ageing in Spain and Italy; Trocken means dry in German; Brut means dry in sparkling wine; "old vines" has no legal definition in most places.

Importer names on the back are also a good tip: find one you like and follow them.

## 3. What do wine ratings mean?

- **source_doc:** `qa-wine-ratings`

Most wine ratings use a 100-point scale, where the wine effectively starts at 50 and the useful range runs from about 85 to 100. They're one person's opinion of one bottle on one day.

A rough reading of the scale: 85–89 is good, 90–93 very good, 94–97 outstanding, and 98–100 exceptional. Critics and publications differ, and some Europeans use a 20-point scale instead.

Useful to know: scores are usually given at a young stage of a wine's life, often in a large tasting of similar wines, which tends to favour bold, concentrated styles over subtle ones. Two respected critics can rate the same wine several points apart.

Scores also move prices. A high score from an influential critic can make a wine hard to find and expensive overnight.

How to use them: treat a score as a filter, not a verdict. Find a critic or shop whose palate matches yours and follow their recommendations, rather than chasing numbers from anyone.

Notice also what's being rated. Awards from competitions vary in rigour, and shelf-talkers sometimes quote scores for a different vintage of the same wine.

Your own opinion is the one that decides whether you buy it again.

## 4. What does "dry" mean in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-dry-wine`

Dry means the wine has very little sugar left in it. During fermentation, yeast converts grape sugar into alcohol; if it finishes the job, the wine is dry.

That's all it means. Dry is not the opposite of fruity, and it isn't about how the wine makes your mouth feel.

The most common confusion is exactly that: people taste a ripe, fruity wine and call it sweet. A New Zealand Sauvignon Blanc bursting with passion fruit or a jammy Australian Shiraz can be bone-dry. Aromas suggest sweetness that isn't there.

Tannin causes the opposite confusion. It dries your gums, which people sometimes describe as a "dry" taste, but that's texture, not sugar.

The scale runs dry, off-dry (a little sweetness, often around 10–30 grams of sugar per litre), medium, then sweet.

Label clues: "Trocken" is German for dry, "Brut" means dry sparkling wine, and "Sec" means dry in French, except on Champagne, where it's actually a little sweet.

If you want reliably dry wine, try Muscadet, Chablis, Sancerre, Provence rosé, most Italian reds and Brut Champagne.

## 5. What does "tannins" mean?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-are-tannins`

Tannins are natural compounds from grape skins, seeds and stems, and from oak barrels. They're what makes your mouth feel dry and slightly rough after a sip of a big red.

You know the sensation from over-steeped black tea or walnut skins: not a flavour so much as a texture, a grip on your gums and the sides of your tongue.

Red wines have them because the juice ferments with the skins. White wines mostly don't, because the juice is separated from the skins first. Orange wines, made like reds from white grapes, do have tannin.

Winemakers control how much ends up in the wine through how long the juice sits on the skins, how gently the grapes are pressed, and what kind of barrel is used.

Tannins matter for two reasons. They give a wine structure, and they help it age: over years, they link together and fall out as sediment, which is why old reds taste softer and throw a deposit.

They also explain a classic pairing rule. Fat and protein soften tannin, which is why steak and Cabernet work so well together.

Very tannic grapes include Nebbiolo, Tannat and Cabernet Sauvignon; gentle ones include Pinot Noir, Gamay and Grenache.

## 6. What is acidity in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-acidity`

Acidity is what makes your mouth water and a wine taste fresh rather than flabby. It comes mainly from tartaric and malic acid in the grapes.

Grapes lose acidity as they ripen and gain sugar, so cool climates and early picking give higher acidity, while hot climates and late picking give less.

You feel it rather than smell it: a tingle along the sides of your tongue and a rush of saliva. Lemon juice is the extreme version.

Acidity does several jobs. It balances sweetness, which is why great sweet wines don't taste cloying. It makes wine refreshing with food, cutting through fat and salt. And it acts as a preservative, which is why high-acid wines like Riesling age so well.

Winemakers can adjust it. Malolactic conversion turns sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid, giving that rounder, creamier feel in many Chardonnays and most reds. In warm regions, acid can be added; in cool ones, it's carefully preserved.

High-acid wines to try: Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Chablis, Champagne, Sangiovese, Nebbiolo, Assyrtiko.

Low acidity isn't a fault on its own, but a wine without enough of it tastes heavy and dull.

## 7. What is body in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-is-body`

Body is how heavy or light a wine feels in your mouth. The usual comparison is skim milk, whole milk and cream: light, medium and full-bodied.

It isn't about flavour intensity or colour. It's weight and texture.

Alcohol is the biggest factor, since alcohol is viscous: a 15% wine feels fuller than an 11% one. Sugar adds weight too, as do extract from the grape skins, oak ageing and time on the lees.

Climate does much of the work, because warm places produce riper grapes with more sugar, and therefore more alcohol.

Rough guides: light-bodied reds include Pinot Noir, Gamay and Frappato; medium-bodied ones include Sangiovese, Merlot and Grenache; full-bodied ones include Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz, Malbec and Zinfandel.

For whites, Pinot Grigio, Muscadet and Riesling are light; Sauvignon Blanc and unoaked Chardonnay are medium; oaked Chardonnay, Viognier and Marsanne are full.

Body matters mainly for matching wine to food: light dishes want light wines, rich dishes can take fuller ones.

A quick check: if the wine coats your mouth and lingers heavily, it's full-bodied. If it feels like water with flavour, it's light.

## 8. What is alcohol content in wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-alcohol-content`

Most wine is between 11% and 15% alcohol by volume, and the number is printed on every label.

Alcohol comes from sugar. Yeast eats the sugar in grape juice and produces alcohol, so riper grapes with more sugar make stronger wine. Warm climates and late picking push the number up; cool climates and early picking keep it down.

Typical ranges:

- Light whites like Moscato d'Asti and German Kabinett Riesling: 5–10%.
- Most whites and lighter reds: 11–13%.
- Fuller reds like Cabernet, Shiraz and Zinfandel: 13.5–15.5%.
- Fortified wines like Port and Sherry: 15–22%.

A standard 750 ml bottle at 13% contains roughly ten units of alcohol by many national measures, though definitions vary by country.

Alcohol affects taste, not just strength. It adds body and a sense of sweetness, and at high levels it can taste hot at the back of your throat. It also makes spicy food burn more, which is why off-dry, low-alcohol wines suit chile heat.

Average alcohol levels have risen over the past few decades, from riper picking and warmer growing seasons, though a number of producers are deliberately going the other way.

## 9. What are wine faults?

- **source_doc:** `qa-what-are-wine-faults`

A wine fault is something that's gone wrong and makes the wine taste unpleasant. The common ones are cork taint, oxidation, excessive volatile acidity, reduction and brettanomyces.

Cork taint, caused by a compound called TCA, makes wine smell of damp cardboard, wet dog or a musty cellar, and strips out its fruit. It affects a small percentage of cork-sealed bottles and has nothing to do with bits of cork floating in your glass.

Oxidation happens when too much air gets in. Whites turn deep gold and taste of bruised apple and sherry; reds go brown and dull. An open bottle left for days shows you exactly what it tastes like.

Volatile acidity, in excess, smells of vinegar or nail-polish remover. Small amounts add lift and are normal.

Reduction is the opposite of oxidation, from too little oxygen, giving struck match, rubber or rotten-egg aromas. A mild version often blows off after some air or a vigorous swirl.

Brettanomyces, a yeast, gives farmyard, band-aid or smoky-bacon notes. In small amounts some drinkers like it; in large amounts it dominates.

Not faults: sediment in old reds, tartrate crystals, and the petrol note in mature Riesling.

If a wine seems faulty in a restaurant or shop, say so. A good one will replace it.

## 10. What is "corked" wine?

- **source_doc:** `qa-corked-wine`

Corked wine is wine tainted by TCA, a compound that can form in natural cork and makes the wine smell musty. It has nothing to do with crumbled cork in your glass.

TCA stands for trichloroanisole. Humans are extraordinarily sensitive to it, detecting it at just a few parts per trillion.

What it smells like: wet cardboard, a damp basement, mouldy newspaper or a wet dog. In mild cases there's no bad smell at all; the wine just tastes muted and short, with the fruit stripped away.

How to check: smell the wine, not the cork. A wet, stained cork is normal; a musty smell in the glass is not. Leaving the glass a few minutes often makes it more obvious.

It's not harmful to drink, just unpleasant, and it can't be fixed. It's also no reflection on the producer, since the taint comes from the cork itself.

Estimates of how often it happens vary, but roughly one bottle in a hundred is a common figure today, down from far worse rates a few decades ago thanks to better cork production and alternatives.

Screwcaps and glass stoppers avoid the problem entirely. If a bottle is corked, take it back: shops and restaurants will normally replace it.
