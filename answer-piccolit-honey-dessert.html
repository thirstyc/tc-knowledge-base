Why does Piccolit taste so honey-sweet?

Short Answer

Picolit's sweetness comes from a genetic quirk as much as winemaking. The vine suffers from floral abortion—a large share of its flowers fail to set fruit—so what little fruit remains gets an outsized share of the vine's sugar and nutrients, ripening to very high concentration. Growers in Friuli's Colli Orientali often let the grapes hang late or dry slightly on the vine or on straw mats, concentrating sugars further before a slow, cool fermentation stops well short of dry. The result is a wine with intense honey, dried apricot and acacia-flower notes, balanced by acidity that keeps it from feeling cloying. It's not botrytized in the Sauternes sense—the richness is mostly about scarcity and concentration, not noble rot. That same low yield is why bottles are small and prices aren't shy. Serve it cool, around 10-12°C, in a small glass, and don't rush it: a little goes a long way.
