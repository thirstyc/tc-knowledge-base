Why is Petite Sirah so powerful?

Short Answer

Blame the skins. Petite Sirah, really the grape Durif, produces small berries with thick skins, and since tannin, color, and a lot of flavor compounds live in the skin rather than the juice, small berries mean a much higher skin-to-juice ratio than most red grapes. Ferment and macerate that, and you extract a huge amount of tannin and pigment relative to the volume of wine, which is why the color is often nearly opaque and the tannins can feel almost chewy. It's typically grown in warm regions like California and Israel, and warmth pushes sugar and phenolic ripeness further, adding alcohol and body on top of the tannin. Winemakers sometimes extend maceration too, which pulls out even more structure. The result is a wine built to age or to sit next to fatty food, on its own young it can feel clenched and monolithic. Give it a few years in bottle, or pour it with something rich enough to meet it halfway.
