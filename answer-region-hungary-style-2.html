Why does Tokaji Furmint taste so electric?

Short Answer

The electricity comes from two things stacked on top of each other. First, the soil: Tokaj sits on volcanic rock and clay, fast-draining, which stresses the vines just enough to concentrate flavor without bloating the grape with water. Second, the site: the confluence of two rivers throws up autumn fog most mornings, ideal humidity for botrytis cinerea, noble rot, to take hold on the Furmint grapes. Botrytis punches microscopic holes in the skins, water evaporates, and what's left is intensely concentrated sugar, but Furmint's naturally high acidity concentrates right alongside it, so the sweetness never turns flabby. That's the whole mechanism behind the classic Aszú style: enormous sweetness held upright by equally enormous acid. It's what let Tokaji dominate European courts for centuries before anyone had heard of a Bordeaux château, and it's a tension modern winemakers are now chasing in bone-dry Furmint too, minus the botrytis, keeping just the volcanic edge and the acid.
