Why does Campania wine taste so ancient and volcanic?

Short Answer

That volcanic character is literal, not a marketing word. Vines on Vesuvius and in the hills around Avellino grow in soils full of ash, pumice and mineral-rich volcanic rock, which drains aggressively and forces roots deep to find water — that stress is part of what gives the wines their intensity. Some of these vines are also ungrafted, planted on their own roots, because the sandy volcanic soil is inhospitable to the phylloxera louse that devastated most of the world's vineyards in the 1800s. That means a handful of vines here are genuinely centuries old, still producing on original rootstock. Aglianico, the main red, ripens late and holds onto serious tannin and acid, so young bottles can be gripping and need food or time to soften. Fiano and Greco, the whites, come off ashy soil with a smoky, nutty, almost saline edge. Serve the whites around 10-12°C and give Aglianico a decade if you can — it rewards patience more than most Italian reds outside Piedmont.
