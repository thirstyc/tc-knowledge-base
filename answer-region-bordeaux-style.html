Why does Bordeaux taste so structured and savory?

Short Answer

The savoriness comes from soil and grape working together rather than fighting. Gravel soils, deep beds left behind by ancient rivers, drain fast and reflect the sun's heat back up into the vine canopy, which is exactly what thick-skinned Cabernet Sauvignon needs to ripen fully this far north. Those thick skins are also where the tannin and dark, savory character come from. Clay and limestone patches, cooler and more water-retentive, favor Merlot instead, which ripens earlier and adds flesh and a softer, plummier fruit. The maritime climate, moderated by the Atlantic and the Gironde estuary, keeps things from getting too hot or too cold, but also brings enough rain and humidity that vintages vary more than people assume. None of this is built for immediate pleasure: young Bordeaux can taste closed, even a bit stern, because the tannin and acid are there to protect the wine over a decade or more in the cellar. Give a serious bottle eight to ten years, or at minimum a long decant, before deciding what it's actually saying.
