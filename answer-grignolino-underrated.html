Why does Grignolino fly under the radar?

Short Answer

Piedmont built its international reputation on Nebbiolo, which gives you Barolo and Barbaresco, wines that age for decades and command serious prices, and on Barbera, which is reliable, food-friendly, and easy to grow in volume. Grignolino does neither of those things well. It has low pigment, so it never gets the deep color buyers associate with a serious red. Its tannin is minimal, so it doesn't age past a few years. Yields are also finicky and lower than growers want, since the grape is prone to uneven ripening and needs careful site selection to avoid diluted, thin results. None of that translates into marketing appeal or cellar-worthy prestige, so producers in Asti and Monferrato mostly keep it as a local, early-drinking wine rather than pushing it abroad. It's a case of a grape that's genuinely interesting, pale, perfumed, high-acid, being overshadowed simply because it doesn't fit the template of what a serious red is supposed to look or age like.
