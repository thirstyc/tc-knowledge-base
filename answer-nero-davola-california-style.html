What does Nero d'Avola from California actually taste like compared to the Sicilian stuff?

Short Answer

Expect darker fruit and softer edges. Sicilian Nero d'Avola typically shows sour cherry, dried herbs and a saline, almost smoky finish, especially from volcanic sites near Etna. California versions, grown in warmer, sunnier conditions without the sea breezes that temper Sicily's coast, usually swing riper: think blackberry, plum, sometimes a raisiny edge if the grower pushed for maximum ripeness. Acidity is naturally moderate to high in this grape regardless of where it's grown, since Nero d'Avola holds onto malic acid well even in heat, which is exactly why it doesn't taste flabby despite the fruit intensity. Tannins are medium, silky rather than gripping. What you lose in translation is usually the mineral, briny quality Sicilian examples get from volcanic ash and limestone soils; California soils rarely replicate that. So you get a plusher, more crowd-pleasing wine, less about earth and salt, more about ripe fruit and warmth. Neither is wrong, they're just different translations of the same grape.

What should I actually serve with California Nero d'Avola, and at what temperature?

Serve it at 16 to 17 degrees, slightly cooler than room temperature, because the ripe fruit character can taste jammy and flat if it's too warm.
