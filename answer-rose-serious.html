Why is dry rosé finally taken seriously?

Short Answer

For decades, pink wine in North America meant White Zinfandel: a sweet, low-acid, mass-produced style born partly by accident when a winemaker's stuck fermentation left residual sugar in what was meant to be a dry wine. It sold by the tanker load and cemented rosé's reputation as sugary and unserious. Provence quietly built the opposite case: pale, bone-dry rosés with real acidity and structure, made with the same care as serious whites - controlled skin contact, cool fermentation, sometimes barrel aging - designed to earn a place on the list rather than sit as a warm-weather afterthought. As those wines gained a following outside France, sommeliers and critics started treating good rosé as its own legitimate category instead of a beginner's wine. The category still carries baggage - plenty of people assume pink means sweet - but the best producers, in Provence and increasingly elsewhere, have proven dry, structured, food-friendly rosé is entirely possible. Judge it the way you'd judge a serious white: by acidity, balance and length, not by color.

What pairs with rosé?

Rosé works at the table because it splits the difference between white and red without committing fully to either: it has white wine's acidity and chill-friendly freshness, but often enough fruit and texture to stand up to slightly richer food than a delicate white could handle.
