Why does Grappa taste so harsh and powerful?

Short Answer

Grappa isn't wine, it's pomace brandy: distilled from the skins, seeds and stems left over after pressing grapes for wine, not from the wine itself. That leftover mass, called vinaccia, still holds aromatic oils and a bit of residual sugar, and distillers steam-distill it, often in small batch copper stills, to pull out alcohol that typically lands around 40 to 50 percent. Unlike Cognac or aged whisky, most grappa sees no wood at all, or only brief contact, so there's nothing softening the edges, no vanilla, no time to round out fusel alcohols. That rawness is the point: it's meant to be a jolt, traditionally knocked back after a heavy meal to cut grease and aid digestion, a habit called far la resentin, rinsing the glass with a last coffee-laced pour. Serve it cold, around 8 to 10 degrees, or at room temperature if it's an aged riserva version, which does mellow with oak time. Don't expect subtlety from a young one; that's not a flaw, it's the job description.

What pairs with Grappa?

Grappa is traditionally a digestif, meant to be drunk after the meal is over, not alongside it — the idea, at least in Italian tradition, is that the alcohol and bitterness help settle a heavy meal, a practice called 'ammazzacaffè' when it's dropped straight into leftover espresso.
