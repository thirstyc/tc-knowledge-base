Why does Caladoc taste so Languedoc-dark?

Short Answer

Caladoc gets its darkness from its parentage, not from where it's grown, though the Languedoc's climate certainly helps. It's a deliberate crossing of Grenache and Malbec, bred by French researchers in 1958 to solve a specific problem: Grenache gives lovely warm fruit but pale color and unreliable yields, since its flowers can fail to set fruit in poor weather. Malbec contributed the deep pigment and firmer tannic backbone that Grenache lacks. Grown under the Mediterranean sun, with long, hot, dry summers, the grape accumulates plenty of sugar and color compounds before harvest, which is why the finished wine looks nearly black in the glass and tastes of ripe blackberry, plum, and dried herbs rather than red-fruited and bright. It's usually a supporting player in blends rather than a solo act, but where it leads, expect fullness and weight over finesse. Pour it a little cooler than room temperature, around 16-17°C, so the ripe alcohol doesn't overwhelm the fruit.
