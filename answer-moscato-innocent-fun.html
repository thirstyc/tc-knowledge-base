Why does Moscato (low-alcohol-sweet) taste so innocent?

Short Answer

Moscato's innocence is a fermentation trick. Winemakers chill the fermenting must and filter out the yeast before it finishes converting sugar to alcohol, so a big chunk of the grape's natural sweetness survives while alcohol stays down around 5-7%. That's the opposite of a dry wine, where fermentation runs until virtually all the sugar is gone. The grape itself, Muscat Bianco, is one of the most naturally aromatic varieties on earth, loaded with terpenes that read as orange blossom, peach and fresh table grape rather than anything oaked or tannic. Moscato d'Asti, the best-known style from Piedmont, is also only lightly fizzy because it's fermented once in a sealed tank rather than given a second fermentation like Champagne. Low alcohol, real sweetness, gentle bubbles and pure grape aromatics together read as harmless and a little goofy, but it's a deliberate, fairly delicate winemaking style, not an accident. Serve it cold, around 6-8C, and drink it young since it isn't built to age.
