What does Washington State Gewürztraminer actually taste like, and why is it different from the Alsace version?

Short Answer

Washington sits on basically the same latitude band as some cooler European regions but gets far more sun exposure and diurnal swing, especially in the Columbia Valley, where hot days and cold nights let the grape ripen sugar while holding onto acid. The result is a wine that's more lifted and fruit-forward than a dense Alsace Gewürztraminer, with lychee, rose petal, and stone fruit up front, but it rarely has the same oily texture or that petrol-tinged weight you get from old-vine Alsace fruit. Washington versions tend to be picked a bit earlier, so they lean floral and zesty rather than waxy and rich. Some producers leave a touch of residual sugar to balance the grape's naturally low acidity, which is the opposite problem you'd expect from a cool region. Don't assume sweet means low quality here, it's a style choice, not a flaw. Serve it cold, around 8-10°C, cold enough to keep the perfume from turning blowsy but not so cold you mute the spice notes that make the grape interesting in the first place.
