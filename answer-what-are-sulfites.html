What is sulfites in wine?

Topic

Sulphites

Difficulty

Beginner

Short Answer

Sulfites are sulfur dioxide and related compounds used in winemaking as a preservative. They stop wine from oxidising and protect it from unwanted bacteria and yeast.

Every wine contains some, because fermentation produces sulfites naturally, usually around 10 milligrams per litre. Winemakers then add more, at the crush, during ageing or at bottling.

How much varies by style. Reds generally need less, because their tannins also protect the wine. Whites need more, and sweet wines the most, since residual sugar is an invitation to microbes.

Legal limits differ by country and category, and organic and biodynamic certifications set stricter ones. Wines labelled "no added sulfites" still contain the small amount fermentation creates.

Labels in many countries must say "contains sulfites" above a low threshold, which is why the phrase appears on almost every bottle, including ones with very little.

For context, dried fruit typically contains far more sulfites per gram than wine does.

Low-sulfite wines can be wonderful, but they're more fragile: store them cool and drink them younger.
