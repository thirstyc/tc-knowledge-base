Why does Torrontés taste so floral and fruity?

Short Answer

Torrontés is a Muscat cross, and Muscat's signature is linalool and geraniol, aromatic terpenes that read as orange blossom, jasmine and lychee before you even taste the wine. What amplifies that in Argentina is altitude: regions like Cafayate in Salta sit at 1,700 metres or higher, where intense UV light and huge day-to-night temperature swings help the grape retain aromatic intensity and a decent backbone of acidity even as sugars ripen fully in the daytime heat. The stone fruit character, think peach and apricot, comes from that same ripening window. Unlike a lot of aromatic whites, Torrontés usually isn't sweet, it just smells like it should be, which trips people up on the first sip. Drink it young and cold, around 8-10°C, because those volatile aromatics fade fast with age. This isn't a wine built for the cellar, it's built for a hot afternoon and something spicy on the plate.
