What is natural wine certification?

Short Answer

There's no global certification for natural wine, which is the main reason the category causes arguments. A few national schemes exist, but most producers simply describe their own practices.

The best-known scheme is France's "Vin Méthode Nature", introduced in 2020. It requires certified organic grapes, hand harvesting, wild yeasts, no additives and no heavy processing, with strict limits on sulfur. Wines that qualify carry a logo, with different versions for no added sulfites and for small amounts.

Other countries have producer associations with their own charters, but these are voluntary and vary in rigour.

Where certification does exist is in farming. Organic and biodynamic certifications are regulated, audited and mean something specific, but they cover the vineyard, not the cellar. A wine can be certified organic and still be heavily manipulated in the winery.

What this means in practice: for natural wine, the trustworthy signals are the producer, the importer and the shop, not a logo. People who taste what they sell are a better filter than any label.

If a bottle's methods matter to you, ask. Most natural producers are happy to explain exactly what they did.

What is minimal wine?

"Minimal intervention" describes winemaking that adds and removes as little as possible: no additives beyond a little sulfur, native yeasts, no fining or filtering, and nothing to correct the wine's acidity, colour or alcohol.
