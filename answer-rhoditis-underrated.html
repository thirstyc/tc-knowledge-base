Why does Rhoditis fly under the radar outside Greece?

Short Answer

It's less about quality and more about marketing and infrastructure. Assyrtiko had Santorini's volcanic soil story and a wave of ambitious producers bottling it as a serious single-varietal wine for export. Vermentino rides on Italy and southern France's established DOC systems and international name recognition. Rhoditis never got that push, it's historically been a workhorse grape, grown widely across Greece mostly to blend into everyday wine or to prop up retsina's acidity, not to headline a label. Producers rarely bottled it alone with any ambition until fairly recently, so there was no flagship wine or region telling its story abroad. Add to that Greece's broader challenge: dozens of indigenous grapes with unfamiliar names competing for shelf space against grapes wine drinkers already know how to pronounce and buy. Rhoditis simply got lost in that shuffle, not because it can't make a lovely, food-friendly white, but because nobody built it a reputation to travel on.

What pairs with Rhoditis?

Rhoditis works the way most high-acid, low-tannin whites do: the acidity cuts through fat and salt instead of fighting them, which is exactly what grilled octopus, sardines, or fried whitebait need.
