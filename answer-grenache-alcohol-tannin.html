Why Grenache Gets You Tipsy Without Grabbing Your Gums

Grenache is the grape that pours soft and drinks strong. Fourteen, fifteen, sometimes sixteen percent, and almost no grip on the finish.

Short Answer

Sugar sits in the pulp, tannin sits in the skins. Grenache ripens fat and sweet with skins thin enough to keep the finish soft. High alcohol, low grip, no contradiction.

Sugar And Tannin Live In Different Rooms

Sugar sits in the pulp. Tannin sits in the skins, seeds and stems. Those are two separate parts of the grape, which means a grape can be loaded with one and short on the other.

Grenache is exactly that. It ripens easily in heat, so the pulp piles up sugar, but the skins are thin — nothing like Cabernet Sauvignon or Nebbiolo. Thin skins, less tannin to give.

What Fermentation Does With That

Yeast eats sugar and makes alcohol. More sugar in, more alcohol out. Tannin is a different job entirely: it gets pulled out of the skins by time, heat and contact with the juice. You can leave Grenache on its skins for weeks and still only get a modest amount, because there wasn’t much there to begin with.

The result is a wine that feels round and almost velvety but warms your chest. Grenache was never built for power. It’s built for charm, and for opening tonight.

Rhône Versus Spain

Cooler Rhône sites — the northern edge of the Côtes du Rhône, parts of Gigondas — don’t fully ripen it. You get twelve or thirteen percent, and slightly more tannin, because winemakers there lean on longer maceration to find structure.

Hot Spanish and Australian sites go the other way: alcohol climbs, tannin stays quiet. So when someone tells you they don’t like Grenache, the useful question is where theirs came from.

If you want soft and generous, buy warm-climate. If you want nerve, buy high altitude or old vines.
