Why does Australian Shiraz taste so ripe and peppery?

Short Answer

Here's the twist: the peppery note doesn't come from warmth, it comes from the opposite. That black pepper character is a compound called rotundone, and it's actually more prevalent in cooler-climate Shiraz, like that from the Grampians or Great Western in Victoria, where grapes ripen slowly and retain the aromatic precursors that produce it. In warm regions like the Barossa, extra heat and sugar ripeness tend to burn off rotundone before harvest, which is why Barossa Shiraz leans more toward jammy blackberry and plum than pepper. What warm-climate Australian Shiraz does reliably deliver is high alcohol, since more sun means more sugar accumulation in the grapes, which ferments into more ethanol, often north of 14.5%. That alcohol reads as warmth and body on the palate and can taste almost sweet even in a technically dry wine. Add in generous use of American oak, which contributes vanilla, coconut and baking-spice aromatics, and you get a wine that seems spicy and ripe simultaneously, even though the actual pepper and the actual ripeness are pulling from different ends of the climate spectrum.

What pairs with Australian Shiraz?

Australian Shiraz, especially the Barossa style, is built around ripe dark fruit, soft-ish tannins from warm-climate phenolic ripeness, and often a good hit of American oak giving vanilla and coconut notes.
