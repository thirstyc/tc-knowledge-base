Why does Chilean Sauvignon Blanc taste so fresh and citrusy?

Short Answer

Credit the Humboldt Current. Cold water runs up the Chilean coast and pushes fog inland through gaps in the Coastal Range, especially into Casablanca and the Leyda and San Antonio valleys. That fog blankets the vines most mornings and burns off by midday, so you get real sun for ripening but nights and mornings cold enough to keep malic acid intact in the grapes. The result is a big diurnal swing, sometimes 20 degrees or more between day and night, which is the actual mechanism behind that snappy, citrusy profile people love. It's not stylistic choice so much as the vines never getting a chance to go soft. Compare it to warmer inland regions like parts of the Central Valley and you'll taste riper, rounder Sauvignon Blanc with less zip. Serve the coastal stuff cold, around 45-50°F, to keep that acid line crisp rather than let it turn flabby as it warms in the glass.

Why is Chilean Carménère a national treasure?

Carménère is actually an old Bordeaux grape, one of the original six blending varieties in Médoc before phylloxera tore through Europe in the 1860s and growers replanted with hardier vines, mostly Merlot.
