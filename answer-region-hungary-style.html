Why does Tokaji taste so honeyed yet balanced?

Short Answer

Noble rot, botrytis cinerea, is the mechanism. It's a fungus that, under the right damp-then-dry conditions, punctures the skin of Furmint grapes and lets water evaporate while leaving sugar, acid, and flavor compounds behind, shriveling the fruit into something closer to a raisin than a grape. That's where the honeyed, dried-apricot, orange-peel character comes from, and why sugar levels in true Aszú can get extreme. What keeps it from tasting like candy is that Furmint is a naturally high-acid grape to begin with, and the same concentration process that spikes the sugar spikes the acid too, so the two rise together instead of one overwhelming the other. Volcanic soil underneath adds a mineral, almost saline edge that keeps the whole thing feeling taut rather than heavy. Tokaj was one of the earliest wine regions anywhere to get a formal quality classification, back in the 1700s, precisely because this balance was recognized as rare and worth protecting even then.
