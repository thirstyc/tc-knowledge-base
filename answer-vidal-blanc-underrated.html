Why does Vidal Blanc fly under the radar?

Short Answer

Vidal Blanc's problem is typecasting. It's thick-skinned and cold-hardy enough to survive hanging on the vine into a hard freeze without rotting, which is exactly why it became Canada's default icewine grape — picked frozen, pressed for tiny amounts of intensely sweet, syrupy juice. That association is so strong that most drinkers only ever meet Vidal Blanc in dessert form, expensive and sticky, and never register the dry, still version as the same grape at all. Which is a shame, because dry Vidal is a different wine entirely: crisp, apple- and citrus-driven, sometimes with a faint grapefruit bitterness, closer in spirit to a straightforward Alsace Pinot Blanc than anything dessert-like. It's widely and affordably planted across Ontario, Quebec and other cold-climate regions, and does an honest job as an everyday white without pretending to be more. The issue is marketing, not quality — the name on the label reads as icewine, so the crisp, cheap, table-ready version sits ignored on the shelf. Worth a second look.
