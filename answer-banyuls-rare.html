Why is Banyuls hard to find outside France?

Short Answer

Banyuls comes from a narrow strip of coastline near the Spanish border, where Grenache grows on schist terraces too steep and tight for machinery — everything is hand-harvested, which caps volume before the wine even reaches the cellar. Fortified sweet wine is also a shrinking global category; demand isn't what it was a century ago, so there's little incentive for small producers to scale up or chase export markets aggressively. Add a strong domestic market — the French drink a healthy share of what's made close to home — and not much is left heading overseas. It's less deliberate scarcity than a small, labour-intensive style from a small appellation serving a market that's mostly local and no longer especially large. Fortification and extended oxidative aging also tie up cellar space and capital for years before a bottle is even ready to sell, which discourages the kind of scale that makes export easy. If you find one outside France, credit an importer who went looking.
