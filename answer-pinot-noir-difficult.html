Why is Pinot Noir so hard to grow?

Short Answer

Pinot Noir is thin-skinned, early-budding and prone to disease, and it only tastes like itself in a narrow band of climates. Get any of that wrong and the wine is thin, jammy or simply dull.

The thin skins are the root of most problems. They offer little protection against rain, so the grapes split and rot easily, and tight bunches trap moisture, which makes matters worse.

It buds early, so spring frost is a constant threat, and it ripens early, so a hot spell can push it past elegance into stewed fruit.

Climate is unforgiving. Too cool and the wine tastes green and weedy; too warm and it loses its perfume and acidity. The band in between is narrow, which is why the great examples come from a handful of regions: Burgundy, Oregon, the Sonoma Coast, Central Otago, Germany and Tasmania.

Pinot Noir also mutates readily, so growers choose between many clones, each behaving differently.

None of this is forgiving in the cellar either. With so little tannin and colour to hide behind, every decision shows in the glass.

When it works, that's exactly the appeal: a wine that tastes unmistakably of where it grew.
