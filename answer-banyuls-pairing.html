What pairs with Banyuls?

Short Answer

Dark chocolate is the textbook match, and for once the textbook is right — Banyuls' dried-fruit, rancio richness lines up almost exactly with cocoa's bitterness, so instead of competing they extend each other. Beyond chocolate, look for concentrated dark fruit and spice: fig tart, spiced cake, anything leaning on cinnamon or clove. Less obviously, it holds its own against blue cheese, where the sweetness balances the salt and funk the way a good vintage port would with Stilton. What it doesn't want is a delicate dessert — anything light gets flattened by the alcohol and residual sugar, which sit around 16-17% and noticeably sweet respectively. Serve it cool, 14-16°C, never at room temperature, which keeps the sweetness from turning syrupy and the rancio notes from going flabby. Pour smaller glasses than you would for a table wine too; Banyuls is dense enough that a full pour usually outlasts the food, and you end up drinking dessert alone.

Why does Banyuls taste so dark and sweet?

Banyuls is Grenache grown on steep schist terraces above the Roussillon coast, fortified the same way port is: neutral grape spirit is added mid-fermentation, which kills the yeast before all the sugar converts.
