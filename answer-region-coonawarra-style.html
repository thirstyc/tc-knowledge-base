Why does Coonawarra Cabernet taste so distinctive?

Short Answer

The signature comes from a soil profile working in tandem with the local climate. Terra rossa — red clay rich in iron oxide — sits over limestone bedrock that drains well but holds a water table close enough to keep vines from ever fully drying out, so the fruit ripens slowly and evenly rather than racing to sugar ripeness. That slow ripening preserves acidity and lets tannins develop real structure instead of just bulk. The minty, eucalyptus note that shows up in almost every glass isn't a metaphor — it's cineole, a volatile compound from the eucalyptus trees that border many vineyards, which drifts onto the grape skins and gets absorbed. Combine that with a genuinely cool growing season and you get Cabernet that's firm, savoury, and herbal rather than jammy. Serve it around 16-18°C. It's a rare instance where one narrow strip of dirt and one particular tree species combine to produce a wine style nobody else in the world quite replicates.
