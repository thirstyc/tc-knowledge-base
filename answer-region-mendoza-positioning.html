Why is Mendoza Malbec everywhere right now?

Short Answer

It's a supply-chain story as much as a taste story. Mendoza sits at the foot of the Andes, and vineyards climb up to 1,000-1,500 metres in places like the Uco Valley. That altitude means intense sun but also near-zero humidity, so there's almost no fungal disease pressure — growers don't need to spray constantly or drop fruit to fight rot, which keeps costs down. Water comes from Andean snowmelt via drip irrigation, so yields are dialed in precisely rather than left to weather's mood. Land and labour are also cheaper than in Bordeaux or Napa. Put that together and you get ripe, generous fruit at a price nobody in Europe can match. Malbec itself helps: it's soft-tanninned and fruit-forward, nothing bitter or tannic to argue with, so it doesn't need bottle age or a lecture to enjoy. It's the rare wine that's genuinely good and genuinely cheap, which is why it took over restaurant lists and grocery shelves alike.

What pairs with Mendoza Malbec?

Mendoza's high-altitude vineyards, many over 900 metres in the shadow of the Andes, give Malbec intense sun exposure during the day and cold nights, which builds thick skins, deep color and ripe, substantial tannin without losing acidity.
