Why does Rías Baixas matter for Spanish wine?

Short Answer

For a long time, Spain's international reputation was built almost entirely on big oaky reds from Rioja and Ribera del Duero, so a crisp, low-alcohol, high-acid white from the rainy northwest corner didn't fit the story anyone was telling. Galicia sits above Portugal, right on the Atlantic, and gets far more rain and far less sun than the image of Spain suggests. That climate forced growers toward a completely different kind of winemaking, one built around freshness and restraint rather than power. Albariño turned out to be the grape that proved Spain could do precision as well as muscle, and it opened the door for people to take Spanish whites seriously at all. If you already gravitate toward Muscadet from the Loire or Vinho Verde just across the border in Portugal, Rías Baixas sits comfortably in that same coastal, seafood-driven lane, just with a bit more weight and ripeness on the mid-palate. It's less austere than either, which makes it an easy entry point.
