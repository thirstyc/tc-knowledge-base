How Wine Works

Biodynamic Wine

Organic farming taken further, following a lunar and cosmic calendar and using specific natural preparations instead of synthetic sprays. Whether or not you buy the cosmology, the vineyard discipline it demands often produces genuinely better fruit.

Founded By
Rudolf Steiner, 1920s
Core Idea
The farm as one self-sustaining organism
Certifying Body
Demeter
Common In
Loire Valley, Alsace, Jura, Priorat

What biodynamic farming involves

Biodynamic farming starts with organic rules (no synthetic pesticides, herbicides or fertilisers) and adds its own practices. It comes from a series of lectures on agriculture given by the Austrian philosopher Rudolf Steiner in 1924.

The main ideas:

  • The farm as one living system: growers aim to produce their own compost, keep animals and encourage biodiversity rather than buying inputs.
  • Preparations: nine specific mixtures, made from manure, silica and plants such as yarrow, chamomile, nettle and valerian, used in tiny doses on the soil, the vines or the compost. The best known is cow manure buried in a cow horn over winter, then stirred into water and sprayed.
  • A calendar: work such as planting, pruning and harvesting is timed to the phases of the moon and the positions of the planets.

What the evidence says

The honest answer is mixed.

  • Studies comparing biodynamic and organic vineyards generally find similar results for soil health, vine health and grape quality.
  • There is little scientific evidence that the preparations or the calendar have the specific effects claimed for them.
  • A 2017 study in New Zealand found that the calendar's "fruit days" and "root days" made no difference to how experts rated the same wines.

What most people agree on: biodynamic growers watch their vineyards very closely, work by hand and build living, diverse soils. That attention is good farming, and it often shows in the grapes, whatever you think of the cosmology.

Certification and labels

"Biodynamic" is a protected term only when a wine is certified. The main certifiers are:

  • Demeter, the international biodynamic organisation, founded in 1928;
  • Biodyvin, a French growers' association.

Their rules cover the cellar too, not just the vineyard: fewer permitted additives, and lower sulphite limits than conventional or even organic wine.

Many respected producers farm biodynamically without certifying, because it's expensive and bureaucratic, so the absence of a logo doesn't always mean much. Ask your shop.

What it means for you

A biodynamic label tells you a producer farms without synthetic chemicals and with unusual care. It doesn't guarantee a better-tasting wine, and it isn't a health claim.

Biodynamic wines are often more expensive, mainly because the work is slow and done by hand.

The best approach is the same as with organic wine: treat the label as useful information about how the grapes were grown, then judge the wine by how it tastes. Many famous estates in Burgundy, the Loire, Alsace, the Rhône and Champagne farm this way, so good examples are easy to find.
