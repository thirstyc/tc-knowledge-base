Why does Chilean Cabernet taste so structured and mineral?

Short Answer

Chilean Cabernet's structure comes mostly from where it's grown, not some magic dirt. Maipo Valley sits close enough to the Andes that meltwater-fed rivers and cool nighttime air rolling down the mountains create a big swing between day and night temperatures. That swing matters: grapes build sugar during hot days but hold onto acidity overnight, because acid degrades fast in heat and cool nights slow that decline. The result is fruit that reaches full ripeness in tannin, color and flavor without losing the backbone that makes a wine taste alive rather than flabby.

The "mineral" note people describe isn't literally tasting rocks - it's shorthand for that bright acidity plus a lack of jammy overripeness, sometimes with a saline or graphite-like edge from the alluvial, rocky soils along the Maipo River. Pair that acid with thick-skinned grapes, toughened by strong UV at altitude, and you get tannin plus freshness together - the "structure" people keep mentioning. Serve it around 60-65°F, not fridge-cold, or you'll mute the fruit and exaggerate the tannin.
