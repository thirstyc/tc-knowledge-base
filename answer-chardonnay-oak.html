Why is Chardonnay so polarizing?

Short Answer

It's polarizing because two winemaking choices completely change what's in the glass. Malolactic fermentation, a bacterial process that converts sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid, is what gives Chardonnay that buttery, creamy quality, and diacetyl is the compound responsible. Oak aging, especially in new barrels with the wine stirred on its lees (batonnage), adds vanilla, toast, and a rounder texture. Old-world Chardonnay, think Burgundy, often uses oak too, but limestone soils and a cooler climate keep acidity high enough to balance it, so the wine stays taut rather than heavy. New-world versions, especially from warmer regions, tend to ripen grapes further, lean harder into malo and oak, and end up broad and buttery with less acid to hold it together. Neither approach is wrong, they're just different tools. Serve unoaked styles at 10-12°C, oaked ones closer to 12-14°C so the aromatics actually open up instead of staying locked down by the cold.

Can Chardonnay age?

Yes, the best Chardonnay can age for ten to twenty years or more, but most is made to drink within a few years of release.
