What pairs with Grappa?

Short Answer

Grappa is traditionally a digestif, meant to be drunk after the meal is over, not alongside it — the idea, at least in Italian tradition, is that the alcohol and bitterness help settle a heavy meal, a practice called 'ammazzacaffè' when it's dropped straight into leftover espresso. It's rarely paired with food in the way wine is; instead it punctuates the end of dinner, often alongside or immediately after a strong coffee, when the sharpness and heat feel more like a reset than an assault. The ritual matters here as much as the flavor: small glass, small pour, sipped slowly rather than shot back despite what movies suggest. Young grappa is best served cold, around 8-10°C, which tempers the alcohol bite; aged (invecchiata) versions are smoother and better at slightly warmer, room-adjacent temperatures where their wood notes can show. It's not a wine-table pairing exercise — it's a full stop at the end of the meal.

Why is young Grappa so harsh?

Grappa is distilled from pomace — the skins, seeds, and stems left over after winemaking — and unlike brandy made from wine itself, it skips the aging that would normally round out the fusel alcohols and volatile compounds produced during fermentation and distillation.
