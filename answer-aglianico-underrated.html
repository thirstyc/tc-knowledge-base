Why is Aglianico called the "Barolo of the south"?

Short Answer

The nickname is really about structure, not geography. Aglianico ripens late and stays thick-skinned even in the heat of Campania and Basilicata, which means it packs in serious tannin and acidity while everything else in southern Italy goes soft and jammy. That combination, tannic grip plus real acid backbone, is what people associate with Nebbiolo and Barolo: wines built to soften and gain complexity over a decade or two rather than drink young. Taurasi, grown on volcanic soils around Avellino, is the benchmark version, often aged for years before release because it needs the time to unwind. Young Aglianico can taste closed and almost aggressive, all iron and dark fruit and clenched tannin; give it a decanter or five-plus years in bottle and it opens into tar, dried cherry, leather and smoke. It's an ancient grape, likely brought south by the Greeks, and it's stayed under the radar mainly because southern Italy has never marketed itself the way Piedmont has. The wine's there. The reputation is still catching up.

What pairs with Aglianico?
