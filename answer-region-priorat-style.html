Why does Priorat taste so dark and mineral?

Short Answer

Because almost everything about the place makes vines struggle, and struggling vines make intense wine.

Priorat is a small, rugged corner of Catalonia, an hour or two inland from Tarragona. The vineyards sit on steep terraces carved into hillsides of llicorella, a crumbly dark slate that glitters with mica. It holds almost no water and very little in the way of nutrients, so vine roots push deep through the cracks looking for both. The result is tiny yields of small, thick-skinned grapes with a lot of flavour packed into very little juice.

The grapes themselves help. Old Garnacha and Cariñena vines, some of them close to a century old, give dark fruit, warmth and structure. Many wines also include Cabernet Sauvignon or Syrah. Summers are hot and dry, so ripeness is never the issue; the challenge is keeping freshness.

That's where the "mineral" comes in. Nobody is tasting rock, but Priorat often has a savoury, stony, slightly graphite edge that sits under the ripe fruit and keeps it from feeling jammy. Wine people reach for "mineral" because nothing else quite describes it.

Priorat was nearly forgotten until a small group of winemakers revived it in the late 1980s. Today it is one of only two Spanish regions with the top DOCa/DOQ classification, alongside Rioja.
