Why is Chardonnay so versatile?

Short Answer

Chardonnay has a mild flavour of its own, so it takes on the character of where it grows and how it's made. That blank-canvas quality is exactly why it appears in so many styles.

In the vineyard, it adapts to almost any climate. Cool Chablis gives green apple and lemon; warm California and Australia give peach, pineapple and mango.

In the cellar, the winemaker's choices define it. Oak adds vanilla and toast. Malolactic conversion softens sharp acidity and can add a buttery note. Time on the lees brings nuttiness and texture. Skip all three and you get something lean and crisp.

It's also easy to grow: reliable yields, early ripening, and tolerant of different soils.

That's why it's used for still wine, for sparkling wine as one of Champagne's three main grapes, and for wines that range from everyday to the most expensive whites in the world.

The downside is that heavily oaked, overblown versions in the 1980s and 1990s gave the grape a bad name, which lingers.

If you think you dislike Chardonnay, try an unoaked one. You may just dislike oak.
