Why is Muscadet so dry and mineral?

Short Answer

Muscadet is made from Melon de Bourgogne, grown at the western edge of the Loire Valley near Nantes, close enough to the Atlantic that cool, damp conditions keep acidity high and sugars modest, so alcohol rarely climbs above 12%. The grape itself is fairly neutral, which is actually the point: it doesn't throw big aromatics, so it lets the site talk. Vines grown on granite, gneiss and schist soils around the Sèvre-et-Maine area pick up a saline, stony edge that shows on the palate more than the nose. The other big factor is élevage sur lie: the wine rests over winter on its spent yeast lees, sometimes bottled straight off them without racking, which adds a fine textural weight and a faint savoriness that balances the sharp acidity. That combination of high acid, low alcohol and lees-derived texture is why it reads as dry and mineral rather than fruity. Serve it cold, around 8-10°C, and it's the classic partner for oysters and shellfish because that acidity does exactly what a squeeze of lemon does.
