Why does Chablis taste so mineral and steely?

Short Answer

Chablis sits on Kimmeridgian limestone, a soil packed with fossilized oyster shells from an ancient seabed, and while wine geeks argue endlessly about whether vines can literally taste soil, there's no doubt this terroir produces a distinctive flinty, saline character that shows up glass after glass. More concretely important is the climate: Chablis is the coolest, northernmost pocket of Burgundy, which means grapes ripen slowly and retain sharp natural acidity rather than converting it all to sugar. Winemakers reinforce that starkness by skipping new oak almost entirely, fermenting in stainless steel or neutral vessels so nothing softens or rounds out the wine. No butter, no vanilla, no toast, just naked Chardonnay shaped by cold nights and chalky soil. The acidity itself isn't decorative; it's what makes the wine snap against briny, fatty foods like oysters or a good goat cheese, cutting through richness instead of getting lost in it. Serve it properly cold, around 10-12°C, because that mineral edge disappears fast the moment the wine warms up in the glass.

Is Chablis Chardonnay?

Yes, Chablis is 100% Chardonnay, but the name refers to the place, not the grape, which is exactly why people get confused when it tastes nothing like the buttery, vanilla-laced Chardonnay they know from warmer regions.
