Why does Petite Sirah get dismissed so easily?

Short Answer

Blame the name and the blending bin. Petite Sirah is actually Durif, a Syrah x Peloursin cross bred in France in the 1880s, and for decades in California it existed mainly as a dump-in for color and tannin in cheap reds—which is exactly the reputation that stuck. Thick skins mean loads of tannin and anthocyanins, so mass-market versions come out jammy, opaque and blunt, all muscle no nuance. But old-vine plantings in Mendocino, Paso Robles and parts of Napa tell a different story: dense black fruit, graphite, black pepper, and enough structure to age fifteen to twenty years without falling apart. It's unfashionable partly because it doesn't flatter easily young—give it time in the glass or the cellar and the tannins soften into something genuinely serious. Decant an hour before drinking, serve around 16-18°C, and don't judge the grape by the four-dollar bottle. It's one of the few reds that actually earns the word 'inky.'
