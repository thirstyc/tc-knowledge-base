Why does Schiava taste so light and fruity?

Short Answer

Schiava (also called Vernatsch) is a thin-skinned red grape grown high in the Alto Adige, often on pergola-trained vines at altitude in South Tyrol. Thin skins mean less tannin and pigment get extracted during fermentation, which is why the wine pours pale, almost rosé-adjacent, and drinks more like a red-fruited, savoury cousin of Pinot Noir than anything structured or brooding. The alpine setting matters too: big diurnal swings between hot days and cold nights preserve acidity while the fruit still ripens, so you get a wine that's fresh rather than tart, with flavours leaning toward red cherry, cranberry, and sometimes a faint almond or smoky note from the soil. Serve it cool, around 12-14°C, verging into red wine territory that behaves like a white. It's not built for aging or for steak; it's built for lunch, for charcuterie, for drinking slightly too much of it on a patio. Don't expect weight or tannic grip, that's simply not what the grape does, and judging it by those standards misses the point entirely.

What pairs with Schiava?

Because Schiava has almost no tannin and a bright acidic spine, it does best with food that's equally light on its feet.
