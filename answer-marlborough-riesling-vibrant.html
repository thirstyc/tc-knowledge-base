Why does Marlborough Riesling taste so vibrant and mineral?

Short Answer

Riesling holds onto malic acid even when it's fully ripe, which is unusual among white grapes that tend to burn through acidity as sugar climbs. Marlborough amplifies that quirk: cool Pacific air, big swings between warm days and genuinely cold nights, and a long slow ripening season that lets sugars accumulate gradually while acid barely budges. The result is a wine that can hit real ripeness and flavour without turning flabby. Marlborough's stony, free-draining alluvial soils along the Wairau add to the tension, giving that wet-stone, almost saline edge people call minerality, though it's really about texture and purity of fruit more than the wine literally tasting like rock. Most bottlings sit somewhere between bone-dry and off-dry, and the residual sugar (when present) is there to balance the acid, not to sweeten for its own sake. Serve it cold, 8 to 10°C, and don't be afraid of it with ten years on the cork: good examples develop a honeyed, petrol-tinged depth without losing the citrus spine that makes them so refreshing.
