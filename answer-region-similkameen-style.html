Why does Similkameen wine taste so intense?

Short Answer

Unlike the Okanagan just over the ridge, the Similkameen has no large lake to act as a thermal battery, soaking up heat by day and releasing it at night. So the valley swings hard: scorching, dry desert days push sugar and phenolic ripeness, then a sharp drop after sunset preserves acidity and locks in aromatic compounds. That's the mechanism behind the intensity — concentrated fruit and real tannin backbone, but with acid still holding the whole thing together rather than sagging into flab. The soil does its part too: rocky, glacially deposited ground that drains fast and stresses the vines just enough to keep yields low and flavour concentrated. Think of it as the Okanagan's smaller, wilder cousin — same general latitude and often the same grapes, but rawer and more mineral because the moderating hand of the lake isn't there. Syrah and Cabernet Franc in particular tend to show real muscle here. Serve reds a little cooler than instinct suggests, 16-17°C, so the intensity reads as structure rather than heat.

Why do winemakers rave about Similkameen?

Organic and biodynamic farming isn't a marketing checkbox here, it's close to the default — a large share of the valley's vineyards farm this way, partly because the desert-dry climate makes it genuinely feasible: low humidity means less disease pressure, so growers don't need the chemical arsenal a damper region would require.
