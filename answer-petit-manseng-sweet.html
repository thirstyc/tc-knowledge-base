Why does Petit Manseng taste so sweet and fortified?

Short Answer

It's not fortified, that's a common mix-up. No spirit gets added, all the concentration comes from the vine itself. Petit Manseng has thick, resilient skins that let it stay on the vine well past when most grapes get picked, sometimes into late autumn. As the berries hang, they lose water and shrivel slightly, a process called passerillage, which concentrates both sugar and acidity at the same time rather than one at the expense of the other. That's the key mechanism: most sweet wines get their acidity diluted as sugar climbs, but this grape holds onto both, so the wine tastes sweet without being cloying. The result is intensely flavored, often with notes of dried apricot, honey, and citrus peel, with enough acid backbone to keep it lively. It's a genuinely rare combination and part of why the grape is prized in Jurançon and Gascony despite being planted almost nowhere else in the world.
