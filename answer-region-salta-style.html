Why does Salta Torrontés taste so perfumed?

Short Answer

Torrontés carries Muscat in its lineage, which explains the grapey, floral nose - it shares the same aromatic compounds, linalool and geraniol, responsible for that jasmine-and-orange-blossom lift. Altitude turns the volume up: thinner air means more UV hitting the grapes, and vines under that kind of stress produce more of these compounds as a defense mechanism. So the nose gets more intense the higher the vineyard sits. What surprises people is the palate - it doesn't finish sweet. Big diurnal swings, hot days and genuinely cold nights, let sugars accumulate for ripeness while acidity holds steady, so winemakers can pick and ferment fully dry without the wine turning flabby or cloying. You end up with something that smells like it should be a dessert wine and drinks bone dry and crisp, which throws people every time. Serve it properly cold, 8-9°C, to keep the acid tight and stop the aromatics from tipping into soapy or blowsy. It's a genuine trick of geography and grape genetics working together, not winemaking sleight of hand.
