Why does Finger Lakes Riesling fly under the radar?

Short Answer

California built its reputation on cabernet and chardonnay, big grapes that photograph well and travel well, and that story swallowed most of the American wine conversation for fifty years. The Finger Lakes never had that kind of marketing muscle, partly because riesling is a harder sell to a market trained to want dry and bold, and partly because a cold, lake-moderated climate two hours from Buffalo just doesn't fit the sunny vineyard postcard people expect from American wine. But growers here have been quietly refining riesling since the 1970s, learning which slopes catch enough lake-reflected warmth and which clones handle the short growing season, and the wines have gotten dramatically better in the last two decades without the price catching up. That's the real reason it flies under the radar: no glamour, no scarcity story, just steady technical improvement in a region nobody was watching. If you like German-style riesling and don't want to pay German-style prices, this is where to look, and it's aging into real seriousness that most people still haven't noticed.
