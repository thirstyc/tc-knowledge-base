Why does Fiano taste so nutty and honeyed for a dry white?

Short Answer

Fiano is an old grape, documented since Roman times, grown mainly around Avellino in Campania on soils built from Vesuvius's old eruptions. That volcanic ground, high in minerals and low in organic matter, stresses the vine just enough to concentrate flavor without pushing sugar too high. The nutty, honeyed character isn't sweetness or oak; it comes from specific aromatic compounds in the grape's skin, the same family that gives some whites a slightly waxy, beeswax-like edge, plus thicker skins that add phenolic grip and texture during fermentation. None of this requires botrytis or residual sugar, which is why a bone-dry Fiano can still smell like hazelnut and honeycomb. The result is a white with more weight and chew than Pinot Grigio or basic Falanghina, closer in feel to a lightly worked Chardonnay. Serve it around 10-12°C rather than icy cold, so the texture and those toasted notes aren't muted.

What pairs with Fiano?

Fiano has more phenolic texture and body than most Italian whites, which is what lets it stand up to richer food without acidity alone doing all the work.
