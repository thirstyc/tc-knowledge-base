Can you age Champagne?

Short Answer

Yes, but the answer depends entirely on what's in the bottle. Non-vintage Champagne is built to drink now, it typically spends around 15 months to a couple of years on its lees before release, enough to give it that fresh brioche lift, but it isn't designed to develop much further and will start to fade after a few years. Vintage Champagne and prestige cuvées are a different story, made only in strong years, aged far longer on lees before release, with the acidity and structure to keep evolving in bottle for 10 to 20 years or more. As they age, the primary citrus and green apple notes fade and give way to honey, toasted nuts, dried fruit and brioche, driven by slow oxidation through the cork and continued yeast autolysis. If you're holding a bottle to see what happens, store it lying down, cool, stable and away from light. But if it's a basic non-vintage bottle, don't wait, drink it young and cold, around 8-10°C, while the fruit is still bright.
