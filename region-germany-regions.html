Mosel

The Mosel (with its tributaries the Saar and Ruwer) is Germany's most famous Riesling region and one of the world's greatest white-wine areas. Vines cling to breathtakingly steep blue-slate slopes above the twisting Mosel river, where the slate stores heat and reflects sunlight onto grapes at this cool northern latitude. Mosel Riesling is the most delicate and ethereal in Germany — low in alcohol, piercingly racy, with lime, green apple, white flowers, and a distinctive slate minerality. It spans dry to lusciously sweet; the classic fruity Kabinett and Spätlese are featherlight and ageless. Famous villages include Bernkastel, Piesport, Wehlen, Ürzig, and Erden (and Scharzhofberg on the Saar).
Serve at: 8–10°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a delicate, low-alcohol, racy, mineral Riesling; an off-dry wine for spicy food; or an ageworthy sweet white.

Rheingau

The Rheingau, where the Rhine briefly flows east–west (giving south-facing slopes), is a historic, aristocratic Riesling region. Its wines are typically fuller, firmer, and more structured than the Mosel's — powerful, mineral, and ageworthy, often made bone-dry. The region has deep tradition (it pioneered Spätlese and estate bottling) and includes top sites around Rüdesheim, Johannisberg, and Hochheim, plus excellent Spätburgunder around Assmannshausen.
Serve at: 9–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a fuller, structured, dry Riesling; a serious ageworthy German white; or German Pinot Noir (Assmannshausen).

Nahe

The Nahe, on a Rhine tributary, is a somewhat under-the-radar region making Rieslings that split the difference between Mosel delicacy and Rheingau structure — pure, aromatic, mineral, and precise, thanks to a patchwork of diverse soils (slate, volcanic, sandstone). Top villages include Schlossböckelheim and Niederhausen. A connoisseur's region offering great quality-to-price.
Serve at: 8–10°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a pure, mineral, expressive Riesling with a balance of delicacy and structure — often at better value than the famous names.

Rheinhessen

Rheinhessen, Germany's largest region, was once the source of cheap sweet Liebfraumilch but has undergone a dramatic quality revolution, led by a new generation of ambitious growers. Its best sites — especially the Roter Hang ("red slope") of red slate/clay near Nierstein and Nackenheim along the Rhine — make superb, mineral, structured dry Riesling. It's also a hotbed of dry Pinots (Weissburgunder, Grauburgunder, Spätburgunder) and experimentation.
Serve at: 9–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants an exciting, modern, dry German white; a mineral Riesling from a rising region; or good-value dry Pinots.

Pfalz

The Pfalz (Palatinate), a warm, sunny region on the same geological continuation as Alsace just across the border, makes some of Germany's ripest, richest, most powerful wines. Dry Riesling here is full-bodied and generous, and the Pfalz is also a leading region for Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir), Weissburgunder, and even Mediterranean-leaning styles. Warmer and more approachable than the northern regions.
Serve at: 9–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a riper, fuller, dry German Riesling; serious German Pinot Noir; or a warmer-climate German style.

Baden

Baden is Germany's warmest, southernmost major region, a long strip running down toward the Swiss border opposite Alsace. It's Pinot country: some of Germany's most complex, full-bodied Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir), Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris), and Weissburgunder (Pinot Blanc), especially from the volcanic Kaiserstuhl hill. Baden's wines are riper and rounder than the rest of Germany. The far-southern Markgräflerland favors Gutedel (Chasselas).
Serve at: 10–12°C whites; 14–16°C Pinot Noir.
Recommend when: the user wants Germany's fullest Pinots (red or white/grey), a food-friendly dry white, or a warmer German style.

Württemberg

Württemberg, around Stuttgart, is Germany's red-wine stronghold — unusually, more than half its vineyards are dark-skinned. Its flagship is Lemberger (Blaufränkisch), now making serious, structured, ageworthy reds from top producers, plus respectable Spätburgunder. The most-planted grape remains the pale, light Trollinger (Schiava), a simple local red. More continental than Baden, so site selection matters. Better known within Germany than abroad.
Serve at: 14–16°C reds.
Recommend when: the user wants a serious German red (Lemberger/Blaufränkisch), or is exploring German reds beyond Pinot.

Franken

Franken (Franconia), in Bavaria around Würzburg, is distinctive for its focus on Silvaner — earthy, mineral, textured dry white — rather than Riesling, and for its unique flask-shaped Bocksbeutel bottle. The wines are dry, savory, and food-friendly, from shell-limestone and other soils. A characterful, traditional region.
Serve at: 9–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a dry, earthy, mineral white (Silvaner); a distinctive food white; or something off the usual Riesling path.

Ahr

The Ahr is a tiny, northerly region — remarkably, dedicated mostly to red wine. On steep slate slopes along the Ahr river, it makes elegant, mineral Spätburgunder (Pinot Noir) that surprises with its quality given the cool, far-north latitude. A specialist's region for German Pinot.
Serve at: 14–16°C.
Recommend when: the user wants elegant, cool-climate German Pinot Noir from an unexpected place.

Smaller & eastern regions

  • Mittelrhein — steep, scenic Rhine-gorge slopes; racy, mineral Riesling; small production.
  • Rheingau's neighbors & Hessische Bergstrasse — Germany's smallest region; bold dry Riesling, rarely exported.
  • Saale-Unstrut and Sachsen (Saxony) — Germany's northernmost/easternmost regions, in the former East; dry whites (Riesling, Silvaner, the Pinots) from a cool continental climate; small but rising.

Recommend when: the user wants a rare or scenic-region German wine, or is completing a picture of German regions.

Quick regional summary germany-regions

  • Mosel (+ Saar/Ruwer): steep blue slate; the most delicate, racy, low-alcohol Riesling, dry to sweet.
  • Rheingau: fuller, structured, often-dry Riesling; historic; Spätburgunder at Assmannshausen.
  • Nahe: pure, mineral Riesling; delicacy-meets-structure; great value.
  • Rheinhessen: largest region, modern revival; mineral dry Riesling (Roter Hang) and dry Pinots.
  • Pfalz: ripe, powerful dry Riesling; leading Spätburgunder; warm.
  • Baden: warmest; Germany's finest Pinots (Noir/Gris/Blanc), volcanic Kaiserstuhl.
  • Württemberg: red stronghold; Lemberger (Blaufränkisch); light Trollinger.
  • Franken: Silvaner in the Bocksbeutel flask; dry, earthy.
  • Ahr: tiny, northerly, elegant Pinot Noir.
  • Mittelrhein, Hessische Bergstrasse, Saale-Unstrut, Sachsen: small specialist regions.
