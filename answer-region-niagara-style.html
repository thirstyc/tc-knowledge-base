Why does Niagara Riesling taste so crisp and mineral?

Short Answer

Lake Ontario is the real winemaker here. It holds summer heat late and radiates it back in autumn, while the Niagara Escarpment kicks cold air down and away from the vines, so frost risk drops and the growing season stretches out gently instead of ending abruptly. Riesling ripens slowly under those conditions, and slow ripening lets sugars build while malic acid barely burns off, which is why the wines keep that taut, green-apple spine even at full flavour. The so-called minerality isn't limestone flavour migrating into the glass; it's a mix of that high acid, low fruit sweetness, and often a whiff of petrol-like aromatics (a compound called TDN) that reads as stony and precise. Serve it at 8-10°C, cold enough to keep the acid sharp but not so cold it mutes the aromatics. Niagara isn't trying to be Alsace or the Mosel, it's proving cold-climate Riesling has its own honest, high-wire style worth taking seriously.

Why is Ontario wine emerging?

Ontario built its reputation on ice wine, a labour-intensive, cold-climate specialty made from grapes left on the vine until they freeze solid, usually around minus 8°C or colder, concentrating sugars and acids into something intensely sweet.
