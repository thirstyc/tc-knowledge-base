Why is Gewürztraminer often off-dry?

Short Answer

Alsatian tradition leans into ripeness, and Gewürztraminer is a grape that ripens with unusually high natural sugar while its acidity drops faster than most whites. Left to ferment fully dry, that combination can taste flat and one-dimensional, all perfume and no lift. So winemakers often stop fermentation slightly early, or let a touch of residual sugar remain, which does two things: it balances the low acid so the wine doesn't feel heavy, and it tempers the grape's intensely floral, lychee-and-rose-petal aromatics so they read as generous rather than overwhelming. It's a deliberate structural choice, not sloppiness or a failure to ferment dry. That said, Alsace also makes genuinely dry versions, and vendange tardive bottlings push in the other direction entirely, toward late-harvest sweetness. Check the label or ask, because the sweetness level varies more in this grape than almost any other white, and serving a bone-dry expectation against an off-dry glass is the most common way to be disappointed by it.

Why does Gewürztraminer smell so floral and exotic?

Because its skins are packed with aromatic compounds, especially terpenes like geraniol and linalool, which smell of roses and flowers, plus other compounds that give lychee and spice.
