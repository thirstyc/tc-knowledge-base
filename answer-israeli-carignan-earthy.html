Why does Israeli Carignan taste so rustic and earthy?

Short Answer

Carignan was planted across Israel's coastal plain decades ago mostly for bulk, high-yield production, the kind of grape nobody expected to make something worth talking about. But old vines, once yields are cut back and growers stop treating them as filler, turn out to be capable of real character: naturally high acidity, firm tannin, and a rustic, earthy, almost dusty quality that comes from both the grape itself and the way these vines are often dry-farmed with minimal irrigation. Producers making serious versions now tend to work with whole clusters, native yeasts, and light oak handling, letting the vineyard show through rather than smoothing it over. The Mediterranean climate pushes ripeness, but the tannin and earthiness keep it from feeling soft or generic. It's a grape that used to be an afterthought and is now one of the more interesting stories in Israeli wine precisely because winemakers stopped trying to make it taste like something else.
