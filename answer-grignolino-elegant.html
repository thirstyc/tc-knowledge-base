Why does Grignolino taste so light and elegant?

Short Answer

Grignolino is a strange case: the grape is loaded with seeds relative to pulp, and seeds carry harsh, bitter tannins, so you'd expect a tough wine. Instead producers keep skin contact short and avoid pressing hard, which limits extraction and leaves you with pale color, wispy tannin, and bright acidity instead. Piedmont's cooler pockets around Asti and Monferrato also keep sugar levels moderate, so alcohol stays light and the wine never gets heavy. What you're left with is closer in structure to a rosé than to Barolo, even though it's technically a red: rose petal, red currant, a peppery snap on the finish. It's not built for aging or bold extraction, it's built for drinking young and slightly chilled, around 13-14°C. That's also why it never gets the prestige treatment Nebbiolo does. Elegance here is really the absence of things, low tannin, low pigment, low weight, rather than any winemaking trick.
