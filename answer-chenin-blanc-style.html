Why does Chenin Blanc taste so different depending on the bottle?

Short Answer

Chenin's versatility comes down to two traits baked into the grape: naturally high acidity that persists at almost any ripeness level, and a thin skin that's unusually receptive to noble rot. That acid means winemakers can pick early for a bone-dry, taut style, or leave the fruit hanging longer for something off-dry without the wine turning flabby — the acid holds the structure together either way. The same skin susceptibility to botrytis, common in humid Loire autumns, lets Chenin swing all the way to lusciously sweet dessert wine when conditions cooperate, as they sometimes do on the sweet side of Vouvray. Base wines with that much acid also make excellent sparkling wine, which is why traditional-method Loire fizz leans on Chenin so heavily. So a bottle labelled Vouvray could be bone-dry, off-dry or sweet depending on the vintage and the producer's choice — there's no single reliable house style the way there is with, say, Chablis. Read the label closely, because the appellation name alone tells you almost nothing about what's actually in the glass.
