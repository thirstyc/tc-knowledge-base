Why does Argentinean Syrah fly under the radar?

Short Answer

Malbec sucked up all the attention Argentina had to give, and Syrah got left in the room next door. That's a shame, because the same conditions that make Malbec great — high-altitude vineyards in Mendoza, Salta's Cafayate valley up around 1,700 metres, blazing sun during the day and a hard drop in temperature at night — do something genuinely interesting to Syrah. The heat pushes ripeness and colour, the cold nights lock in acidity and aromatic lift, so you get wines with dark fruit and pepper and violet, but without the flabbiness you'd expect from a hot climate. Some producers blend it with Malbec, a nod to the old Rhône habit of co-fermenting Syrah with a white grape for lift, though here it's just two reds working together. If you find a single-varietal Argentine Syrah from Salta, buy it — it's usually more distinctive than another bottle of Malbec, and it's still priced like nobody's noticed.

Why does Argentinean Syrah taste so ripe and structured?

Syrah in Argentina is planted at some of the most extreme altitudes used for wine anywhere, including sites in Salta's Cafayate valley that push past 1700 metres, higher even than most Mendoza Malbec.
