What's the Difference Between Tokaji Sweet and Dry Furmint?

Same grape, two completely different winemaking decisions. Sweet Tokaji is built around botrytis — noble rot that shrivels the berries, concentrating sugar and acid while adding a honeyed, saffron-like complexity. Those aszú berries get picked individually, sometimes fermented alongside a base wine, then aged oxidatively in part-filled barrels that let air in slowly, the way Sherry is made. The result lands anywhere from moderately sweet to intensely so, with real complexity and real cost, since hand-harvesting shriveled berries is brutally labour-intensive. Dry Furmint skips all of that: grapes are picked at normal ripeness, fermented to bone dry, usually in stainless steel or neutral oak, so the volcanic minerality and searing acidity come through unfiltered by sugar or oxidation. One is a sipping wine for the end of a meal, in small glasses, savored slowly. The other is a table wine you'd happily drink through a whole dinner. If you've only had sweet Tokaji, dry Furmint will feel like an entirely different grape — that's the point.
