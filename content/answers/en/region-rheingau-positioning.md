Why is Rheingau historically important?

Rheingau is where German Riesling stopped being seen as a curiosity and got taken seriously as fine wine. Its steep, south-facing slopes along the Rhine were planted and refined by Cistercian monks at Kloster Eberbach centuries ago, and it was here, almost by accident at Schloss Johannisberg, that a delayed harvest revealed what botrytis could do to Riesling, effectively discovering the late-harvest style that German wine built its reputation on. The region later became a testing ground for quality classification, the precursor to today's VDP vineyard rankings that rate sites by soil and exposure rather than just sugar level at harvest. It's smaller than the Mosel, more prestige-driven, and less about volume. If Mosel Riesling is about delicacy and tension, Rheingau is where growers proved the grape could also be powerful, fully dry, and capable of aging for decades, holding its own against any serious white wine in the world.
