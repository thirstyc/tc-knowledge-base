Why Does Tannat Have Such High Tannin?

Tannat is just a naturally tannic grape: thick skins loaded with phenolic compounds, and traditional winemaking left plenty of seed contact during fermentation, which pulls out even more tannin. It originated in southwest France, in Madiran, where it was already known as a bruiser, but Uruguay's warmer climate ripens the fruit tannins while the skin tannins stay just as forceful, so you get power without the green edge. Modern Uruguayan producers have gotten smart about it, using micro-oxygenation to slowly soften tannins in tank, or blending in Merlot to round things out. Older-style, unblended Tannat is still a jaw-clencher young. It's not a flaw, it's the grape's whole identity, the same way Nebbiolo or Sagrantino build their reputations on structure. You either give it time in the cellar or time at the table with something fatty enough to fight back. Either way, don't expect it to be polite.
