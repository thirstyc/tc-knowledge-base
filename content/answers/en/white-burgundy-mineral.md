Why does White Burgundy (Chardonnay) taste so mineral?

The mineral streak in White Burgundy comes from a mix of soil, climate, and restraint rather than any literal taste of rock, no matter how often that phrase gets thrown around. Burgundy's limestone and marl soils drain well but hold enough moisture to stress the vines just slightly, concentrating flavour without ripening the grapes too fast, and the region's genuinely cool, marginal climate keeps natural acidity high. That acidity is the real engine behind the sensation people call minerality: it reads on the palate as a chalky, saline, almost electric tension rather than a specific flavour. Winemaking reinforces it, many top producers use neutral or older oak, indigenous yeasts, and lees stirring instead of new oak and heavy malolactic fermentation, so the fruit and soil signal stays clear instead of getting buried in vanilla and butter. Serve it at 10-12°C, cold enough to keep that acid-driven precision intact but not so cold you mute the aromatics. It's one of the reference points for what serious, food-driven Chardonnay can be.
