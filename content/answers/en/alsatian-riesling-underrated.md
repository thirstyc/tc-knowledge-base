Why does Alsatian Riesling fly under the radar?

Blame the label - literally. Alsace doesn't require producers to state sweetness on the bottle, so a bone-dry Riesling and one with noticeable residual sugar can look identical on the shelf. Shoppers who got burned once by an unexpectedly sweet bottle tend to write off the whole category rather than learn to read producer style. There's also guilt by association: Riesling as a grape still carries decades of baggage from mass-produced, sugary German exports, and Alsace's similarly Germanic-looking flute bottles don't help. In reality, most Alsatian Riesling made today is dry to off-dry, with the genuinely sweet stuff - Vendanges Tardives, Sélection de Grains Nobles - clearly reserved for late-harvest and noble-rot wines priced and marketed as dessert wines. The bottles that actually fly under the radar are the everyday village and Grand Cru releases: tightly wound, mineral-driven, built to age. If a producer's style is unfamiliar, a quick question at the wine shop clears up the sweetness guessing game before you buy.
