Why does Sagrantino taste so tannic-powerful?

This grape carries some of the highest tannin and polyphenol levels of anything grown in Italy, and the reason traces back to its skins. Sagrantino has unusually thick, robust skins, an adaptation to Umbria's sun and wind around Montefalco, and tannin lives almost entirely in the skins and seeds, not the pulp. Traditional vinification there leans into this with extended maceration, extracting even more structure than the grape would give up on its own. Young, that translates into a wine that grips the palate hard, dry and almost chalky, before you get much fruit. It's also why Sagrantino was historically dried into a sweet passito style, since the tannin and acidity could balance out that much sugar without collapsing into flab. The dry table wine version needs a decade or so in bottle for the tannin to soften and integrate, or serious decanting and fatty food if you're drinking it young. Serve around 17-18°C, never warmer.
