Why do Douro reds fly under the radar?

Port has owned this valley's reputation for three centuries, and for most of that time the region's grape-growing quotas were designed to funnel the best fruit toward fortified wine, leaving dry table wine as the leftover project nobody bothered marketing. That only really shifted in the last few decades, as producers started deliberately holding back top parcels, often old, mixed-variety vineyards planted with a dozen different grapes intermingled, for still red wine instead of Port. Same schist soils that force vine roots deep for water, same heat-loving, thick-skinned grapes, same brutal terraced hillsides, just vinified dry instead of fortified and sweetened. The result is structured, tannic, age-worthy red wine that rivals plenty of more famous regions, made by people who've been growing world-class fruit here for generations but only recently got permission, commercially and culturally, to show what it can do without fortifying it first. It's a region still catching up to its own quality, which is exactly why the prices haven't caught up yet either.
