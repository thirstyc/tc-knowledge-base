Why does Sancerre taste so crisp-mineral?

It comes down to what's under the vines and how the grape reacts to it. Sancerre sits on a patchwork of Kimmeridgian limestone and clay, plus flinty silex soils in parts of the appellation, all of it cool-climate Loire terrain far enough north that ripening is a real challenge every vintage. Sauvignon Blanc thrives on that stress: it retains malic acid because the growing season rarely gets hot enough to burn it off, and the limestone drains well while reflecting a bit of heat back onto the vines. That combination gives you the bracing, palate-cleansing acidity and the chalky, almost smoky mineral edge people associate with the wine, rather than the tropical fruit bomb Sauvignon Blanc becomes in warmer places like parts of California. Winemakers mostly stay hands-off, using neutral stainless steel instead of oak, so nothing masks that acid-mineral backbone. Serve it cold, 8 to 10°C, with goat cheese or shellfish, and don't cellar it long. This is a wine built to drink young and taut.
