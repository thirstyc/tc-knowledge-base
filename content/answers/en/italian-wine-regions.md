What are Italian wine regions?

Italy has twenty administrative regions, and every one makes wine. A handful are especially important to know.

In the north:

- Piedmont: Barolo and Barbaresco from Nebbiolo, plus Barbera, Dolcetto and Moscato d'Asti.
- Veneto: Prosecco, Valpolicella, Amarone and Soave.
- Friuli-Venezia Giulia: some of Italy's best whites, and a home of orange wine.
- Trentino-Alto Adige: crisp, alpine whites and sparkling Trento.
- Lombardy: Franciacorta, Italy's top traditional-method sparkling wine.

In the centre:

- Tuscany: Chianti, Brunello di Montalcino, Vino Nobile and the Super Tuscans, mostly built on Sangiovese.
- Marche: Verdicchio, a crisp, food-friendly white.
- Abruzzo: generous, affordable Montepulciano d'Abruzzo.

In the south and islands:

- Campania: Aglianico, Fiano and Greco.
- Puglia: ripe reds from Primitivo and Negroamaro.
- Sicily: Nero d'Avola, and the volcanic wines of Etna.
- Sardinia: Cannonau and Vermentino.

Italian wine is classified as DOCG (the strictest), DOC and IGT, but some excellent wines use the broader IGT category to escape the rules.

Italy grows hundreds of native grapes, which makes it one of the most rewarding countries to explore.
