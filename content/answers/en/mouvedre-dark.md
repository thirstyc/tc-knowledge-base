Why does Mourvèdre taste so dark and spicy?

Mourvèdre is a late-ripening grape that needs real heat to reach full maturity, which is why it thrives in places like Bandol on the Mediterranean coast or as Monastrell in southern Spain. That long hang time on the vine does two things: it builds sugar, which becomes alcohol, often pushing wines past 14%, and it lets the thick skins develop deeply. Those skins are the real story. They're loaded with tannin and pigment, which is why the wine comes out so dark and structured, with a grip that can feel almost chewy when young. The spice and gamey, dried-herb character come from compounds that develop during that extended ripening under sun and, in Bandol's case, coastal wind exposure. Young Mourvèdre can taste closed and a bit fierce, tannin dominating fruit, which is why producers often blend it or age it in oak and bottle for years before release. Given time, the tannins soften and the meaty, peppery depth comes forward properly.
