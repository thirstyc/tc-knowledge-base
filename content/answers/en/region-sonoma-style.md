Why does Sonoma Pinot taste so bright and red-fruited?

Fog is the mechanism. Cold Pacific air pushes inland through gaps in the coastal range - Petaluma Gap, the Russian River corridor - and blankets vineyards most mornings, sometimes lingering past noon. That daily cooling slows ripening and keeps grapes hanging longer without piling on sugar, meaning lower potential alcohol at harvest and malic acid that doesn't burn off the way it would in a hot, sunny climate. Lower sugar accumulation also means less of the dark, jammy fruit you get from riper Pinot grown somewhere warmer - Sonoma's version stays in red-fruit territory, cherry, cranberry, red currant, with real acid underneath holding the shape together. It's the opposite mechanism from Napa's inland valley floor, which bakes in heat reflected off the hills and is built for structured, tannic Cabernet instead. Chill Sonoma Pinot slightly, around 14°C, and that acid keeps everything precise instead of the wine turning soft and vague as it warms up in the glass.
