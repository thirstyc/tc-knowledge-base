Why does Kékfrankos taste so spicy and structured?

The spice and structure in Kékfrankos, known outside Hungary as Blaufränkisch, come down to where and how slowly it ripens. Grown in cooler corners of the country like Eger and Sopron, the grape hangs on the vine long enough to develop flavour without losing its natural malic acidity, which is what gives the wine its brisk, savoury edge rather than a soft, fruit-forward one. That acidity works alongside a firm, moderate tannin that leans more peppery and grippy than plush, giving the wine real backbone on the palate. The peppery, slightly spicy character people notice is partly aromatic, coming from compounds the grape develops in these cooler sites, and partly structural, since the acid-tannin interplay makes the wine feel taut and energetic rather than round. It's the same grape family that makes serious, age-worthy reds in Austria's Mittelburgenland, so the structure isn't a fluke, it's the grape's signature. Decant it or give it a year or two in bottle and that spice integrates into something more savoury than sharp.
