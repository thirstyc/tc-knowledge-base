Why does white Rioja taste so mineral?

That mineral edge comes mostly from Viura, also called Macabeo, the dominant grape in white Rioja, grown on limestone and clay-limestone soils around Haro and Laguardia. Viura keeps decent acidity even at moderate ripeness, and that acid is largely what your palate reads as "mineral" - a stony, saline tension on the finish, since vines don't actually transmit dissolved rock into wine flavour. Two winemaking traditions shape how pronounced that character comes across. Traditional white Rioja ages for years in American oak, turning nutty, waxy, and golden, with the mineral edge folded into oxidative complexity. Modern white Rioja is made in stainless steel to preserve freshness, showing citrus, white flower, and a crisper, more overtly saline finish. Either way, look for that dry, stony grip on the back end - it's what separates real Rioja Blanco from a generic soft, fruity white. Serve around 10-12°C; too cold and you'll mute the texture, especially in the oak-aged style.
