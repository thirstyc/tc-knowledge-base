Why does Tinta de Toro fly under the radar?

Same grape as Rioja and Ribera del Duero, different reputation entirely. Toro spent most of the 20th century as a bulk-wine region, its wines rustic and tannic enough that they needed a decade in a cellar before they were pleasant, which is not a great pitch when Rioja was busy building an international brand around gentler, oak-aged Tempranillo. Ribera del Duero got there too, with big investment and critics anointing it Spain's answer to Bordeaux in the 1980s and 90s. Toro's renaissance came later and with less marketing muscle behind it - a handful of ambitious producers modernizing winemaking, taming the tannin without losing the concentration those old sandy-soil vines naturally give. The wines now rival their better-known cousins for depth and often beat them on value, but the name recognition and the price premium haven't caught up. For drinkers that's good news: you can buy serious, powerful Tempranillo out of Toro for less than you'd pay for a mid-tier Ribera del Duero of similar quality.
