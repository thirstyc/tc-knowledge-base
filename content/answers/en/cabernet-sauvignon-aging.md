Why Does Cabernet Sauvignon Age So Well?

Cabernet Sauvignon has thick skins, which means more tannin and pigment extracted during fermentation, plus naturally high acidity from grapes that ripen late and hang onto structure even at high sugar levels. Tannin and acidity are both preservatives in effect: tannin binds and slowly precipitates out over years, softening the wine's texture, while acidity resists microbial spoilage and keeps the wine tasting fresh rather than flat. That combination gives Cabernet a long runway. Over 10-20 years the primary fruit -- blackcurrant, plum -- fades and gives way to secondary and tertiary notes: cedar, tobacco, leather, dried herbs, sometimes graphite. This isn't automatically "better," just different; some people prefer the raw fruit of a young bottle. What ages well is specifically well-structured Cabernet from a good vintage -- thin, over-cropped, or heavily diluted versions won't improve, they'll just fall apart. Store it around 12-14C and don't rush it open; a 20-year-old Cabernet needs an hour of air minimum. The tannin and acid are exactly why serious Cabernet costs more as a young wine -- you're paying for the aging potential, not just the taste in the glass today.
