What's the Difference Between Napa and Bordeaux Cabernet?

The biggest driver is climate. Napa's warm, sunny, reliable growing season pushes grapes to full ripeness every year, giving wines with riper black fruit, higher alcohol (often 14.5% plus), and a plush, powerful style that's approachable relatively young. Bordeaux's cooler, more variable Atlantic climate means Cabernet Sauvignon rarely ripens on its own terms, so it's traditionally blended with Merlot and Cabernet Franc -- Merlot softening the tannin and filling out mid-palate, Cabernet Franc adding perfume and lift. That blending isn't dilution so much as balancing an unripe backbone. The result is a wine built on tension: firmer tannin, higher acidity, savoury and earthy notes (graphite, tobacco, cedar) alongside the fruit, and a structure that often demands a decade or more before it's pleasant to drink. Napa Cabernet is usually 100% or nearly so, leaning on ripeness rather than blending for complexity. Serve both around 16-18C. Neither style is objectively better -- Napa rewards you sooner, Bordeaux rewards patience, and which one you reach for says more about your mood than your palate.
