Why does Alvarinho fly under the radar outside Portugal?

Most people's mental image of Vinho Verde is the cheap, faintly spritzy, low-alcohol blend sold in tall green bottles, meant to be chugged on a patio. That wine is a blend of several grapes, picked early, sometimes given a small CO2 boost, and it's genuinely a different animal from what Alvarinho can do on its own. In the Monção e Melgaço subregion near the Minho river, the grape gets a warmer microclimate and a later harvest, so it ripens fully instead of staying green and tart. Bottled as a single varietal, it comes in fuller-bodied, with real alcohol (often 12.5 to 13.5%), concentrated citrus and orchard fruit, and enough structure to age a few years in bottle. It's closer in seriousness to a good Chablis than to a summer cooler. The export market never really corrected the branding, so the cheap stuff still defines the grape's reputation outside Portugal, while the good stuff quietly ages in cellars nobody's heard of.
