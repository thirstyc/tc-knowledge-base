Why does Valpolicella Ripasso taste so complex?

The complexity comes from two things stacking on top of each other: the grape blend and the process. Corvina and Corvinone bring bright red cherry and a savory, almost bitter-almond edge, while Rondinella adds color and fruit weight. Then the wine gets repassed over Amarone's leftover dried-grape skins, which still hold concentrated sugar, tannin and glycerol even after fermentation. That second contact pulls raisin, fig, baking spice and a touch of chocolate into what would otherwise be a simple, fresh red, while also nudging up the alcohol and body. Many producers age it briefly in oak afterward, which rounds the tannins and adds a layer of vanilla or clove without erasing the fruit underneath. The result reads more layered and structured than its everyday Valpolicella sibling, but without the raisined intensity of a wine that was fully dried from the start. Decant it for twenty minutes if you can, and serve around 17 degrees so the spice notes actually show up.
