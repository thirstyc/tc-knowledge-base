Why does Limnio taste so earthy and mineral?

Limnio is one of the oldest named grape varieties still in commercial production, and it grows almost exclusively on volcanic and sandy soils around the northern Aegean, particularly its namesake island of Limnos. Volcanic soil is mineral-rich but poor in organic matter, which stresses the vine and tends to push savoury, stony, almost smoky notes into the fruit rather than pure sweetness. Limnio itself is naturally low in anthocyanins and moderate in tannin, so it never gets heavy or jammy, it stays structured but light on its feet, closer in weight to a Cinsault than a Cabernet. The earthiness people pick up, mushroom, dried herb, a whiff of iron, comes from that combination of volcanic terroir and the grape's naturally restrained fruit profile letting the site show through instead of masking it. Serve it around 15-16°C, slightly cooler than a typical red, so the mineral edge stays crisp instead of going flat.
