Why does Central Otago Pinot Noir taste so bold and structured?

Central Otago sits further south than almost anywhere else Pinot Noir is grown seriously, and that latitude comes with real extremes: intense UV exposure, big diurnal temperature swings, and a short, dry, continental growing season very unlike Burgundy's maritime moderation. The sunlight ripens fruit fully and builds thick, deeply pigmented skins, which is where Pinot Noir gets its colour, tannin and dark-fruit weight. Meanwhile cold nights lock in acidity even as sugars climb, so you get wines that are genuinely ripe, structured and higher in alcohol than most Pinot Noir, without turning flabby. Add low-vigour, schist-heavy soils that stress the vines just enough, and you get concentrated, small berries rather than diluted ones. The result is a Pinot Noir that's less about Burgundian perfume and more about dark cherry, spice and grip, closer in muscle to a cool-climate Syrah than to a delicate red. Serve it a touch cooler than a big red, around 15-16°C, and don't be afraid of it standing up to red meat.
