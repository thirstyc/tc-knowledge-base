Why does South African Chenin Blanc taste so fruity and mineral?

The fruit and minerality both trace back to where the vines actually sit. Coastal and near-coastal sites get regular cooling from the Atlantic and the Cape's afternoon wind, so grapes ripen slowly and hang onto malic and tartaric acid instead of burning it off, which is what keeps the wine crisp rather than soft. That slow ripening also lets stone fruit flavours like peach and apricot develop fully without tipping into overripe, tropical territory. The mineral edge comes from soil, much of the best Chenin grows on decomposed granite or shale, low-fertility ground that stresses the vine just enough to concentrate flavour rather than volume. None of that is literally rock flavour dissolving into the grape, it's more that stressed, well-drained sites produce wines with a saline, stony finish that reads as minerality. Serve it properly chilled, around 8-10°C, and the acid-fruit balance shows exactly why Chenin from here punches so far above its price.
