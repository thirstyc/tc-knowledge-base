Why does Champagne Dom Pérignon taste so iconic?

Most of what people taste as iconic comes down to a few concrete winemaking choices, not marketing. It's a vintage-only wine, made exclusively in years the house judges strong enough, using Chardonnay and Pinot Noir from a small set of top-rated vineyards, so the raw material starts out unusually concentrated. From there it spends far longer aging on its lees than typical Champagne, sometimes many years past when a base wine would normally be disgorged. That extended contact with spent yeast drives autolysis, the slow breakdown of yeast cells that releases compounds responsible for brioche, toasted nut and creamy texture notes, while also building a very fine, persistent bubble. Dosage tends to be kept low, letting the wine's own acidity and fruit show through rather than masking anything with sweetness. None of this is unique in isolation, other top houses do versions of the same thing, but the combination of vineyard selection, single vintage and unusually patient aging is why it reads as more layered and precise than everyday non-vintage Champagne. Serve it around 10°C, not colder, or you'll mute everything that aging built.
