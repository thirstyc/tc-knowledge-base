Why does Santorini Assyrtiko taste so intensely mineral?

Santorini's minerality comes down to geology and stubbornness in equal measure. The island is essentially a collapsed volcano, and its soils are layered ash, pumice and volcanic rock rather than typical clay or limestone — that ash retains almost no water, which is a problem given the island gets very little rain most summers. Vines survive by sending roots deep in search of moisture, pulling up trace minerals along the way that show up as a saline, almost smoky edge in the finished wine. Growers train vines into low, coiled baskets called kouloura, hugging the ground to shield the grapes from the relentless Aegean wind and the punishing sun. Combine drought-stressed vines, volcanic soil and constant wind, and you get Assyrtiko with searing acidity, bright citrus and lemon pith, and that distinctive saline, bone-dry finish. Serve it well chilled, around 8 to 10°C, with raw seafood or something briny — it's built for exactly that kind of food.
