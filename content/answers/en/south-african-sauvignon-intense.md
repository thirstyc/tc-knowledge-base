Why does South African Sauvignon Blanc taste so herbaceous and intense?

Cold Atlantic air is doing the work here. In regions like Darling, Elim, and Durbanville, wind and fog off the Benguela current keep daytime temperatures down and nights properly cold, which slows ripening and lets the grape hang onto its natural aromatic compounds — methoxypyrazines, the same chemicals responsible for green pepper and gooseberry notes, and thiols, which give the tropical, passionfruit lift. Warmer sites burn these off before harvest; cool ones preserve them, which is why these wines can taste almost aggressively green and zesty compared to riper Sauvignon from warmer regions. Many producers pick at night specifically to lock in freshness before the sun heats the fruit. Fermentation is almost always in stainless steel, no oak, to keep the wine razor-sharp rather than rounding it out. The acidity isn't a stylistic choice so much as a direct consequence of cool nights slowing sugar accumulation while acid stays high. Serve it cold, around 8-10°C, and pair it with something that can stand up to that intensity — goat cheese, ceviche, or anything with a squeeze of lime.
