Why does Dornfelder taste so dark and juicy?

Dornfelder is a cross bred in Germany in the early 1900s specifically to fix two problems: German reds were pale and thin. It succeeded almost too well. The grape has unusually thick, dark-pigmented skins, and in some clones even the flesh carries color, which is rare and gives the wine its deep purple-black hue without any winemaking trickery. Tannins land in the medium range, soft enough to drink young, and acidity is moderate rather than searing, which lets the black cherry and blackberry fruit read as juicy rather than tart. Most producers ferment it in stainless steel to preserve that fruit-forward freshness rather than aging it in oak, and a touch of residual sugar is common in the simpler bottlings, which amplifies the juicy impression further. Serve it cool, around 12 to 14 degrees, closer to how you'd treat a light red than a Cabernet. It's Germany's answer to easy-drinking red, built for immediacy rather than the cellar.
