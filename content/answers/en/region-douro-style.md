Why does Douro red wine taste so intense?

The Douro is brutal country for a vine. Vineyards are terraced into schist bedrock on slopes so steep that much of the work still has to be done by hand or by helicopter-fed cable systems, and the schist itself matters: it fractures vertically, so roots drive deep looking for water instead of spreading out near the surface. Deep roots plus a scorching, reflective valley mean small berries with thick skins, and thick skins mean more tannin and pigment concentrated into less juice. These are the same field blends of Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz and others that go into Port, so the genetic material was already built for intensity, high sugar, deep colour, structure to survive fortification. When winemakers vinify that fruit dry instead of stopping fermentation with grape spirit, all that natural concentration stays in the glass with nowhere to hide. Serve these around 16-17°C, decant the younger ones an hour ahead, and expect wines built for grilled meat and hard cheese rather than polite sipping.
