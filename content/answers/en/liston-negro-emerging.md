Why is Liston Negro rare?

Rarity here isn't a marketing story, it's structural. Canary Islands vineyards are split into thousands of tiny plots, often inherited and farmed by hand because the terraced volcanic hillsides can't take a tractor. There's no way to scale production the way you could on a flat Mediterranean vineyard, so yields stay small no matter how much demand grows. For most of the twentieth century that meant the wine barely left the islands, drunk locally and largely unknown even within Spain. What's changed is attention, not supply: as sommeliers went hunting for old-vine, low-intervention reds with real acidity, Listán Negro's ungrafted volcanic vineyards became exactly the kind of thing they were looking for. Small négociants started bringing in tiny allocations, and now a few bottles reach international markets each vintage. But the underlying production hasn't grown to meet the new interest, and given the terrain, it probably can't. That's why it stays scarce even as its reputation grows.
