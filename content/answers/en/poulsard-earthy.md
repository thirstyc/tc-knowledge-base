Why does Poulsard taste so light and earthy?

Poulsard, sometimes spelled Ploussard, has famously thin skins and low pigment content, so even though it's technically a red grape it often looks closer to a dark rosé in the glass. Thin skins mean minimal tannin extraction, and many Jura producers lean into that by using gentle techniques like whole-cluster, semi-carbonic maceration rather than heavy punch-downs, which keeps things light and fresh rather than structured. The earthiness comes from where it's grown: the Jura's cool continental climate and clay-limestone soils, combined with a strong natural-wine tradition of low sulfur use, produce savory, forest-floor, sometimes faintly animal notes alongside red berry fruit. Think of it as sitting somewhere between a light Pinot Noir and a serious rosé, but more rustic and savory than either. Serve it lightly chilled, around 12-14°C, which sharpens the fruit and keeps the earthy side from tipping into funky.
