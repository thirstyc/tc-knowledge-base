Why are Rieslings so good?

Riesling has an unusual combination: high acidity, low alcohol, intense aroma and the ability to age for decades. Very few grapes do all four.

That acidity is the key. It keeps the wine fresh, balances sweetness when there is any, and preserves it in the bottle for years. A good Riesling at fifteen or twenty years old can taste younger than most whites at five.

It's also one of the most transparent grapes. Riesling is almost never oaked, so nothing hides the vineyard. Slate soils in the Mosel, granite in Alsace and limestone in Austria all show through in the glass.

Its range is another strength. The same grape makes bone-dry wines, gently off-dry ones, and lusciously sweet late-harvest and ice wines, so it fits almost any moment.

At the table it's exceptionally useful, especially the off-dry styles, which handle spicy food, pork and Asian dishes better than almost any other wine.

With age, Riesling develops a distinctive petrol-like note that devotees love. It isn't a fault.

The one hurdle is the label: German Rieslings don't always say how sweet they are. Look for "trocken" if you want dry.
