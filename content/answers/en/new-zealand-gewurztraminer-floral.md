Why does New Zealand Gewürztraminer taste so aromatic and floral?

Gewürztraminer's perfume comes from terpenes in the grape itself, mainly linalool and geraniol, the same aromatic compounds found in rose petals and lychee. Every Gewürztraminer has them, but cool climates let them show. In New Zealand, especially Central Otago and pockets of Marlborough, long slow ripening under wide diurnal swings means the grape hangs longer without piling on sugar, giving the vine time to build those aromatic compounds without the wine turning heavy or blowsy. Warm regions push sugar and alcohol up before the aromatics fully develop, and the wine ends up flabby and perfumed in a clumsier way. The result here is lifted rose, lychee, ginger and a faint white pepper note, usually with enough natural acidity to keep it from feeling like drinking potpourri. Serve it cold, around 8 to 10°C, cool enough to keep the aromatics from turning blowsy in the glass but not so cold you mute them entirely. Give it a few minutes to open once poured.
