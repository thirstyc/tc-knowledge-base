Why does Tinta de Toro taste so powerful and dark?

Tinta de Toro is really just Tempranillo, but a distinct clone that mutated on its own in the Toro DO along the Duero west of Valladolid. The region sits high, 650-750 metres, on sandy soils that phylloxera never managed to colonize, so a lot of the vines are old, ungrafted, and naturally low-yielding. Low yields plus thick-skinned, small berries mean a much higher skin-to-juice ratio than standard Tempranillo, and skins are where colour, tannin and flavour compounds live. The climate is brutally continental: scorching days push sugar and phenolic ripeness hard, cold nights hang onto acidity so the wine doesn't turn flabby. The result is dense, dark-fruited wine with real structure and alcohol often north of 14%. Traditional producers age it in American oak, which layers in vanilla and coconut and makes the whole thing feel even bigger. Serve it at 16-18°C, give a young bottle an hour in a decanter, and put it next to something fatty and grilled - lamb, chorizo, aged manchego - the tannin needs the resistance.
