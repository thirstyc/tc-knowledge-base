Why does Georgian wine taste so tannic and earthy, even the whites?

The tannin in white Georgian wine comes from skin contact, full stop - white grapes fermented like reds, with skins and seeds left in the juice for weeks or months instead of being pressed off right away. That's what makes it 'orange': the colour and grip come from the same compounds that structure red wine, just extracted from white grapes. This happens inside qvevri, large clay vessels buried underground, which keep fermentation cool and steady without mechanical intervention - the earth does the job a temperature-controlled tank would elsewhere. The clay is also slightly porous, letting in tiny amounts of oxygen over months, which softens sharp edges and adds a savoury, almost tea-like earthiness. None of this is a modern stylistic choice; it's simply how wine was made here for thousands of years before stainless steel existed. Serve these around 12-14°C, warmer than a typical crisp white, so the tannin has room to show instead of being chilled into numbness.
