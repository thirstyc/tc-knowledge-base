Why does Grenache get less credit than Syrah or Mourvèdre?

Grenache has thin skin and ripens late with huge sugar accumulation, which means high alcohol, pale color, and tannin so soft it barely registers. That's exactly why it got typecast as a blending grape: on its own it can taste plush and simple, even flabby, so winemakers lean on Syrah for color and structure and Mourvèdre for grip and earthiness. The GSM blend isn't a compromise, it's division of labor. But give Grenache old vines, low yields, and poor rocky soil like the galets of Châteauneuf-du-Pape or the schist of Priorat, and the vine stresses just enough to concentrate flavor without losing its silky texture. Suddenly you get something perfumed, spicy, almost weightless despite 14-15% alcohol. Most drinkers have had gallons of Grenache without knowing it, buried in Côtes du Rhône blends or cheap Spanish rosé. Serve a serious single-varietal bottling around 16-17°C, slightly cooler than a typical red, so the alcohol doesn't dominate the fruit.
