Why Is Dry Furmint So Mineral and Age-Worthy?

Tokaj sits on a volcanic base of rhyolite and andesite tuff, broken down over millennia into soils that force vine roots deep and imprint a stony, saline edge on the wine. That's the mineral half. The acid half comes from Furmint's natural chemistry: it's a late-ripening, thick-skinned grape that holds onto malic acid even as sugars climb, so you get physiological ripeness and Riesling-level acidity in the same bottle. That acid is what lets the wine age — it acts as a preservative, slowing oxidation and letting the wine develop rather than collapse. Give a serious dry Furmint eight to fifteen years and the linear, citrusy profile turns toward beeswax, dried apricot and wet stone, without losing the spine. Drink young at 10-12°C for the citrus-and-flint version; if you find an old bottle, it's worth the patience. The absurd part is the price: this level of structure and aging pedigree usually costs three times what Furmint does, purely because nobody outside Hungary is bidding it up yet.
