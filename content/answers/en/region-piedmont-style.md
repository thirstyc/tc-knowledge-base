Why does Piedmont Nebbiolo taste so tannic and floral?

Nebbiolo's tannin comes from thick, gritty skins packed with tannic compounds, and the grape ripens so late (often into October or November) that it needs every bit of that structure to handle a long hang time in the fog. The name comes from "nebbia," the mist that rolls through the Langhe hills at harvest, cooling the grapes slowly rather than letting them ripen fast and soft. That slow ripening keeps acidity high even as sugars climb, so you end up with a wine that's pale as Pinot Noir but hits like a much bigger red. The rose petal, tar, and dried cherry aromatics come from the grape's naturally high concentration of specific aromatic compounds, not oak. Barolo and Barbaresco both need years, sometimes a decade, for those tannins to soften and integrate. Decant young bottles hard, or just wait. Serve around 16-18°C, never colder, or the tannins turn harsh and the perfume disappears.
