Why does Ribera del Duero taste so much darker and denser than Rioja?

Same grape as Rioja, Tempranillo, but the place changes it completely. Ribera del Duero sits at serious altitude, often 800 metres or higher, which means big swings between hot days and cold nights. That diurnal shift slows ripening and lets grapes build thick skins and concentrated pigment while still holding onto acidity, so the wine ends up darker in color and denser in texture than its Rioja cousin. Local Tempranillo clones here, sometimes called Tinto Fino or Tinta del País, also tend to have smaller berries and thicker skins than what's typically planted in Rioja, pushing tannin and color even further. Winemakers here also lean less on American oak and its vanilla sweetness, favoring French barrels or more restrained oak regimes that let the fruit's own concentration show through rather than layering flavor on top of it. The result is a wine built on structure and raw fruit density rather than oak-driven softness. Give it air and a slightly cooler serve, around 16 to 18°C, and that density opens up rather than overwhelms.
