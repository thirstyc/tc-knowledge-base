Why does Alsatian Riesling taste so fruity and floral?

Riesling is intrinsically aromatic - loaded with terpenes (linalool, geraniol) that read as citrus blossom, honeysuckle, and peach. Alsace amplifies this: a continental climate sheltered by the Vosges mountains gives long, dry, sunny autumns, so grapes hang late and build aromatic intensity without losing acidity, since nights stay cool. Diverse soils - granite, limestone, marl, sandstone - spread across countless small plots let producers match Riesling to sites suited to slow, even ripening. Winemaking stays deliberately hands-off: stainless steel, often native yeasts, no oak, no malolactic conversion. The goal is to bottle what the grape and site already offer, not layer on vanilla or buttery texture. Residual sugar, when present, is usually modest - a few grams per litre - balanced by racy acidity, so it reads as ripeness rather than sweetness. Serve around 10-12°C; too cold and the aromatics shut down. Contrary to its reputation, most Alsatian Riesling made today is properly dry - check the producer if precision matters, since labels rarely state sugar levels.
