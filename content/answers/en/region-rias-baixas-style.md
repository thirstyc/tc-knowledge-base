Why does Rías Baixas Albariño taste so briny and citrusy?

The vineyards sit right along Galicia's Atlantic coast, often within sight of the water, which means constant humidity, salt air, and a real risk of rot. Albariño evolved thick skins to cope with that dampness, and thick skins tend to carry more aromatic intensity and phenolic grip, which is part of where the texture comes from. The granite bedrock underneath drains quickly despite all the rain, so the vines aren't sitting in waterlogged soil, but they still struggle a bit, and that mild stress plus the cool climate keeps sugar accumulation slow and acidity high. That combination, cool damp air and lean granite soil, is what pushes the wine toward citrus, green apple, and that saline, almost oyster-shell minerality people associate with it. It's not actually salt in the wine, there's no mechanism for that, but the perception of brininess is real and consistent enough that it's become the grape's calling card. Serve it properly cold, 8-10°C, so that acidity stays sharp and refreshing rather than flabby.
