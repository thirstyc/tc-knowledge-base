Why is New Zealand Gewürztraminer hard to find?

Sauvignon Blanc dominates almost every vineyard decision in the country, and for good reason: it made New Zealand's name internationally and reliably sells. Gewürztraminer offers none of that certainty. The grape has thin skins, making it prone to rot in humid vintages, and it needs a light touch in the winery, since too much skin contact pulls out bitter phenolics that make the wine taste harsh rather than perfumed. Get the winemaking wrong and it goes from lychee and rose to soapy and bitter fast. Yields are also naturally low, and land in prime cool-climate sites is expensive and mostly already planted to Sauvignon Blanc or Pinot Noir, which sell more predictably. So it survives mostly as a passion project for growers willing to sacrifice yield and certainty for something more distinctive. That scarcity is exactly why the good examples are worth seeking out, they're made by people who care about the grape rather than the market.
