Why is Ripasso between wines?

Ripasso sits in the middle because of what literally happens to it: after Amarone finishes fermenting, the winery is left with leftover skins, the pomace, still loaded with sugar, color, tannin and glycerol from the dried grapes. Instead of tossing that pomace, producers add it back into a batch of already-fermented basic Valpolicella, which kicks off a second, smaller fermentation. The young wine soaks up richness, alcohol and body from those skins without ever going through the drying process itself. That's the "repassing" in the name. You get more weight and dried-fruit depth than everyday Valpolicella, but nowhere near the concentration or price of Amarone, which drove the whole drying process from scratch. It's a genuinely clever bit of recycling that turns a byproduct into a distinct style, not a marketing tier invented after the fact. Expect medium-plus body, dried cherry and a warm spice note, best around 16 to 18 degrees with something on the table, not sipped alone.
