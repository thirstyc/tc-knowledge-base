Why does Torrontés fly under the radar outside Argentina?

Malbec owns Argentina's international reputation so completely that its best native white barely gets mentioned. Malbec had a marketing head start: it arrived from France in the 1800s, found a perfect home in Mendoza's high-altitude vineyards, and by the 2000s had become shorthand for Argentine wine on export markets worldwide. Torrontés, by contrast, is genuinely Argentine, a natural grape cross that likely developed there rather than an import, grown mostly in smaller regions like Salta's Cafayate valley that don't have Mendoza's export infrastructure or brand recognition. It's also a harder sell logistically: Torrontés is aromatic and delicate, best drunk young and shipped carefully, while Malbec's dark fruit and oak treatment travel and age more forgivingly. There's also just less of it planted, so volumes for export are smaller. None of that reflects quality, a good Torrontés from a cool, high-elevation site is a distinctive, well-made wine, it's simply been squeezed out of the spotlight by its bigger, redder, more marketable sibling.
