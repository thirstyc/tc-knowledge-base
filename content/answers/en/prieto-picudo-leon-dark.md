Why does Prieto Picudo taste so León-dark?

The colour isn't marketing, it's genetics. Prieto Picudo is a native León grape with tiny, thick-skinned berries, which means an unusually high skin-to-juice ratio. Skins are where colour pigments (anthocyanins) and tannin live, so small thick-skinned berries pack a disproportionate punch of both compared to a plumper grape like Tempranillo. Add León's continental climate, high altitude and big swings between hot days and cold nights, and the vine holds onto acidity even as it piles on colour and tannin during ripening. Historically growers used a technique called sangrado, bleeding off some juice early to concentrate what's left and also to make rosado separately. What ends up in the deep reds is genuinely inky, structured wine, not a stylistic choice by the winemaker but a trait baked into the variety. Serve it around 16-17°C, not too cold, or the tannin turns harsh and the fruit shuts down. Give it air. This is not a sipping wine, it wants food.
