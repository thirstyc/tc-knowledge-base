Why do vintage years matter?

The vintage is the year the grapes were picked, and because weather changes every year, so does the wine. In some regions that difference is dramatic; in others it barely registers.

Weather shapes everything: a cool, wet summer gives lighter, higher-acid, sometimes underripe wines; a hot, dry one gives riper, fuller, higher-alcohol wines; rain at harvest dilutes the fruit; hail or frost can wipe out much of a crop.

Vintage matters most in marginal, cool climates like Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne, Germany and the Loire, where ripening is never guaranteed. It matters least in consistently warm regions like much of California, Chile, Australia and southern Spain.

It also affects how long a wine will keep. Structured vintages age longer; soft, ripe ones drink well earlier.

A caution about vintage charts: they average a whole region, and a good producer in a difficult year usually makes better wine than a weak one in a celebrated year. Producer first, vintage second.

Non-vintage wine, common in Champagne, blends several years deliberately to keep a house style consistent.

If you're buying to drink soon, vintage matters far less than most people think.
