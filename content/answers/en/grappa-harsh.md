Why is young Grappa so harsh?

Grappa is distilled from pomace — the skins, seeds, and stems left over after winemaking — and unlike brandy made from wine itself, it skips the aging that would normally round out the fusel alcohols and volatile compounds produced during fermentation and distillation. Young, unaged grappa (often called grappa bianca or giovane) goes straight from the still to the bottle, sometimes within months, so those sharp, hot, higher alcohols haven't had time to mellow or bind with wood tannins the way whisky or aged brandy would. Proof is typically high, often 40-50% ABV or more, which amplifies the burn. None of this makes it bad — it makes it intense, and it's meant to be. If the harshness bothers you, look for grappa invecchiata or riserva, aged in wood for a period that softens the edges considerably. Serve young grappa cold, around 8-10°C, which tames some of the heat; aged versions are better slightly warmer, closer to room temperature.
