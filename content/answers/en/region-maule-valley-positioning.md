Why are natural wine drinkers obsessed with Maule Valley?

Because it's the closest thing Chile has to living history that you can actually drink. Maule's Carignan vines were mostly planted decades ago, dry-farmed with no irrigation, some pushing past a century old, and for years they were considered a liability, low-yielding and unfashionable next to the polished Cabernet coming out of the Central Valley. The VIGNO project, a collective of producers who agreed to farm and vinify these old vines under strict rules, essentially rescued them from being pulled out and replanted with something more commercially convenient. That story is exactly what natural wine drinkers gravitate toward: unirrigated, low-intervention, site-driven fruit from vines that predate the country's export boom, farmed by people who chose preservation over yield. The wines themselves, gripping, savoury, a little wild, back up the narrative instead of just riding on it. It's Chile's counter-argument to the idea that its wine industry is only about scale and consistency.
