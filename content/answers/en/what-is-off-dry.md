What does "off-dry" mean?

Off-dry means a wine has a small amount of noticeable sweetness, somewhere between fully dry and properly sweet.

Sweetness comes from sugar left over when fermentation stops before the yeast has converted it all into alcohol. Winemakers can stop fermentation deliberately, by chilling the wine or filtering out the yeast, to keep a touch of sweetness.

As a rough guide:

- Dry: up to about 4 grams of sugar per litre, with no perceptible sweetness.
- Off-dry, or medium-dry: roughly 4 to 12 grams per litre, sometimes more.
- Medium-sweet and sweet: above that.

Acidity changes how sweet a wine tastes. A high-acid Riesling with 15 grams of sugar can taste only slightly sweet, while a low-acid wine with less sugar can seem sweeter.

Common off-dry wines:

- German Riesling Kabinett, or labelled "feinherb".
- Vouvray labelled "demi-sec" or "tendre".
- Many Alsace Gewürztraminer and Pinot Gris.
- Some rosés and New World Rieslings.
- Lambrusco labelled "amabile".

Off-dry wines are brilliant with spicy food, since a little sweetness tames chili heat. They also suit pork, salty cheese and dishes with a sweet element, like glazes and fruit sauces.
