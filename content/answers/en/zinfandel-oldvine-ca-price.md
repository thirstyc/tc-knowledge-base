Why is Old-Vine California Zinfandel expensive?

It comes down to how little fruit an old vine actually gives you. Once a Zinfandel vine passes 50 years, and some California plantings are past 100, its yield drops off a cliff -- the plant channels its limited energy into fewer, smaller berries instead of a big crop. Less water and sugar diluting the fruit means more concentrated flavor, color and structure in the glass, but it also means a grower is harvesting a fraction of what a younger vineyard produces off the same acreage. That math alone raises the cost per bottle. Add to that the fact that many of these vineyards are dry-farmed, hand-harvested because the gnarled old trunks don't suit mechanical pickers, and increasingly rare since replanting with higher-yield clones is more profitable short-term. Producers are essentially paying to keep low-yield heritage vines alive for their character rather than their volume, and that trade-off between quality and quantity is what you're paying for at checkout.
