Why is Arinto so mineral and crisp?

Arinto is a late-ripening Portuguese grape with an unusual habit: it holds onto malic acid well past the point where most grapes have burned through theirs as sugar accumulates. That's not a cool-vintage fluke, it's just how the grape is wired, which is why Arinto stays crisp even in a hot year. It's grown mainly around Lisbon, in Bucelas, and along the Atlantic coast where sea air caps daytime heat, and it also does quiet backbone duty in Vinho Verde blends. The mineral, almost saline edge comes from limestone and clay soils combined with winemaking that gets out of the way entirely, stainless steel, cool fermentation, zero oak, so nothing covers up the citrus and wet-stone character or dulls the naturally low pH. Serve it at 8 to 10°C and the tension is obvious immediately: this is a grape holding real acid at full ripeness, which is exactly the trick oak-aged, warmer-climate whites usually can't pull off.
