Why is Etna Sicily's most exciting wine story?

Twenty years ago Etna was a footnote, Sicily's reputation was built on cheap bulk wine from the hot flatlands. Then winemakers from Tuscany, Piedmont and beyond started buying up small parcels on the volcano's slopes, drawn by something the rest of the island couldn't offer: old, ungrafted vines, some over a century old, surviving because phylloxera struggles in volcanic sand. Vineyards climb to 900 meters and higher, splitting into distinct contrade, essentially a grand cru system, each with its own exposure and soil age depending on which historic lava flow it sits on. Add genuine altitude-driven acidity and a wine style, mostly Nerello Mascalese, that lands somewhere between Nebbiolo and Pinot Noir, and you get the kind of terroir story that attracts serious money and serious critics fast. Prices have climbed accordingly. It is the rare case of an island's most exciting wine coming from its most inhospitable-looking ground.
