Why does Lebanon matter in wine history?

Viticulture in the Bekaa Valley predates the Phoenicians, who then used their maritime trade networks to spread grapevines and winemaking know-how across the ancient Mediterranean, seeding vineyards in Greece, Sicily and North Africa from a base that included Lebanon's coast. Indigenous varieties like Obeideh and Merwah have grown there for centuries, later joined by Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah introduced during the French mandate period. What makes Lebanon matter now isn't just that lineage, it's Château Musar's refusal to stop producing through the 1975-1990 civil war, when grapes were moved past checkpoints and wines aged far longer in barrel than planned because bottling and export were simply impossible. That forced patience became a stylistic signature — oxidative, evolving, unmistakable. It's a rare, concrete demonstration that winemaking culture can survive conflict, embargo and isolation, and it's why a country producing a tiny fraction of global volume still gets serious attention from anyone paying attention to wine history rather than just wine shelves.
