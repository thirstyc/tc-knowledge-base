Why does Negroamaro taste so Puglian-dark?

Negroamaro literally translates to black-bitter, and that name is doing real descriptive work. It's a thick-skinned grape grown in the heel of Italy, Puglia, where the Mediterranean climate is hot and dry with long, even ripening seasons. Thick skins mean more anthocyanins and tannin extracted during fermentation, which is exactly why the wine comes out dark, dense and rustic rather than pale and delicate. The heat pushes sugars and phenolic ripeness hard, giving dark cherry, black plum and often a savory, earthy or slightly bitter finish that echoes the grape's name. It's the backbone of wines like Salice Salentino and shows up blended with Primitivo in some Puglian reds, usually to soften Negroamaro's grip with Primitivo's riper fruit. Serve it around 16-18°C and decant if it's young, since the tannins benefit from air. It's built for grilled meats, tomato-based sauces and hard cheeses — anything that can stand up to its rustic, slightly gritty character.
