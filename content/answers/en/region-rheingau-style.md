Why does Rheingau Riesling taste so structured compared to Mosel?

The difference comes down to shelter and sun. The Taunus hills sit behind Rheingau's vineyards and block cold air, while the slopes face the Rhine directly, giving the grapes a warmer, steadier ripening season than the narrow, shadier Mosel valley gets. That extra warmth pushes sugars and phenolic ripeness further before acid drops off, so the grapes come in riper and fuller-bodied. Soils differ too: more loess and clay over quartzite here, versus the blue and red slate that gives Mosel Riesling its wiry, mineral edge. Winemakers in Rheingau also lean toward fermenting fully dry rather than leaving residual sugar, so what you taste is ripe stone fruit and firm structure holding straight through the finish, not sweetness cushioning the acid. The acid is still there, still serious, but it's carrying more fruit weight and body around it, which is exactly why the wine can shoulder heartier food than a typical Mosel bottle ever could.
