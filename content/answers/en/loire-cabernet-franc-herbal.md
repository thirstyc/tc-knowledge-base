Why does Loire Valley Cabernet Franc taste so herbaceous?

That green pepper, tomato leaf, graphite note comes from methoxypyrazines, aromatic compounds that Cabernet Franc produces naturally and that break down as grapes ripen. In the Loire's cool climate, growing season warmth is marginal, so pyrazine levels stay higher at harvest than they would in the Médoc or Napa. This isn't a flaw to hide, it's the calling card of Chinon, Bourgueil and Saumur-Champigny. Warmer vintages soften it into something closer to violets and crushed herbs. The tuffeau limestone that underlies much of the region also plays a role, giving the wines a chalky, almost saline grip on the finish alongside the herbal lift. Bad or underripe examples read as raw bell pepper and thin tannin. Good ones balance the vegetal edge with red currant and a savoury structure that makes them genuinely food-friendly rather than just curious. Serve at 14 to 16 degrees, slightly cool, which sharpens the herbal notes without letting them dominate. Give it a decant if it's young and tight, the aromatics open up fast.
