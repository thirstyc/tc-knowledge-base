How do tannins age?

Over time, tannin molecules slowly link together into longer chains, which feel softer and smoother in the mouth. Some grow large enough to fall out of the wine as sediment.

Tannins come mainly from grape skins, seeds and stems, and from oak barrels. In a young red, they feel grippy and drying, like strong tea.

What happens as the wine ages:

- Polymerisation: small, astringent tannin molecules combine with each other and with colour pigments into larger ones, which taste less harsh.
- Sedimentation: the largest chains become insoluble and drop out, forming the deposit in old bottles.
- Colour change: as pigments bind with tannins and settle, reds shift from purple to ruby, then to brick and tawny.

The result is a smoother, rounder texture, and room for more complex aromas to show.

This happens slowly in the bottle, over years. Tiny amounts of oxygen through the cork play a part, which is why storage matters.

Not all tannic wines get better. The fruit, acidity and alcohol need to be in balance, or the fruit can fade before the tannins soften, leaving a dry, hollow wine.

Decanting gives young, tannic wines a gentler preview of this effect by exposing them to air.
