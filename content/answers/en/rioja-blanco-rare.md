Why is white Rioja rare?

White Rioja is rare because Rioja built its identity almost entirely on Tempranillo, and generations of growers planted red varieties because that's what the market and DOCa rules rewarded. Viura, also called Macabeo, is the traditional white grape, but only a sliver of Rioja's vineyard land is planted to it, and even less goes into wines bottled to show it off - most historically got blended away or used for cheap bulk wine. Modern white Rioja splits into two camps: a young, fresh, tank-fermented style meant to be drunk within a year or two, and a more traditional oxidative style aged in old oak barrels, sometimes for years, developing nutty, waxy, almost sherry-like notes from controlled oxygen exposure. Both styles are made in small quantities compared to the region's reds, so bottles rarely make it past specialist shops. Serve the fresh style cold, around 8-10°C, and the barrel-aged style a touch warmer, closer to 12°C, to let the oxidative character show. Worth seeking out - it's one of the more interesting things happening in Rioja and few people bother to look.
