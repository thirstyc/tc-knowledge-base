Why does Rioja taste so vanilla and red-fruited?

That vanilla note isn't an accident of terroir, it's a deliberate technique. Rioja has aged Tempranillo in American oak barrels since the 19th century, originally influenced by Bordeaux producers who came south fleeing phylloxera, and American oak brings a different chemistry than French oak, more overt vanillin and coconut character, softer tannin structure, and a rounder mouthfeel. Combine that with Tempranillo's naturally soft tannins and its tendency toward red fruit like cherry and strawberry rather than the darker fruit of thicker-skinned grapes, and you get the classic profile: red fruit turning toward dried fruit with age, a distinct vanilla sweetness from the barrel, and tannins that feel gentle rather than gripping. This is oak used as a stylistic signature, not a mistake to be corrected, and it's exactly what separates Rioja from most of the rest of Spain, where French oak and less oak contact are increasingly the norm. Serve around 16 to 18°C so the wine's softness doesn't collapse into flabbiness.
