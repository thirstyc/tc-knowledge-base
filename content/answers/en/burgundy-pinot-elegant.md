Why does Burgundy Pinot Noir taste so elegant?

It comes down to climate and restraint. Burgundy sits at the cool edge of where Pinot Noir ripens reliably, so the grapes accumulate flavour slowly while retaining sharp natural acidity -- that acidity is what reads as "elegant" rather than heavy, because it lifts the fruit and keeps the wine feeling light on the palate even when there's real concentration behind it. Pinot Noir itself is thin-skinned, so tannins are naturally soft, not gripping like Cabernet's. Winemakers lean into this: oak is usually neutral or lightly used, so it frames the fruit instead of masking it with vanilla and toast. The other factor is Burgundy's patchwork of limestone and clay soils, which shift subtly from one small parcel to the next -- this is why the same grape, grown a few hundred metres apart, can taste noticeably different. Serve it a touch cool, around 14-16C; too warm and the delicacy collapses into flatness. The result is a wine built on tension and detail rather than power, which is exactly what people mean when they call it elegant instead of just light.
