Why does Sauternes taste so rich and golden?

It's fungus, and it's on purpose. Botrytis cinerea, noble rot, needs a very specific weather pattern to work in your favour instead of just rotting the crop: morning mist off the Ciron river to get the mould going, then warm afternoon sun to dry things out before it turns to grey rot. The fungus perforates the grape skins and the water evaporates, leaving behind shrivelled berries with sugar, acid and flavour compounds concentrated far beyond normal ripeness. Semillon is the main grape here because its thin skin takes botrytis well. The result is a wine with real sugar, real acidity to balance it, and a texture that's almost oily from the glycerol botrytis produces. That's where the honey, apricot and marmalade notes come from, not from fruit alone. Serve it cold, around 8 to 10°C, cold enough to keep the sweetness from feeling heavy but not so cold you kill the aromatics.
