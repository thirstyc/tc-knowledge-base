Why does Lebanon matter?

During Lebanon's civil war, Serge Hochar kept making Château Musar by driving grapes across checkpoints to reach the cellar at Ghazir, sometimes with shelling within earshot. Shipping routes were cut for years, so bottles sat in barrel and bottle far longer than intended — not by design but by necessity — and that accidental extended aging became the house style: oxidative, savory, unpredictable, alive in a way that polished modern wine rarely is. The Bekaa Valley, where most of the fruit is grown, has been under vine for millennia, first for indigenous grapes like Obeideh and Merwah, later for Cabernet Sauvignon and Syrah brought in under the French mandate. None of that history is decorative. When a country is at war and someone still shows up to prune, pick and press, the wine that results carries the weight of that decision. It's not a marketing story. It's the actual reason Musar tastes like nothing else, and why Lebanese wine gets talked about with more reverence than its volume would otherwise justify.
