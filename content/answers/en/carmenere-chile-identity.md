Why Is Carménère Chile's Flagship?

For most of the 1800s, Carménère was a Bordeaux blending grape, planted alongside Cabernet and Merlot. Then phylloxera tore through European vineyards, and because Carménère ripens late and is finicky to grow, Bordeaux simply didn't replant it when rebuilding with grafted, phylloxera-resistant vines. It vanished from France almost entirely. Chile, isolated by the Andes and the Pacific, never got hit by phylloxera the same way, and for over a century growers there had Carménère growing in their vineyards misidentified as Merlot — the two look nearly identical in the vineyard. In the 1990s, DNA testing sorted out the confusion, and Chile suddenly owned a grape nobody else was really growing anymore. Rather than treat that as a footnote, the industry built an identity around it: a variety with genuine Bordeaux pedigree that only survived because of Chile's geography. That's the real story — less marketing trick, more a country inheriting an orphaned grape and finally giving it a home.
