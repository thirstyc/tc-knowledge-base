Why is Jumilla so affordable?

Jumilla sits on sandy, poor soils in a hot, dry pocket of southeastern Spain, conditions that happen to be nearly ideal for cheap, reliable wine production. The sand kept phylloxera out for a long time, so a lot of vines are ungrafted and old, which normally costs a fortune elsewhere but here just happened by accident of geography. The climate is hot and dry enough that mildew and rot pressure is low, so growers spend little on spraying or canopy management, and the region has long been organized around large cooperative wineries built for volume rather than boutique bottlings. Land is cheap, labor is cheap, and the sun does the ripening work every single year with almost no vintage risk, which keeps quality consistent even at low prices. What has changed recently is that people finally noticed those old, low-yielding Monastrell vines were capable of real depth, so the top end has improved without the bottom end getting more expensive. You are essentially benefiting from decades of infrastructure built for bulk wine now producing something better than it needs to.
