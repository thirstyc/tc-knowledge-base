Why does New Zealand Pinot Noir taste so ripe and structured?

New Zealand's Pinot regions—Central Otago, Martinborough, parts of Marlborough—sit at high latitude but get intense, clear sunlight and huge swings between day and night temperatures. That combination is the trick: the sun pushes sugar and phenolic ripeness hard during the day, while cold nights slow respiration and lock in acidity that would otherwise burn off. You end up with fruit that tastes properly ripe—cherry, plum, sometimes dark and jammy—without turning flabby. Structure comes from winemaking choices layered on top: increasing use of whole-cluster and stem inclusion during fermentation adds grip and a savoury, slightly stemmy backbone, while a good dose of new oak reinforces tannin and framework. The result reads riper and more muscular than Burgundy, but with a similar acid spine holding it together. Serve at 14–16°C, slightly cooler than room temperature—too warm and the ripe fruit tips into jammy and the alcohol shows.
