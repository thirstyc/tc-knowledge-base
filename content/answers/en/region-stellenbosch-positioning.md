Why is Stellenbosch called South Africa's Bordeaux?

The Bordeaux comparison isn't marketing fluff, it's geology and grape choice. Stellenbosch sits in a bowl of granite mountains that funnel cool air off False Bay, giving vines the diurnal temperature swing they need to ripen slowly and hold onto acidity, much like Bordeaux's maritime climate. The soils are decomposed granite and clay, weathered over millions of years into a base that drains well but holds enough moisture to stress vines just right. Add a settler history dating to the 1600s, when Cape Dutch farmers planted Cabernet Sauvignon, Cabernet Franc and Merlot in the same blending tradition, and you get a region built around structured, age-worthy reds rather than the jammy, high-alcohol style found in warmer Cape valleys. It's also where the country's oldest estates and most established producers are concentrated, so when people invoke Bordeaux they mean serious infrastructure and generational winemaking, not just similar wine. Drink the reds a few years after release, the tannins need it. Serve around 17°C.
