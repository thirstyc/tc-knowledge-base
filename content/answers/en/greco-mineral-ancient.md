Why does Greco taste so mineral and ancient?

Greco's minerality isn't marketing poetry, it comes from where it's grown: volcanic soils around Vesuvius and the hills of Campania, rich in ash, pumice and mineral salts left by centuries of eruptions. Vines planted in that kind of soil tend to produce grapes with naturally high acidity and low yields, which concentrates flavour and gives the wine that flinty, wet-stone edge instead of soft fruitiness. The grape itself is thought to have arrived with Greek settlers well over two thousand years ago, making it one of Italy's oldest continuously cultivated varieties, and the vineyards themselves are often old, low-yielding, and untrained to modern trellising, which adds textural depth. Serve Greco di Tufo or similar bottlings around 10 to 12 degrees, cool enough to keep the acidity crisp but warm enough that you still get the honeyed, smoky undertones. It's not a fruit-forward wine chasing freshness for its own sake, it's structured around soil and age, which is exactly why it holds up so well with rich, savoury food.
