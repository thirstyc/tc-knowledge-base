Why does Île d'Orléans matter to Quebec wine?

The island has been farmed since the 1600s, long before anyone thought grapevines could survive a Quebec winter, so seeing vineyards now sitting next to the orchards and market gardens is genuinely new territory. The grapes are cold-hardy hybrids, not classic vinifera, because nothing else reliably survives temperatures that regularly drop past minus 25°C. Production is small and boutique, more a handful of growers proving a point than an industry, but that's exactly why it matters: it's tangible proof that Quebec can grow something worth drinking a few minutes from Quebec City, not three thousand kilometres away. It's not competing with Burgundy or Napa on prestige, and it doesn't need to. The value here is historical continuity and local curiosity in the same glass, a working answer to whether this province can make real wine, delivered from a landscape most people only know from postcards.
