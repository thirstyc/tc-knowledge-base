Why is Viognier hard to find?

Viognier is a genuinely difficult grape to farm well. It buds early, which exposes it to spring frost, and it ripens in a narrow window where sugar and aromatics don't always arrive together. Pick too early and you get a flat, neutral wine with none of the apricot-and-honeysuckle character that makes Viognier worth planting in the first place; wait those extra few days for the aromatics to fully develop and the sugar can spike, pushing alcohol high and acid lower still. It's also a shy, low-yielding vine, which is part of why it nearly went extinct in the mid-20th century — at one point almost all of it left in the world was confined to Condrieu, on those steep granite slopes above the Rhône. Condrieu rebuilt the grape's reputation and its prices along with it, but plantings have since spread to the Languedoc, California, and elsewhere, where warmer, easier sites turn out simpler but genuinely enjoyable versions for a fraction of the cost. If you want to understand the fuss without the Condrieu price tag, those are worth hunting down.
