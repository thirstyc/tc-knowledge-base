Why does Moscato d'Asti taste so sweet and fizzy?

The sweetness and fizz both come from deliberately stopping fermentation before it finishes. Winemakers ferment Moscato Bianco grapes in a sealed pressurized tank, then chill and filter the wine while there's still unconverted sugar left and only partial alcohol, typically around 5-6%. That leftover sugar is the sweetness; the CO2 produced during that partial fermentation, trapped in the tank, is the fizz, which comes out as a soft, low-pressure mousse rather than a full Champagne-style sparkle. The Muscat grape itself is also unusually aromatic even before any of this happens, rich in terpenes that give it that grapey, floral, orange-blossom character other grapes don't have naturally. So you're getting a grape that already smells intensely aromatic, stopped mid-fermentation to lock in sugar and gentle bubbles. Serve it cold, 6-8°C, and drink the bottle within a day or two of opening since it's built for freshness, not aging.
