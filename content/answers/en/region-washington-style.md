Why does Washington Cabernet taste so structured?

The Columbia Valley is technically a desert, and that's the whole story. Long summer days at this northerly latitude mean the vines get more total sunlight hours than most Cabernet regions, which drives real ripeness and concentration in the fruit. But the nights drop hard and cold, since there's little cloud cover or humidity to hold heat in, and that swing preserves acidity while keeping tannins firm and well-defined rather than soft or jammy. It's the same Bordeaux grapes grown in Napa or Bordeaux itself, but the climate math is different: more extreme heat during the day, sharper cooling at night. The result is Cabernet with dense, dark fruit, real backbone, and tannins that need time or food to soften. Volcanic and windblown loess soils add another layer, giving the wines a mineral, almost dusty edge under the fruit. Serve it slightly below room temperature, around 16 to 18°C, and don't rush it open, this is a style that rewards a bit of air and a decade of patience if you have it.
