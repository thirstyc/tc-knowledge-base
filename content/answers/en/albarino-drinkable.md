Why is Albariño so drinkable?

Because it combines bright acidity, ripe peach and citrus fruit and moderate alcohol, so it tastes fresh and lively rather than heavy.

Albariño comes mainly from Rías Baixas in Galicia, Spain's rainy Atlantic corner, and from northern Portugal, where it's called Alvarinho. The cool, damp, sea-influenced climate keeps acidity high, while summer sun ripens the fruit enough to give generous peach, apricot and lemon flavours.

A few things make it especially easy to enjoy:

- Balance: juicy fruit and zesty acidity pull in opposite directions, so it never feels sour or flabby.
- A salty, sea-spray edge that makes you want another sip.
- Moderate alcohol, usually around 12–13%.
- Usually fermented in stainless steel, so the fruit stays clean.
- Thick skins that add a little aromatic intensity and texture.

It's also one of the most food-friendly whites around, especially with seafood. Octopus, grilled fish, oysters, prawns and ceviche are classic partners.

Most Albariño is best young, within two or three years. Some producers make richer versions aged on their lees, which gain texture and can age longer.

Serve it well chilled, at about 7–10°C.
