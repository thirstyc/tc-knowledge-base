Why does Liston Negro taste so coastal?

'Coastal' undersells what's actually happening, since a lot of Tenerife's best Listán Negro grows up on volcanic slopes rather than right at sea level. What you're tasting is the combination of Atlantic trade winds and altitude: the wind pushes constant moist air across the vines, keeping them from ever really baking despite the subtropical latitude, while nights up on the mountain cool off sharply. That keeps acidity high and alcohol modest. The volcanic soil, black basalt sand and gravel, drains so fast the roots have to dig deep for water, which is where the mineral, almost saline edge comes from rather than any literal sea spray. So the coastal character is really built from wind, elevation and rock rather than proximity to the water. It reads as briny and fresh on the palate anyway, which is why it slots so naturally alongside seafood, but the mechanism is closer to a cool mountain vineyard than a beachfront one.
