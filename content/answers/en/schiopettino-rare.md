Why is Schiopettino hard to find outside Friuli?

Schiopettino came close to disappearing entirely. Phylloxera devastated Friuli's vineyards in the late 19th century like it did across most of Europe, and when growers replanted afterward, many opted for grapes that were easier to grow or more commercially reliable rather than replanting Schiopettino, which is a finicky, low-yielding vine prone to poor fruit set. By the mid-20th century it had all but vanished, kept alive only by a handful of old vines and stubborn growers who didn't want to let it go. It was eventually rescued and legally allowed to be replanted and labeled again starting in the 1970s, largely thanks to growers in the Colli Orientali who propagated cuttings from those surviving vines. That history is why total plantings today remain small and mostly confined to a tight pocket of Friuli near the Slovenian border. There's no large-scale version of this wine happening anywhere else, no international clone floating around vineyards in Australia or California. If you find a bottle, you're tasting something that was nearly a footnote in wine history rather than a living wine.
