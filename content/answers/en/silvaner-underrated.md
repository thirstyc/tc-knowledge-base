Why does Silvaner fly under the radar outside Germany?

Silvaner's image problem is mostly bottle-related. It's Franconia's signature grape, traditionally packaged in the squat, round Bocksbeutel, which by the 1970s and 80s had become visually shorthand for cheap, sweetish German exports—even though the actual mass-produced plonk flooding foreign markets at the time was usually Müller-Thurgau or blends, not Silvaner. The association stuck anyway. Meanwhile, dry Franconian Silvaner was quietly staying serious: grown on limestone and marl soils, fermented dry, built around structure and site expression rather than fruit or sugar. It's closer in spirit to a savoury white Burgundy than to anything resembling Liebfraumilch. Outside Germany, though, few people bother to look past the bottle shape or the unfamiliar name, so it never built the international following that Riesling did. Locals know better—it's one of Franconia's most respected white wines, just one that's never bothered to market itself.
