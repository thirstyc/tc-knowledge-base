Why does Turkish Boğazkere taste so rustic and tannic?

Boğazkere grows on high Anatolian plateaus, often over 1,000 metres, where hot days and sharply cooler nights create a wide diurnal swing. That gap slows sugar accumulation while preserving acidity, so the grape can hang longer and build flavor without turning flabby. The catch is that intense high-altitude sunlight also thickens the grape skins as a defense mechanism, and thick skins mean more tannin and pigment once you ferment on them. Boğazkere is naturally one of the most tannic grapes grown anywhere, which is why it's traditionally blended with softer Öküzgözü to make something drinkable young. Left alone, it wants years in bottle or barrel to shed its rough edges. None of this is new technique — it's the same growing conditions that have shaped wine here since antiquity, in a region that predates most of what we call 'classic' winemaking. The rusticity isn't a flaw; it's the fingerprint of the terrain.
