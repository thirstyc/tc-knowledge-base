How does climate affect wine?

Climate decides how much sugar and acidity grapes have at harvest, which in turn sets the wine's alcohol, freshness and flavour.

In cool climates, grapes ripen slowly and keep more acidity, so wines are lighter, fresher and lower in alcohol, with green apple, citrus and red-fruit flavours and herbal notes. Think Chablis, Mosel Riesling and Champagne.

In warm climates, grapes ripen fully and build sugar, giving fuller, riper wines with higher alcohol and flavours of tropical fruit, black fruit and jam. Think Barossa Shiraz or Napa Cabernet.

The difference between day and night temperatures matters too. Big swings, common at altitude, let grapes ripen while keeping acidity, which is why high-altitude Argentine Malbec tastes fresh despite the sunshine.

Maritime climates, moderated by the sea, have mild, even seasons. Continental ones, inland, have hot summers and cold winters, and more variation between vintages.

Which is why vintage matters more in cool regions: in a marginal climate, one wet or cool summer changes everything. In consistently warm regions, vintages vary much less.

Warming is now changing this map, pushing some classic regions toward riper styles and making cooler places, like England and Tasmania, newly viable.
