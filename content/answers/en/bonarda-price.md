Why is Bonarda so affordable?

Bonarda (actually Douce Noir/Corbeau, not the Italian Bonarda) is Argentina's second most-planted red grape after Malbec, but almost nobody outside the country has heard of it, which tells you everything about its pricing. It's planted at huge scale across Mendoza and San Juan, often on high-yielding trellis systems rather than the low-yield, hand-tended vines reserved for Malbec's premium tier. More grapes per hectare means more juice, less concentration, and lower cost per bottle. Historically it was grown for bulk blending and everyday jug wine, and that infrastructure - big co-op wineries, minimal new oak, quick turnaround - never went away even as quality improved. There's no export mystique driving demand the way there is with Malbec, so producers price it for what it is: a soft, juicy, low-tannin red meant for Tuesday night, not for collectors. Serve it lightly chilled, around 14-16°C, with empanadas or a burger, and don't overthink it. The affordability isn't a compromise, it's the whole point.
