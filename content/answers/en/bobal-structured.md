Why does Bobal taste so structured and mineral?

That structure comes straight from the skins and the place. Bobal, grown mainly around Requena in inland Valencia, develops thick skins as protection against a hot, high-altitude, continental climate, and thick skins mean more tannin and phenolic material available during fermentation. Extended skin contact pulls that structure into the wine, giving it real grip rather than just fruit. The mineral character people pick up isn't some vague marketing word; it's tied to the limestone and clay soils common in the region, which tend to produce wines with a firmer, drier, stony edge rather than lush, purely fruity ones, alongside the cool nights that preserve acidity even after hot days. That acid-tannin combination is what reads as structured: the wine has a backbone rather than just softness. It also means young Bobal can taste austere or closed if served too warm; keep it around 16 to 18°C and pair it with food, and that structure turns into something that frames the wine instead of dominating it.
