Why does Sagrantino taste so powerful and tannic?

Sagrantino isn't just tannic for an Italian red, it's tannic for any red anywhere, with polyphenol levels that rank among the highest measured in wine grapes. The grape has unusually thick skins, an adaptation to the intense sun and wind of Montefalco in Umbria, and since tannin and colour compounds live in the skins, more skin means more of both. Traditional winemaking there involves extended maceration, leaving the juice in contact with skins and seeds for a long stretch, which extracts even more structure. The result is a wine that's deeply coloured, dense, and grips the palate with a dry, almost powdery intensity when young. This isn't a flaw, it's the point: Sagrantino was historically used partly as a passito (dried-grape) sweet wine precisely because its tannin and acid could stand up to that much residual sugar. The dry version needs years in bottle or serious food to show anything besides structure. Serve around 17-18°C, and give it air.
