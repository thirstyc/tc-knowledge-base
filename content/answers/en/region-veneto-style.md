Why does Veneto wine taste so different bottle to bottle?

The reason Veneto wine seems inconsistent is that it isn't really one wine region so much as three loosely connected ones sharing an administrative boundary. Soave, in the east, is built on Garganega and made in a straightforward, unoaked, bone-dry style meant to be drunk young and cold. Prosecco, made largely from Glera using the tank method, is fermented a second time in pressurized steel rather than in bottle, which locks in fresh fruit character and keeps costs down. Then there's Valpolicella, where Corvina is the backbone: fresh and light in basic Valpolicella, richer in Ripasso where it's refermented on Amarone's leftover skins, and finally transformed in Amarone itself, where grapes are dried for months before pressing, concentrating sugar and flavour into something dense and high-alcohol. So a bottle-to-bottle tasting swings from crisp still white to sweet-leaning sparkling to a raisinated 15%+ red — not inconsistency, just three distinct winemaking traditions filed under one regional name.
