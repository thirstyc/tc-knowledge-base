Why does Alsatian Riesling taste so fruity-aromatic?

Alsace sits in the rain shadow of the Vosges mountains, which gives it a longer, drier, sunnier growing season than most of cool-climate France, and that extra hang time lets Riesling build up terpenes, the aromatic compounds responsible for its signature notes of lime blossom, white flowers, and stone fruit. The region's granite, limestone and volcanic soils add further nuance depending on the vineyard. Here's the misconception worth correcting: Alsatian Riesling isn't necessarily sweet, most is fermented fully dry despite tasting intensely fruity and floral, which throws people expecting German-style residual sugar. That perceived sweetness is aromatic intensity, not sugar. Winemakers there tend to favor minimal intervention, native yeasts and neutral vessels, letting the fruit and terroir speak rather than masking it with oak. Serve it cool, around 10-12°C, with richer dishes than you'd expect, roast pork, choucroute, or Asian food with some spice, since the acidity and aromatics can stand up to flavor without needing sweetness to balance it.
