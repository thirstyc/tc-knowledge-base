Why does Washington Syrah taste so peppery and structured?

That black pepper note is a real compound, rotundone, and Washington's Columbia Valley makes a lot of it. The region sits at a northerly latitude but bakes in long summer daylight, then drops sharply at night once the sun goes down over the desert-like Yakima and Walla Walla areas. That diurnal swing keeps grapes photosynthesizing hard during the day while acids and aromatic precursors are preserved overnight instead of burning off. Rotundone actually builds up more in cooler nights and partially shaded fruit, which is why Washington Syrah, unlike its jammier, riper Australian cousins, keeps that cracked-pepper, olive, and bacon-fat edge alongside dark fruit. The volcanic and windblown loess soils add tannic grip too, so you get structure as well as spice. Serve it around 16-18°C, a touch cooler than a big Cabernet, so the pepper and violet notes stay lifted instead of turning hot and jammy. Give it a decant if it's young; the tannins need air to unclench.
