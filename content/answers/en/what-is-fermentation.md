What is fermentation in wine?

Fermentation is how grape juice becomes wine: yeast eats the sugar in the juice and produces alcohol, carbon dioxide and heat.

The equation is simple, but the choices around it define the wine.

Yeast is the first. Some winemakers use ambient yeasts already present on the grapes and in the cellar, which gives less predictable, often more complex results. Others add selected cultured yeast for a reliable ferment.

Temperature is the second. Cool fermentation, common for whites, preserves delicate fruit and floral aromas. Warmer fermentation, common for reds, extracts more colour, tannin and flavour from the skins.

Colour comes from contact, not the juice. Almost all grape juice is clear, so red wine gets its colour by fermenting with the skins, while white wine is usually pressed first.

Fermentation stops when the yeast runs out of sugar, giving a dry wine, or when it's halted deliberately by chilling, filtering or adding spirit, which leaves sweetness behind.

A ferment that stops early by accident is called stuck, and it's every winemaker's least favourite phone call.

Most wines then undergo a second, different process: malolactic conversion.
