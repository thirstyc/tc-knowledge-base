Can you age Madeira?

Yes, and it's not an exaggeration - bottles from the 19th century still drink well, which almost no other wine can claim. It's baked into how Madeira is made: it's deliberately heated (estufagem, for younger, cheaper bottlings) or aged slowly in warm lodges (canteiro, for the finer stuff) and exposed to oxygen on purpose. Most wines treat heat and oxidation as flaws to avoid; Madeira embraces both, which means it has already survived conditions that would destroy anything else. That's why an opened bottle can sit on your counter for months, even years, without spoiling - it's already been cooked and oxidized past the point of further damage. Older Madeira doesn't fade so much as concentrate: sugars caramelize further, acidity stays remarkably intact (this is one of the most acid-driven wines made), and you get layers of walnut, burnt orange peel, and dried fig. Vintage-dated bottlings, when declared, genuinely gain complexity and value over decades. Serve slightly cool, around 14-16°C, never at room temperature.
