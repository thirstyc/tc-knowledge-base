Why does Pinotage taste so dark and rustic?

Pinotage is a deliberate cross of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, made in South Africa in the 1920s to combine Pinot's character with Cinsault's heat tolerance. It didn't quite inherit Pinot's elegance. The grape has thick skins and naturally high tannin, which is where the rustic grip comes from, especially in wines that see extended skin contact or heavy oak. The banana and nail-polish note isn't imagination, it's isoamyl acetate, a fermentation ester that shows up more when fermentation runs hot or with certain yeast strains, common in cheaper, early-release styles. The smokiness is different, often reductive sulfur compounds or deliberate oak toasting, sometimes both. Better producers ferment cooler and use gentler extraction to tone down the banana and let the plum and tar underneath speak. If you hit an aggressively fruity, tannic bottle, that's the grape's default setting, not a flaw exactly, just Pinotage being Pinotage.
