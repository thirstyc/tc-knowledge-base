Why does Nielluccio taste so light and cherry-forward?

Nielluccio is Corsica's version of Sangiovese, and it behaves much like its Tuscan cousin: thin skins that don't yield much tannin or color, and high natural acidity that keeps the wine feeling light even when ripe. Corsica's granite and schist soils drain fast and don't hold much nutrient, so the vines produce small, concentrated berries rather than big tannic ones. The island's mistral-driven winds and maritime influence keep nights cool despite plenty of Mediterranean sun, which preserves acidity while sugars climb—so you get ripe red cherry and a touch of herbal, garrigue-driven aromatics without heaviness. Maceration times tend to be short and gentle, since there's little tannin to extract anyway, which is why the wine stays pale and juicy rather than structured. It's built for the table, not for aging decades. Serve slightly cool, around 14–16°C, and treat it the way you would a good Chianti—with tomato, herbs, or grilled meats, not heavy cream sauces.
