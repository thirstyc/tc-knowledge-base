Why is dry Furmint so mineral?

The minerality comes from where it's grown, not some vague marketing gesture. Tokaj's vineyards sit on volcanic bedrock — rhyolite and andesite tuff broken down into soils that are low in nutrients and high in trace minerals, which stresses the vines and concentrates flavour rather than diluting it into fruit. That stress shows up as a flinty, wet-stone character rather than jammy fruit. Furmint also happens to be a naturally high-acid grape: it ripens late, holds its malic acid through the growing season, and keeps its skins thick enough to resist rot until botrytis is actually wanted. The combination — lean, acid-driven fruit built on mineral-heavy soil — reads as sharp, saline, almost chalky on the palate, similar in register to a good Chablis or a dry Riesling from the Mosel. Serve it cold, around 10°C, and the mineral edge sharpens further; let it warm past 14°C and it starts tasting soft and generic, which defeats the point of buying it in the first place.
