Why does Schiava taste so light?

The lightness comes down to skin and altitude. Schiava has thin skins, so during fermentation there's very little tannin or pigment to pull out, which is why the wine sits pale in the glass and feels soft rather than gripping on the palate. It's grown high up in Alto Adige, where cool nights preserve acidity and keep alcohol modest, usually around 11 to 12 percent, so you get freshness without heaviness. There's no oak involved to add weight or spice, and it's typically bottled and released young rather than aged, so nothing in the process adds density. The result is closer in spirit to a good Beaujolais than to a serious Pinot Noir: bright red fruit, a bit of floral lift, an almond note on the finish, and enough acid to make it food-friendly. It's built to be poured slightly chilled and drunk without ceremony, which is exactly the point rather than a limitation.
