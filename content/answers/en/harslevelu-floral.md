Why does Hárslevelü taste so floral and sweet?

Hárslevelü literally means "linden leaf" in Hungarian, and that's not a coincidence — the wine genuinely smells of linden blossom, along with honey and orchard fruit like quince and apricot. It's grown mostly in Tokaj, usually alongside Furmint, in a spot where the Bodrog and Tisza rivers meet and throw up morning fog that burns off into sunny afternoons. That fog-then-sun cycle is exactly what botrytis cinerea, noble rot, needs to develop on the grape skins without turning into ordinary rot. Once botrytis takes hold it shrivels the grapes, concentrating sugar and glycerol and pushing the wine toward those honeyed, dried-apricot flavours people associate with sweet Hárslevelü. It's the backbone of Tokaji Aszú, though dry versions exist too and show more of the pure floral side without the sweetness. Serve the sweet styles cold, around 8 to 10°C, in small pours; dry Hárslevelü is happier a bit warmer, 10 to 12°C.
