What pairs with Bordeaux?

Bordeaux's tannin is the whole point of the pairing logic. Those firm, grippy compounds come from the skins and pips of Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, extended maceration, and often a stretch in oak barrels. Tannin binds to protein and fat in your mouth, which is why a wine that feels austere on its own turns silky next to a well-marbled ribeye or a leg of lamb with the fat still on. Aged steak works especially well because dry-aging concentrates savory, umami flavors that echo the wine's own development. Hard, aged cheeses like Comté or an aged cheddar do the same job through salt and fat rather than protein alone. What to avoid: delicate white fish, anything heavily spiced, or dishes built on fresh acidity like a vinaigrette-dressed salad, all of which make the tannin taste harsher and more metallic. Serve at 16-18°C, properly decanted if it's young or still tightly wound. Too cold and the tannin turns aggressive; too warm and the alcohol swamps the fruit.
