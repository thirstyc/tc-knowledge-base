Why does Chianti Classico taste more serious?

It comes down to a zone and a set of rules that force patience. Chianti Classico is the historic core between Florence and Siena, hillier and cooler than the wider Chianti area, with poorer soils that make vines work harder — and a vine under a bit of stress concentrates flavour rather than diluting it into big, easy yields. The regulations do the rest: at least 80% Sangiovese, no white grapes at all, where they were once permitted and reliably thinned the wine out, and mandatory ageing before release — a year minimum, two for Riserva, thirty months for Gran Selezione, which must also come from estate-grown fruit only. What lands in the glass has more grip, firmer tannin and real length, sour cherry fruit deepening into dried fig, leather and tobacco with a savoury edge underneath. It also has somewhere to go with age, which basic Chianti generally doesn't; a good Riserva at ten years is a genuinely different wine than it was on release. Same grape and region as everyday Chianti, just held to a much higher standard of discipline.
