Why does rosé taste so different bottle to bottle?

Color in rosé comes almost entirely from how long crushed red grape skins sit in contact with the juice before pressing, not from blending white and red wine, which is illegal in most of Europe outside Champagne. A few hours of contact, sometimes just overnight, pulls minimal pigment and tannin, giving the pale, bone-dry, almost onion-skin pink typical of Provence. Longer maceration, a day or more, extracts more anthocyanins and phenolics, producing darker, fuller, sometimes fruitier wines with more texture. Some producers also make rosé by saignée, bleeding off pink juice early in a red wine fermentation to concentrate what's left - a byproduct method that tends to make heavier, more tannic rosé. None of this tells you anything about sweetness: color and residual sugar are unrelated, so a pale rosé can be bone-dry and a deep pink one can also be dry, or vice versa. Check the label, not the hue, if sweetness matters to you. Serve rosé cold, around 8-10°C, to keep it crisp and hide any hint of flabbiness.
