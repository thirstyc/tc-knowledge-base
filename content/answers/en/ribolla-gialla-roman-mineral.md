Why does Ribolla Gialla taste so mineral-driven?

The mineral character comes largely from where it's grown: Collio and the Colli Orientali sit on flysch, a mix of marl and sandstone locals call ponca, and that soil structure drains well while feeding the vine in a way that shows up as a saline, stony edge in the glass. Ribolla Gialla also carries naturally high acidity, which sharpens perception of that minerality and keeps the citrus notes, think lemon pith more than sweet orange, feeling taut rather than soft. Many producers extend skin contact, sometimes for days or weeks, which pulls extra texture and phenolic grip from the skins and reinforces that stony, almost tannic sensation on the finish, a technique the region's orange-wine producers have leaned into hard. The Roman connection is real too; the grape has documented history in the area going back that far, though the wines being made from it today, especially the skin-contact styles, are a distinctly modern reinvention of a genuinely old variety.
