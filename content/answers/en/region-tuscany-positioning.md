Why is Tuscany Italy's most famous wine region?

Tuscany runs three distinct reputations off largely the same grape. Chianti is Sangiovese made for everyday drinking, historically the wine in the straw-wrapped fiasco bottle, though modern Chianti Classico has shed that image for something more serious. Brunello di Montalcino takes a specific Sangiovese clone and ages it far longer, producing something structured and built to cellar for a decade or more. Super Tuscans came out of the 1970s, when producers frustrated by rigid regional rules started blending Sangiovese with Bordeaux varieties like Cabernet and aging them in small French oak barrels — technically breaking the local regulations, which is why many were initially bottled as basic table wine despite costing more than the region's classified reds. That rebellious streak, layered onto centuries of tradition, is what makes Tuscany the name even casual drinkers already recognize: it's simultaneously the old guard and the wine world's most famous act of local dissent.
