Why does Liston Negro taste so fresh-spicy?

The freshness comes from where the vines actually sit, not just proximity to the ocean. On Tenerife, Listán Negro is often planted on steep volcanic slopes up to 1000-1500 metres, where nights cool off hard even though the latitude is subtropical. That day-night swing keeps malic acid intact in the grapes instead of letting it burn off in the heat, so you get real bite rather than something soft and jammy. The volcanic soil itself, black ash and gravel called picón, drains fast and reflects heat back at the vine, forcing it to work for every drop of water, which concentrates flavour without piling on sugar or alcohol. The spice is the grape's own signature, a black pepper, herbal note that shows up wherever it's grown, not unlike what you'd find in a cool-climate Syrah. Serve it around 13-14°C, slightly chilled like a Beaujolais, and that peppery lift stays sharp instead of getting muddled by warmth.
