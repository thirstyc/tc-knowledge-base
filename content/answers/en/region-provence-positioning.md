Why did Provence rosé become the industry standard?

Rosé used to mean cheap, sweet, and slightly embarrassing, made by leaving red grapes on the skins just long enough to blush and adding sugar to smooth over the rest. Provence flipped that by fermenting fully dry and using direct press, pressing red grapes almost immediately so barely any color or tannin transfers, rather than the saignée method that bleeds juice off a red fermentation as an afterthought. The result was pale, crisp, and taken seriously as a wine in its own right rather than a byproduct. That pale color became shorthand for quality, and producers everywhere started chasing the look even when their methods didn't match it. What began as a regional style became the global template: pale equals premium, at least in perception. Provence didn't just make good rosé, it convinced an entire market that rosé could be a first choice instead of a summer afterthought, and that shift built a category worth billions.
