Why does Pinotage get dismissed so easily?

Pinotage is a 1925 South African cross of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, and for decades it earned a genuinely bad reputation for smelling like burnt rubber and nail polish remover. That wasn't paranoia. Hot fermentations and grapes picked too ripe pushed volatile compounds into the wine that read, unmistakably, as acetone and scorched tire. Add heavy oak on top of that and you had something people rightly avoided. What's changed is technique, not marketing. Growers pick earlier for freshness instead of maximum ripeness, ferments run cooler and slower, and oak is used with more restraint. The result is a wine with juicy bramble and plum fruit, a smoky, earthy edge that's now a signature rather than a flaw, and tannin structure that can actually handle braised meat or a smoky barbecue. The old reputation persists mostly because casual drinkers remember the grape, not the vintage. Try a modern bottling from a serious producer served around 16°C and you're tasting a different wine than the one that gave it a bad name.
