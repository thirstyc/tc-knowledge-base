Why does Cencibel taste so ripe and jammy?

Cencibel isn't actually a different grape — it's the local name for Tempranillo as grown in La Mancha and Valdepeñas, the hot, high, flat plateau of central Spain. That climate is the whole story here: long, intense sun exposure and big daytime heat push the grapes to very high ripeness levels before acidity has a chance to hold them back, so sugars climb, which translates directly into higher alcohol during fermentation. The fruit itself tastes cooked-down and concentrated, more stewed plum and fig than the fresh red cherry you'd get from Tempranillo grown somewhere cooler like Ribera del Duero. Tannins tend to be soft and alcohol noticeably warming on the finish. It's an honest, generous style of wine rather than a refined one, and it's priced accordingly. Serve it a touch cooler than room temperature, around 16 to 17°C, to keep that alcohol heat from dominating the glass.
