Why does Baga taste so powerful and tannic?

Baga grows in Bairrada, a damp, Atlantic-influenced pocket of Portugal, and the grape has essentially armoured itself against the humidity — thick skins, small berries, tight clusters that resist rot. Since tannin and pigment live in the skin, not the juice, a high skin-to-juice ratio means more of both ending up in the glass. Traditionally the grapes were fermented whole-cluster or barely destemmed and trodden by foot in stone lagares with extended skin contact, which drags out even more structure. On top of that, Baga keeps its acidity even at full ripeness, so you get grip and sharpness together instead of just mass. Old-school young Baga could be borderline punishing, which is why it built a reputation for needing a decade in bottle before the tannins knit together. Modern producers destem more and extract gentler, so current bottlings drink younger and softer. Either way, don't treat it as a sipping wine — it wants fat, protein, and ideally some cellar time to stop being a fight and start being a conversation.
