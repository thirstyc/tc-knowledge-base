Why does Baga taste so powerful and tannic?

Baga grows in Bairrada, a damp, Atlantic-influenced pocket of Portugal, and it's built like it's expecting rain — thick skins, small berries, tight clusters. Thick skin means more tannin and pigment relative to juice, since that's where both live. Traditionally the grapes were fermented whole-cluster or barely destemmed and trodden in stone lagares with extended skin contact, which pulls even more tannin and structure out of those skins. On top of that, Baga holds onto acidity even when fully ripe, so you get a wine that's gripping and sharp at the same time rather than just chewy. Historically this made young Baga borderline punishing, which is why it earned a reputation for needing years in bottle to unwind. Modern producers have eased off, destemming more and softening extraction, so newer bottlings are more approachable young. Either way, it's not a sipping wine — it wants fat, protein and time.
