Why does Uruguayan Tannat taste so much softer than French Tannat?

French Tannat, grown around Madiran, comes from a hot, dry, continental patch of southwest France where the grape ripens with brutal tannin and needs years — or heavy oak, or blending with softer grapes — just to become drinkable. Uruguay's climate is a different animal: the Atlantic and the Rio de la Plata estuary keep temperatures moderate and humidity higher, which slows sugar accumulation and lets tannins ripen more gently rather than concentrating into something aggressive. The result is a Tannat with dark fruit, an earthy, iron-like minerality, and real structure, but tannins that are already reasonably polished in youth rather than requiring a decade in a cellar. It's still a serious, food-driven wine — don't expect softness on the level of a Merlot — but it's approachable within a few years of the vintage rather than demanding a long wait. Give it a decant and serve around 16 to 18 degrees; too cold and the tannin turns hard again.
