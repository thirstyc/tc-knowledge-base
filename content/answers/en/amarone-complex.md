Why does Amarone taste so dark and complex?

Amarone starts as a Veneto blend built around Corvina, along with Rondinella and often Molinara, but the real transformation happens after harvest, not in the vineyard. The grapes are laid out on straw mats or in shallow crates for three to four months, in ventilated rooms called fruttai, where they slowly lose water weight, sometimes 30 to 40 percent of it. That concentrates sugars, acids and flavor compounds all at once, and the slow, semi-oxidative drying pushes the fruit character from fresh cherry toward raisin, fig, dried plum, even a chocolate or tobacco edge. Fermentation of that shriveled, sugar-dense fruit pushes alcohol up to around 15 to 16%, and the tannins, already firm in Corvina, get softened by the process rather than sharpened. The result is a wine with far more layers than a normal red table wine, dark fruit stacked on dried fruit stacked on savory, oxidative notes, which is exactly why it tastes so dense and complex.
