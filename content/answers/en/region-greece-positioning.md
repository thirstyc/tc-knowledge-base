Why is Greece finally getting taken seriously for wine?

Retsina, the pine-resin-flavoured wine that dominated Greek exports for decades, gave the whole country a reputation for cheap, tourist-taverna plonk, and it overshadowed the fact that Greece has some of the oldest continuously cultivated vineyards in Europe. Santorini in particular never got hit by phylloxera, the root louse that devastated vineyards worldwide in the 1800s, because its volcanic soil is too sandy for the pest to survive in. That means some Assyrtiko vines are genuinely old and ungrafted, still growing on their original rootstock, which is rare almost everywhere else on earth. Add Agiorgitiko's soft structure in the south and Xinomavro's Nebbiolo-like tannin and acid in the north, and you have indigenous grapes with real terroir expression rather than international varieties planted for a market. What changed is that producers stopped chasing cheap bulk exports and started bottling single-vineyard, terroir-driven wine, and critics finally tasted past the retsina stereotype to what was underneath it all along.
