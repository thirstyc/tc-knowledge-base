Why does Vinho Verde taste so light and slightly fizzy?

Minho, in Portugal's far northwest, is cool and wet — heavy Atlantic rainfall, granite soils, and a climate that struggles to fully ripen grapes even by September. Producers lean into that instead of fighting it: grapes are picked early, before sugars build and acidity drops, which is exactly what keeps alcohol low (often under 11%) and acid levels high. That's the backbone of the wine's freshness — it's a direct product of a marginal, rain-soaked climate, not a stylistic accident. The light spritz comes from carbon dioxide, either a small amount of trapped fermentation gas retained through early bottling or a light injection to reinforce it, a technique that mimics what used to happen naturally when the wine was bottled young and still slightly active. None of this is built for the cellar — it's designed to be bottled fast and drunk within a year or two, while that acid and fizz are still lively. Serve it at 8-10°C; any warmer and the freshness that defines the wine just falls flat.
