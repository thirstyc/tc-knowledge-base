Why is Primitivo so high-alcohol?

Primitivo is genetically the same grape as Zinfandel and Croatia's Crljenak Kaštelanski, and it shares their tendency to ripen unevenly and keep building sugar even after the flavours are technically ready. Growers in Puglia, working in a hot southern Italian climate, often wait for that full ripeness because underripe Primitivo tastes green and hollow. The longer it hangs, the more sugar accumulates in the berries, and since fermentation converts sugar into alcohol, that translates directly into bottles landing at 14-16%. Old, low-yielding vines concentrate this further, less juice per vine means more concentrated sugar and flavour in what's there. This isn't alcohol for its own sake, it's the tradeoff of getting ripe tannin and full flavour development in a hot climate. The catch is that high alcohol reads as heat and sweetness on the palate, so it needs to be balanced at the table rather than sipped alone. Serve it a touch cooler than typical reds, around 16°C, to keep that heat in check.
