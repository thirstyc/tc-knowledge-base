Why does Timorasso taste so mineral and crisp?

Timorasso is grown in the Colli Tortonesi, in hilly Piedmont on soils that are heavy in limestone and clay marl, and it is a genetically late-ripening grape with thick skin, so it hangs on the vine well into autumn. That long, slow ripening lets sugars build without acids dropping the way they would in a grape that ripens fast in the heat. The limestone underneath does not literally taste like rock in the glass, but it drains well and stresses the vine just enough to concentrate flavour rather than dilute it. The result is a white with real cutting acidity, a saline, almost chalky texture, and enough phenolic grip to age for a decade, developing honeyed, petrol-like notes not unlike a good Riesling. That aging capacity is the real tell that this is a serious grape, not a simple crisp white. Serve it around 10-12°C, and if you have an older bottle, do not fear the colour going gold, that is the grape doing exactly what it is built to do.
