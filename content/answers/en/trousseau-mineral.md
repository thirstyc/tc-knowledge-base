Why does Trousseau taste so mineral and tense?

Trousseau grows in the Jura on clay-limestone and marl soils that don't offer much in the way of nutrients, forcing the vine to work harder and yielding thin-skinned, high-acid grapes even at full ripeness. That acidity is the real source of the tension you're tasting, not some vague terroir magic, it's a grape that simply doesn't accumulate sugar and drop acid the way, say, Grenache does. The so-called minerality is partly perception: high acid plus low tannin plus pale colour reads as 'stony' on the palate because there's nothing else filling in the gaps, no fat, no sweetness, no big tannic grip. It's a light-bodied red, often served slightly cool at 13-14°C, which sharpens that sensation further. Don't expect fruit-forward or plush here; Trousseau is closer in spirit to a savoury, saline Pinot Noir than to anything jammy, and it rewards patience over immediate seduction.
