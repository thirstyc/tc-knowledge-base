Why is Salta considered extreme even for Argentina?

Mendoza gets the attention, but Salta's Calchaquí Valley vineyards climb past 1,700 meters, among the highest commercial vineyard sites anywhere. At that altitude the atmosphere is thinner, so UV exposure is brutal - vines respond by thickening skins and packing in aromatic compounds, which is part of why Torrontés from here smells like it's been dipped in a flower shop. The desert climate adds another layer: bone-dry air, intense sun during the day, then a hard drop in temperature once the sun goes down. That swing keeps acidity high even as sugars ripen, so the wines stay bright and structured instead of flabby. It's not a marketing line, it's basic vine physiology under stress that few other wine regions experience. Add sandy, rocky, low-fertility soils and you get naturally low yields, concentrated further by everything above. The result is genuinely singular: a grape that in cooler, lower-altitude climates can taste blowsy and forgettable turns into something taut and vivid up here. Once you've tasted Torrontés grown at this altitude, you understand why nobody in Salta feels the need to apologize for it.
