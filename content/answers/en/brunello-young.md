Can you drink young Brunello?

Technically you can, but you probably shouldn't enjoy it much. Italian law already forces a minimum five years of aging before release, two of them in large oak casks, specifically because Brunello's tannin and acid are too aggressive to be pleasant on their own straight out of the barrel. Even after that legal minimum, a lot of Brunello is still tightly wound when it hits the shelf, especially from a good vintage, and benefits from another five to ten years in the cellar before the tannins fully resolve into something silkier. If you do open one young, decant it for a couple of hours and serve it slightly cooler, around 16 degrees, to keep the fruit from getting buried under the grip. Pair it with something fatty and rich, that helps mask the roughness. But if you have the patience, holding the bottle another decade will show you a completely different, far more rewarding wine than the one you'd taste today.
