Why is Alsatian Riesling underrated?

Alsace runs one of France's most rigorous vineyard classifications - over four dozen Grands Crus, each tied to a specific soil and exposure, mapped with the same seriousness as Burgundy's climats. Yet the region gets a fraction of the attention, partly because it sits against the German border and its history of shifting between French and German rule left it culturally ambiguous to outsiders. It's also the only major French region that labels by grape variety instead of appellation alone, which confuses buyers trained to hunt for a village name. Add lingering market bias against Riesling as a category - baggage from decades of cheap, sweet German exports - and you get wines of genuine, site-specific complexity selling for far less than comparable Burgundy or Northern Rhône. Structurally these wines can age for decades, developing petrol, honey, and dried-fruit notes while keeping their acid spine intact. If you want serious terroir wine without the price tag that usually comes with it, this is one of the last corners of France where you'll still find it.
