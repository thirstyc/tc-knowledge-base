Why does Tokaji taste so sweet and complex?

Botrytis cinerea, the same noble rot that shrivels Sauternes grapes, attacks Furmint and Hárslevelű in the fog-prone valleys around Tokaj. As the fungus punches through the skins, water evaporates and sugars, acids and glycerol concentrate dramatically. Those shriveled, raisined berries (called aszú) get picked separately and macerated into a base wine, sometimes for days, extracting intense sweetness and a honeyed, apricot-and-saffron flavor. Tokaji also spends time oxidatively in old barrels in Tokaj's mold-lined cellars, which adds nutty, sherry-like complexity without turning the wine into vinegar, because the sugar and acid preserve it. What makes Tokaji special isn't just the sweetness, it's that searing acidity running underneath, which keeps it from tasting cloying. Serve it cool, around 10-12°C, in a small glass alongside foie gras, blue cheese, or on its own as dessert. A misconception worth killing: not all Tokaji is sweet, dry versions exist too, but the aszú style is the one that made the region's name.
