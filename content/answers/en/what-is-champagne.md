What is Champagne?

Champagne is sparkling wine from the Champagne region in northern France, made by a specific method, and the name is protected: sparkling wine made anywhere else can't be called Champagne.

It's made mostly from three grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. The region is cool, which keeps acidity high, the backbone of any good sparkling wine.

The method matters as much as the place. After the first fermentation, a little yeast and sugar are added and the wine is sealed in the bottle for a second fermentation, which traps carbon dioxide. The wine then rests on the spent yeast, which gives Champagne its toast, brioche and hazelnut character. Non-vintage bottles must age at least 15 months; vintage ones at least three years, and often far longer.

Most Champagne is a blend of grapes, vineyards and years, made to a house style. Vintage Champagne comes from a single good year, and grower Champagne is made by the same people who farm the grapes.

Sweetness is on the label: Brut is dry, Extra Brut and Brut Nature are drier still.

Serve it cold, but not freezing, and in a normal wine glass rather than a narrow flute if you want to smell it properly.
