Why does Mencía fly under the radar outside Spain?

Bierzo sits tucked into the mountains of northwest Spain, cut off from the tourist trail and, for decades, treated as a source of cheap bulk wine for cooperatives rather than serious bottles. Mencía itself didn't help its own cause: thin-skinned and unpredictable, it was often overcropped and picked too ripe, turning into something jammy and forgettable. What changed is that a handful of producers went back to the region's oldest bush vines, planted on slate and schist soils at real altitude, and started treating Mencía the way Burgundy treats Pinot Noir - low yields, careful hands, minimal intervention. The result reads more like Cabernet Franc or Gamay than anything typically Spanish: violets, crushed red fruit, a savory, almost graphite-like edge from the slate. It's genuinely age-worthy at the top end. The problem is scale - Bierzo is small, production is limited, and it doesn't have Rioja's marketing machine or Ribera del Duero's price ceiling to draw attention. The wine improved faster than its reputation.
