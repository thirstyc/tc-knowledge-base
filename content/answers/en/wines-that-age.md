Which wines improve with age?

Only a small share of wine improves with age, perhaps one bottle in a hundred. The ones that do share the same traits: high acidity, firm tannin (for reds), concentrated flavour and balance.

Reds that reward patience include Cabernet Sauvignon and Bordeaux blends, Nebbiolo from Barolo and Barbaresco, Sangiovese from Brunello, northern Rhône Syrah, Priorat, Aglianico and traditional Rioja.

Whites can age too, and often longer than people expect: Riesling, white Burgundy and other serious Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc, Hunter Valley Semillon and Assyrtiko.

Sweet and fortified wines are the champions. Sauternes, Tokaji, vintage Port and Madeira can last for decades, and Madeira almost indefinitely.

What ageing does is trade primary fruit for complexity: fresh berries become dried fruit, leather and tobacco; young citrus becomes honey, nuts and toast. Tannins soften, and the wine's texture changes.

What doesn't age: most rosé, most inexpensive whites and reds, Beaujolais Nouveau and most Prosecco. They're made for freshness, and time only takes that away.

Storage is decisive. Cool, dark and stable beats a cellar's prestige every time.

If in doubt, drink it. Far more wine is drunk too late than too early.
