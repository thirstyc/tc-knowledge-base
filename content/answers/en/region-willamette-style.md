Why does Willamette Valley Pinot taste so earthy and tart?

The earthiness and tartness both trace back to temperature, not tradition. Cool Pacific air pushes into the valley through gaps in the Coast Range, keeping nights cold even when days warm up. That diurnal swing slows sugar accumulation and preserves malic acid, so the grapes ripen in flavor before they ripen in sweetness — you get real Pinot character without a boozy finish. Thin skins from that cooler cycle mean lighter tannin but more savory, forest-floor, mushroom and dried-herb notes rather than straight fruit, and the volcanic Jory soil underneath adds a mineral, almost bloody edge some tasters read as earthy. None of this is winemaker trickery; it's what happens when you don't force ripeness. People call it Burgundy's American cousin because the mechanism is the same: marginal climate plus patience equals tension instead of jam. Serve it at 14 to 16°C, slightly cooler than a big red — too warm and the acidity and earth notes get muddled by alcohol.
