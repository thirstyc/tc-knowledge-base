Why does California Sangiovese taste so different from the Chianti I know?

Sangiovese is a fussy grape that wears its home address on its sleeve, and California just isn't Tuscany. In Chianti it grows on skinny, calcareous clay soils at elevation, with cool nights that keep acidity high and tannins wiry. Transplant it to warmer California sites, often deeper alluvial soil with better water access, and the vine happily pumps out riper, softer fruit. You lose some of that savoury, sour-cherry, dusty-tannin backbone and gain darker fruit, plusher texture, sometimes higher alcohol. Winemakers also tend to treat it with more new oak here, which was never really Sangiovese's best friend even in Italy. None of this makes it worse, just a different animal wearing the same name. Think of it as a cover song, not a bad translation. If you're chasing that classic Chianti tension, you'll likely be disappointed. If you're open to a rounder, fruit-forward red with soft edges, it can be genuinely enjoyable on its own terms. Serve around 16-18°C, same as its Italian cousin.
