Why is Italian Malvasia Bianca hard to find?

Malvasia isn't one grape, it's a scattered family of related varieties that Italian growers have been shuffling and renaming for centuries, often without much agreement on which is which. Malvasia Bianca is just one branch of that tree, planted in small, specific pockets rather than anywhere at scale. Unlike Chardonnay or Pinot Grigio, there was never a coordinated push to plant it widely or brand it for export, so most of what exists stays local: sold in nearby markets, poured in regional trattorias, rarely making it onto a container ship. Yields tend to be modest too, since a lot of these old plantings are on hillside sites farmed by small producers rather than large cooperatives built for volume. So when you do find a bottle outside Italy, treat it as a genuine curiosity rather than an oversight in your wine shop's buying. Serve it cool, around 10 to 12°C, and don't expect the next bottle to taste quite the same, since the grape itself shifts personality from one hillside to the next.
