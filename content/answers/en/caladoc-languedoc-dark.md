Why does Caladoc taste so dark-full?

Here's the thing people get wrong: Caladoc isn't a blend, it's a single grape variety. French researchers crossed Grenache with Malbec (Côt) back in 1958, aiming to keep Grenache's warm, generous fruit while fixing its two big flaws: pale color and a tendency to drop flowers before they set fruit (coulure), which tanks yields. Malbec brought the pigment and structure. The result ripens reliably in the Languedoc's heat and packs in anthocyanins, the compounds responsible for both color and a good chunk of tannin, which is why it pours almost opaque and tastes dense with blackberry, plum, and baked fig. Alcohol tends to run high too, since it's picked ripe in a hot climate, and that adds to the impression of weight and darkness on the palate. It's rarely bottled alone, more often anchoring a blend, but where you find it as the main act, expect something plush and full rather than sharp or angular. Serve it around 16-17°C; too cold and the fruit shuts down.
