Why does Mavrud fly under the radar outside Bulgaria?

Bulgaria's wine industry is still climbing back from a Soviet-era focus on bulk production, when quantity mattered more than character and native grapes like Mavrud got shuffled aside in favor of easy, high-yield plantings. That history left a reputation gap that's taken decades to close, and outside a small circle of wine geeks, most people simply never had a reason to look for Bulgarian wine on a shelf. Add to that Mavrud's genuinely difficult nature - it's a late-ripening, thick-skinned grape that's fussy to grow and needs real skill to tame its tannin - and you get a wine that's easy to make badly and much harder to make well, which slowed its reputation even at home. But the raw material has always been there: dense fruit, real structure, a distinct sense of place. As a new generation of Bulgarian producers invests in better viticulture and winemaking, Mavrud is finally starting to get noticed by importers and sommeliers hunting for something that isn't just another Cabernet. It's a grape whose moment is arguably just beginning.
