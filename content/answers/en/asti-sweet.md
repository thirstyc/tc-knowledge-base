Why does Asti taste so sweet and sparkling?

Both qualities trace back to how fermentation is handled with the Moscato Bianco grape. Moscato is intensely aromatic on its own, full of terpenes that give off peach, orange blossom, and honeysuckle notes even before fermentation starts. To make Asti, winemakers stop fermentation early, usually by chilling the must and filtering out the yeast, before all the natural grape sugar converts to alcohol. That leaves noticeable residual sugar and keeps alcohol low, typically around 5-7%. The fizz comes from the Charmat, or tank, method: the wine finishes its fermentation in a sealed pressurized tank, trapping the CO2 that's produced as a byproduct, which gives you that soft, frothy sparkle rather than the fine, persistent bead you'd get from bottle fermentation. The whole process is designed to preserve freshness and aroma rather than build complexity through aging. Serve it cold, 6-8°C, and drink it soon after buying, since it's made to be vibrant now, not later.
