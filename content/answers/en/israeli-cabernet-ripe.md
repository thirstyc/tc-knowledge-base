Why does Israeli Cabernet taste so ripe and structured?

The ripeness comes from where the grapes are actually grown, which surprises people who still think 'Israeli wine' means sweet kosher stuff from a supermarket shelf. Serious Cabernet here is planted at real altitude, up on the Golan Heights or in the Judean Hills, sometimes above 700-800 metres, where daytime sun is intense but nights cool off sharply. That diurnal swing lets the grapes build sugar and flavor during the day while retaining acidity overnight, so you get ripeness without the wine turning flabby. Drip irrigation, standard in Israeli viticulture, also lets growers control water stress precisely instead of relying on unpredictable rainfall, which pushes tannin development and concentration. On the winemaking side, temperature-controlled fermentation and time in French oak add the structure and polish. So the ripeness is genuinely climate-driven, but the structure that keeps it from tasting jammy is a mix of altitude, cool nights, and irrigation discipline rather than luck.
