Can you age Blaufränkisch?

Yes, and it's one of the more underrated ageworthy reds in Europe. Blaufränkisch has thick skins that give it real tannin structure alongside naturally high acidity, especially from cooler Austrian sites like Mittelburgenland and Leithaberg, where the grape ripens slowly and keeps its acid backbone. In youth that combination can taste tight and peppery, sometimes almost austere. Given ten years or more, the tannins soften and integrate, the primary black-fruit and pepper notes give way to something more savoury — dried herbs, leather, a touch of tobacco — and the acidity, which never really fades, keeps the whole thing tasting fresh rather than tired. This is why serious producers age their top bottlings in large oak or a mix of used barrels rather than leaning on new wood: they're banking on the fruit and tannin to carry the wine, not the oak. If you're buying to age, look for the site-specific bottlings rather than entry-level wine, and don't be afraid to hold it a decade.
