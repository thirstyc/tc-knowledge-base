Why does Verdejo taste so herbal with a bitter-almond edge?

Verdejo is native to Rueda, in Spain's high-altitude central plateau, where big day-to-night temperature swings let the grapes hang long enough to develop flavour while keeping acidity intact. Producers pick early and often macerate the skins briefly before pressing, which pulls out extra aromatic compounds along with a bitter, almond-like phenolic edge on the finish, something you don't get from cleaner, skin-free pressing. That bitterness is a genuine varietal marker, not a flaw, and it's part of what separates Verdejo from something like unoaked Chardonnay. Expect grassy, herbal notes alongside green apple and citrus, with a texture that's rounder than Sauvignon Blanc despite the similar freshness. It's often called Spain's answer to Sauvignon Blanc, but the almond bitterness and slightly broader mouthfeel give it its own identity rather than making it a copy. Serve cold, around 8-10°C, and drink young: Verdejo isn't built for cellaring, its charm is in the freshness.
