Why does Madeira taste so fortified and complex?

The process is almost violent compared to normal winemaking. Grape spirit gets added partway through fermentation, which stops the yeast dead and locks in residual sugar - that's the fortification, and it's why Madeira ranges from bone-dry to lusciously sweet depending on when in fermentation the spirit goes in (Sercial and Verdelho earlier and drier, Bual and Malmsey later and sweeter). Then comes the part unique to Madeira: instead of protecting the wine from heat and air, producers do the opposite. Cheaper wines go through estufagem, heated tanks cooking the wine over months; better wines age slowly in barrels in naturally warm lodges for years, called canteiro. Both trigger Maillard reactions and controlled oxidation, the same chemistry that browns bread crust, which is where the caramel, roasted nut, and dried citrus peel notes come from. High alcohol, typically around 18-20 percent, and searing acidity hold it all together - which is also why the wine barely changes once opened. It's already been through more stress than most wines see in a lifetime.
