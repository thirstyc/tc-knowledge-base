Why does Gros Manseng fly under the radar?

Southwest France makes some genuinely serious whites, and Gros Manseng is one of the reasons, but the region has spent decades in Bordeaux's shadow and Bordeaux only really talks about its reds anyway. Gros Manseng rarely gets to headline: it's most often blended with its more famous cousin Petit Manseng, or bottled as everyday Côtes de Gascogne, so it reads as a background grape even when it's doing real work. There's also no château mythology attached to it, no centuries of marketing built around scarcity and prestige — it's a workhorse grape from a region better known for Armagnac and duck confit than fine wine tourism. None of that reflects the quality in the glass. Its natural acidity, resistance to rot, and ability to stay fresh even at full ripeness make it one of the more food-friendly whites in France. It just doesn't have anyone selling that story loudly.
