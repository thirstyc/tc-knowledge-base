Why is Carignan so rustic?

Blame the vine's age and the climate it grew up in. Carignan is planted across the hot, dry stretches of Languedoc-Roussillon and similar Mediterranean zones, where relentless sun pushes sugar and tannin development hard, while the grape's naturally thick skins add even more tannin to the mix. Old vines, often decades old, dig deep for scarce water and produce small, concentrated berries loaded with skin-to-juice ratio — more skin means more tannin and color, less refinement. At the same time, Carignan holds onto acidity even in the heat, which is unusual and gives the wine a rough, almost aggressive backbone rather than the soft, fruit-forward roundness people expect from warm-climate reds. Put high tannin and high acid together in the same glass and you get a wine that feels angular and unpolished young, more of a working wine than a showpiece. Decanting an hour before serving, and pouring around 16 to 18 degrees, softens that edge considerably.
