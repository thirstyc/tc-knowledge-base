What are German wines like?

German wine means Riesling first: high in acidity, low in alcohol, and made in every style from bone-dry to intensely sweet. But the country is also now a serious source of Pinot Noir.

The Mosel is the most famous region, with vineyards on impossibly steep slate slopes above the river. Its Rieslings are delicate and lime-scented, often between 8% and 10% alcohol.

The Rheingau makes fuller, drier Riesling; the Nahe sits stylistically between the two; the Pfalz is warmer and riper; and Rheinhessen has shifted from bulk wine to some of the country's most exciting bottles.

Baden, in the south, is the country's warmest region and the heartland of Spätburgunder, the German name for Pinot Noir. Germany is one of the world's biggest Pinot producers, which surprises most people.

Labels are the hurdle. Terms like Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese describe how ripe the grapes were at harvest, not necessarily how sweet the wine is. "Trocken" means dry, "halbtrocken" or "feinherb" means off-dry.

Where to start: a Mosel Riesling Kabinett with spicy food, or a Baden Spätburgunder if you like Pinot Noir.
