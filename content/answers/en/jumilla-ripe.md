Why does Jumilla taste so ripe and powerful?

Jumilla sits inland at altitude in a nearly semi-desert climate, baking hot by day with a real temperature drop at night. That combination lets Monastrell hang on the vine and accumulate a lot of sugar before the acidity drops off, which is exactly the setup that produces high alcohol, often north of 14.5 percent, alongside ripe, thick-skinned tannins. The grape itself is naturally powerful, with dense color and structure, so a fully ripe Jumilla comes out dark, dense, and warming rather than crisp or lean. This is not a stylistic choice by the winemaker so much as a direct result of the place: less sun and heat would simply produce a different, thinner wine. The one common mistake is serving it too warm, which makes the alcohol taste hotter and the fruit taste jammy instead of just ripe. Bring it down to around 16 to 18 degrees and let a little air get to it before pouring. It is a wine built for grilled food and cooler service, not for sipping warm on its own.
