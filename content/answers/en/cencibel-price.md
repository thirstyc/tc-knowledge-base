Why is Cencibel so affordable?

Cencibel is just Tempranillo's local alias in La Mancha, the vast plateau south of Madrid that produces more wine than most countries manage in a decade. The plain is flat, sun-baked and enormous, which means vineyards get planted in huge blocks, harvested by machine, and irrigated to keep yields high rather than punishingly low like in Rioja or Ribera del Duero. High yields plus mechanization plus cheap land equals cheap grapes, and cheap grapes equals cheap wine. There's no scarcity story here, no steep hillside forcing hand-harvest, no barrel regime driving up costs. Most Cencibel sees little or no oak, gets bottled young, and is built to be poured on a Tuesday, not cellared. That's not an insult: it's a wine doing exactly what its growing conditions and economics set it up to do. Drink it lightly chilled, around 16°C, with something greasy and unfussy, and don't overthink it. The moment producers want prestige and price, they call it Tempranillo and put it in Ribera del Duero instead.
