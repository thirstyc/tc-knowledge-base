Why does Tuscan Sangiovese taste so tart and savory?

Sangiovese is genetically wired for acidity — it ripens sugar well before it ripens tannin and acid drop off, so growers often wait, and the grape holds its malic backbone even at full phenolic maturity. Tuscany's hills push this further: vines planted at elevation, often 300 to 500 metres, get intense midday sun but cold nights, and that swing preserves acid while still developing flavour. Add thin skins that give firm, fine-grained tannin rather than brute force, and you get a wine built around structure rather than fruit weight. This is why Chianti or Brunello taste lean and savoury, more sour cherry and dried oregano than jam. It is also why the wine is nearly unpleasant on its own but comes alive with food — the acid cuts fat, the tannin grips protein, and tomato-based sauces in particular find an echo in that same acidity. Serve it at 16 to 18 degrees, never colder, or the tannin turns hard and the fruit disappears entirely.
