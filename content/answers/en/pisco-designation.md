Why is Pisco protected designation?

Pisco is a geographic indication, and its protection exists because of a long-running dispute between Peru and Chile over who gets to call their grape brandy by that name. Both countries have historic claims: Peru points to a port town called Pisco and colonial-era distilling traditions in regions like Ica, while Chile has its own Pisco-adjacent towns and a large modern industry centered in Atacama and Coquimbo. The two countries settled into separate, incompatible rules rather than a shared standard. Peruvian pisco must be distilled to bottling strength with no water added afterward and can't touch oak, aged instead in glass or stainless steel, which keeps it sharp and aromatic. Chilean pisco can be diluted after distillation and aged in wood, giving it a softer, sometimes vanilla-tinged character. So the protected designation isn't just national pride for its own sake, it's the reason a Peruvian and a Chilean pisco can taste genuinely different despite sharing a name and a grape-based origin.
