Can you age Uruguayan Tannat?

Yes, and with the better bottlings it's almost expected. Tannat is naturally loaded with tannin and phenolic compounds, so young examples can taste tight and grippy even after fermentation and oak aging. Give a serious bottle eight to ten years, sometimes more, and those tannins polymerize and soften, the fruit shifts from primary black-plum and blackberry into dried fruit, leather, and tobacco, and the wine gains the kind of savoury complexity that makes people take Uruguay seriously as a red wine country. Many producers now use micro-oxygenation during vinification specifically to tame the tannin earlier, so not every bottle needs a decade in a cellar to be enjoyable, but the top reserve-style wines are built with long aging in mind. If you're buying one to lay down, look for producers who state cellaring potential on the back label rather than guessing. Serve at 16-17°C, and decant a younger bottle for an hour if you're drinking it before its time.
