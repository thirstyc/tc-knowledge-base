Why does Alsace Riesling taste so aromatic yet bone-dry?

Credit the Vosges mountains. They sit just west of the region and block most of the rain coming off the Atlantic, leaving Alsace one of the driest, sunniest pockets in France, which lets Riesling ripen fully while holding onto its natural acidity. That combination, full ripeness plus high acidity, is what produces the intense floral and stone-fruit perfume without needing residual sugar to carry it. The common mistake is assuming aromatic equals sweet, a habit left over from cheap German Riesling flooding the market decades ago. Alsace Riesling is usually fermented fully dry, the sugar converted, the perfume coming entirely from aromatic compounds in the grape skin, not from sweetness on the palate. Serve it around 10°C so the acid stays bright and the aromatics don't collapse into flabbiness. It's a genuinely useful bridge wine for people who love the smell of off-dry whites but actually prefer a bone-dry finish once the glass hits their lips.
