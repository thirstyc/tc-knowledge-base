Why is Israeli Carignan hard to find?

Carignan arrived in Israel with the Rothschild-backed plantings of the 1880s, brought in as a workhorse grape for the co-operative wineries that supplied bulk and sacramental wine for decades. It was planted for yield, not character, so nobody bothered making a serious bottling out of it. That only started changing in the last fifteen or twenty years, when a handful of producers noticed the old, low-yielding, dry-farmed vines scattered around the country and realised they were sitting on something genuinely good: concentrated, structured reds with real depth once you stop treating the grape like filler. But those old vines are few, the parcels are tiny, and most of what gets made barely leaves Israel because the domestic market drinks it first. You are not dealing with a hidden industry, you are dealing with a handful of producers and a few hectares. Find a bottle and you are tasting a genuine rediscovery, not a marketing story. Serve around 16 to 17 degrees, and do not expect two bottles from the same producer to taste identical year to year.
