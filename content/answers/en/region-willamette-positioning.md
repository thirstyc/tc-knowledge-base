Why do sommeliers rate Willamette Valley so highly?

It comes down to who showed up and what they refused to do. When Burgundy-trained winemakers planted Pinot Noir here in the 1960s and 70s, they brought Burgundian instincts: low yields, native-yeast fermentation, minimal new oak, let the site talk. The Willamette Valley gave them a genuinely cool, marine-influenced climate and volcanic Jory soil alongside sedimentary Willakenzie soil, so grapes ripen slowly and hang onto real acidity instead of turning into jammy fruit bombs. That combination, transparent winemaking plus a climate that actually requires patience, is what separates terroir-driven wine from wine that just tastes like a place on a label. Sommeliers respond to that legibility: you can taste the difference between a Jory hillside and a Willakenzie one, which is rare outside Burgundy itself. It's also still a small, farmer-scale region, not a corporate one, so quality control tends to be personal rather than industrial. Serve it around 14 to 16°C, and don't be afraid to age a good bottle five to ten years — the acid structure is built for it.
