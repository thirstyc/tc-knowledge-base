Why does South African Pinot Noir taste so elegant?

Credit the Benguela current. Cold water off the Atlantic pushes fog and wind into pockets like Hemel-en-Aarde and Elgin, dragging daytime temperatures down even though South Africa sits at a latitude that should be too warm for Pinot Noir. That cooling slows ripening, preserves acidity, and keeps alcohol in check, so you get the grape's real character — red cherry, wet earth, a savoury edge — instead of the jammy, alcoholic mess Pinot turns into when it ripens too fast. Clay and shale soils in these sites add tension rather than fat. The result is closer in structure to cool-vintage Burgundy than to riper New World Pinot from California or Central Otago: lighter body, brighter acid, less overt oak. It's still a young project by Burgundy's standards — most of these vineyards were only planted with Pinot in mind from the 1990s onward — but the sites were clearly worth the wait. Serve it a touch cooler than a red Bordeaux, around 14-16°C, to keep that acidity singing.
