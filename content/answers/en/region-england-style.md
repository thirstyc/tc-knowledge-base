Why does English sparkling wine taste so much like Champagne?

Because it is built from almost the same parts: similar ground, similar grapes, a similar cool climate and the same way of making the bubbles.

Start underground. The band of chalk that sits under Champagne runs beneath the Channel and resurfaces in the South Downs and North Downs of Sussex, Kent and Hampshire. Chalk drains well but holds just enough water, which suits the grapes both regions rely on.

Those grapes are the same trio: Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier. Southern England is cool, which used to be a problem, but warmer growing seasons over the past few decades now ripen them to roughly where Champagne's grapes were a generation ago. The payoff is high, bright acidity, the backbone of any great sparkling wine.

Then the method. The best English sparkling wines use the traditional method, the same one required in Champagne: a second fermentation in the bottle, then time resting on the spent yeast. That lees ageing is where the toast, brioche and fine, creamy bubbles come from.

The comparison stopped being flattering and started being serious when Champagne houses themselves began planting in England. Taittinger, for one, has vineyards in Kent.

The difference you'll usually notice is extra zip. English bottles tend to be a little leaner and more citrus-driven, which makes them brilliant with oysters and fried food.
