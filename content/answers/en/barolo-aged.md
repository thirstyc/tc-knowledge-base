Why does Barolo taste so structured and ageworthy?

Nebbiolo is a paradox: pale like Pinot Noir but loaded with tannin and acid like nothing else in Italy. The grape ripens late in the Langhe hills of Piedmont, often into October, building thick tannin in its skins and seeds while stubbornly holding onto acidity even at full ripeness. Traditional producers ferment on the skins for weeks, extracting even more grip, then age the wine in large old oak botti rather than small new barriques, which lets the tannins polymerize and soften without picking up heavy oak flavour. DOCG rules require years of aging before release precisely because young Barolo is often too austere to enjoy — tar, rose, tannin like a mouthful of tea bags. Given a decade or two, the tannins soften, and you get truffle, dried cherry, leather, orange peel. Decant a young bottle for a few hours if you're impatient, and serve around 16-18°C, cellar temperature, not room temperature in a heated apartment. It's not just expensive for prestige; the aging capacity is genuinely built into the grape's chemistry.
