Why does Languedoc wine taste so sun-soaked and generous?

Languedoc sits under near-constant Mediterranean sun with scrubby garrigue — thyme, rosemary, juniper — growing wild between the vine rows, and that herbal air genuinely perfumes the grapes as they ripen, which is why the wines smell like the landscape they came from. The heat pushes sugars up fast, but old, low-yielding bush vines (Carignan especially, a grape that's harsh and acidic when young and overcropped, generous and dark-fruited when old and restrained) concentrate flavor rather than just alcohol. Grenache adds soft, jammy weight; Syrah brings pepper and structure. Compared to the Rhône villages just north, Languedoc wines tend to ripen further and cost less, trading some of that finesse for outright fruit density. Compared to Bordeaux, there's less oak, less tannic architecture, more sun-drenched immediacy — you're drinking the vintage, not the winemaking technique. Serve these reds around 16-18°C; too cold and the fruit seizes up, too warm and the alcohol takes over.
