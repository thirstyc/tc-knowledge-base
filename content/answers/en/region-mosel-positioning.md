Why is Mosel considered Riesling's spiritual home?

The Mosel's steep, slate-covered slopes have been farmed for wine for roughly two thousand years in some spots, but it's the slate itself that matters practically: it absorbs heat during the day and radiates it back at night, helping grapes ripen on a river this far north where sun alone wouldn't be enough. The river also reflects light back onto the vines, adding a second dose of warmth to compensate for the marginal climate. That precarious balance — just enough ripeness, but never losing the acid — is exactly what makes Mosel Riesling so distinctive: it can carry noticeable residual sugar and still taste taut rather than sweet, because the acidity never lets the sugar dominate. Styles range from bone-dry to lusciously sweet (kabinett through auslese and beyond), but the acid-sugar tension is constant across all of them. That's the reason producers elsewhere use Mosel as the reference point when they're trying to describe how their own Riesling turned out.
