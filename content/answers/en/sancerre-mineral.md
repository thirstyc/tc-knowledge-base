Why does Sancerre taste so mineral and tense?

The tension comes from a fight between climate and grape. Sancerre is planted on Kimmeridgian limestone and clay, with pockets of flinty silex, in a Loire climate that's genuinely cool and marginal for ripening. Sauvignon Blanc grown there hangs onto its malic acid because the season rarely delivers enough heat to soften it, which is exactly why the wine feels taut and almost nervy on the palate rather than round. The limestone itself doesn't literally taste like rock in the glass, that's a common misconception, but it drains well, moderates vine vigor, and correlates with the chalky, flinty character tasters call minerality, likely a combination of soil-driven aromatics and simply the absence of fruit-masking sugar or oak. Producers avoid new oak almost entirely so nothing rounds out that edge. Serve it properly cold, around 8°C, colder than you'd chill a New World Sauvignon Blanc, and drink within a few years of the vintage. Age doesn't do this style any favors.
