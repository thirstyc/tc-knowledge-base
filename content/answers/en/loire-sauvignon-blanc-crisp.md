Why does Loire Valley Sauvignon Blanc taste so crisp?

Acidity in wine comes mostly from malic and tartaric acid, and cool nights in the Loire mean grapes retain more malic acid because heat is what breaks it down during ripening. Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé sit far enough north that the growing season stays cool even when days are warm, so the grapes ripen in flavour without losing that acid backbone. The soils matter too: flinty silex gives a smoky, gunflint edge, while the Kimmeridgian limestone found in parts of Sancerre adds a chalky tension that reads as minerality. Winemakers reinforce the style by fermenting mostly in stainless steel at cool temperatures, which preserves the citrus and green fruit character rather than layering on oak or letting malolactic fermentation soften the acid. The result is a wine built around precision rather than power. Serve it cold, around 8 to 10 degrees, cold enough to keep that acidity taut but not so cold it mutes the aromatics. Drink it young, this isn't a style built for the cellar.
