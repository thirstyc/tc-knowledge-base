Why does Muscadet fly under the radar?

Muscadet earned its cheap reputation honestly, decades of mass production turned it into the default bulk oyster wine, sold young, sold cheap, and never expected to be more than a rinse for shellfish. That image stuck even as serious producers kept working the granite and schist soils of the Sèvre-et-Maine with real care. The wines that separate themselves use extended sur lie aging, sometimes well over a year on the spent yeast lees, which builds textural depth, a faint saline complexity and occasionally a light natural spritz you won't find in the bargain versions. Old vines and specific crus within the region add concentration that basic bulk Muscadet never had. These bottlings can age for a decade or more, gaining honeyed, savory notes that have nothing to do with the thin, forgettable wine most people picture. The problem is purely reputational: the category never rebranded itself, so shoppers keep reaching for it expecting three-euro oyster wine and get exactly that, while the better bottles sit ignored right next to them.
