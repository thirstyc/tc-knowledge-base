Why does Banyuls taste so dark and sweet?

Banyuls comes from Grenache grown on steep schist terraces above the Roussillon coast, and it's fortified the same way port is: neutral grape spirit gets added partway through fermentation, which kills off the yeast before all the sugar converts. That leaves natural sweetness behind and pushes alcohol up to around 16-17%. Many Banyuls are then aged oxidatively — left in old wood or glass demijohns outdoors, exposed to heat and light on purpose — which drives a nutty, dried-fruit, faintly caramelized character winemakers call rancio. That's where the darkness comes from: not just Grenache's natural depth of fruit, but deliberate slow oxidation that turns fresh fruit flavors into something closer to fig, coffee and toffee. It's a controlled, intentional aging style, not a flaw. Serve it cool, around 14-16°C — too warm and the alcohol and sweetness both feel heavier than they should.
