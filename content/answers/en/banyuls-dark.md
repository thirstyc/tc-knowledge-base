Why does Banyuls taste so dark and sweet?

Banyuls is Grenache grown on steep schist terraces above the Roussillon coast, fortified the same way port is: neutral grape spirit is added mid-fermentation, which kills the yeast before all the sugar converts. That leaves natural sweetness in the wine and pushes alcohol to around 16-17%. Many Banyuls are then aged oxidatively on purpose — left in old wood or glass demijohns outdoors, exposed to heat and light instead of shielded from it — which builds a nutty, dried-fruit, faintly caramelized character winemakers call rancio. That's where the darkness actually comes from: not just Grenache's inherent depth, but deliberate slow oxidation turning fresh fruit into fig, coffee, toffee. It's a controlled aging style, the opposite of a flaw, closer to how Madeira or tawny port get their colour. Serve it cool, 14-16°C, not room temperature — warmer and the alcohol and sweetness both start to feel heavier and less precise than they're meant to.
