Why does Vermouth vary so much?

Because there's barely a rulebook. Vermouth has to be wine-based, has to reach 14.5% alcohol, and has to be flavoured with wormwood. Beyond that, producers do as they please.

The old division was national. French vermouth meant dry, pale and mineral — Chambéry, Marseille, built for a Martini. Italian meant sweet and red, Turin-style, caramel and warm spice, built for a Negroni or for drinking on its own over ice. Both traditions still exist, but the labels stopped being a reliable guide decades ago. Italy makes excellent dry vermouth; France makes sweet.

What actually varies is everything that matters: the base wine, anywhere from industrial neutral white to a decent regional bottling; the botanical mix, proprietary and sometimes fifty deep; sweetness, from bone dry to pudding; and colour, which is frequently just caramel.

The practical upshot is that two bottles both labelled sweet vermouth can taste nothing alike. Buy by producer, not by category.
