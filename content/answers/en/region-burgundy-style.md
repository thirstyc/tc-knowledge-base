Why does Burgundy Pinot taste so elegant and mineral?

It comes down to limestone and cold. The Côte d'Or sits on a band of Jurassic limestone and marl that drains fast but holds just enough water to stress the vine gently — not enough to shut it down, just enough to keep yields low and flavour concentrated. Pinot Noir is thin-skinned and fussy, so it shows soil and site more honestly than thicker-skinned grapes like Cabernet. Add a cool climate where grapes ripen slowly over a long season, and you get wines that finish ripening sugar before they finish building acid, which is the opposite of what happens in warmer regions. That acid is what makes red Burgundy taste 'mineral' — it's really just high natural acidity reading as saline or stony on the palate, and it's why the wine cuts through fat and salt at the table instead of getting steamrolled by it. Serve it around 16°C, slightly cooler than room temperature, or the alcohol and earthiness will dominate the fruit.
