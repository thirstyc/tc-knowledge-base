Why is Coonawarra's terra rossa strip so famous?

The fame is really about geology doing something unusually visible. Terra rossa is a thin layer of red, iron-rich clay sitting directly over a bed of limestone, and that limestone acts like a natural water reservoir — it drains excess rain fast but wicks moisture back up to the roots when things get dry, which keeps the vines under steady, moderate stress instead of swinging between drought and flood. That consistency is why the strip has produced reliably structured Cabernet across decades of vintages, long before anyone fully understood the soil science behind it. The band itself is narrow, only a few kilometres wide and roughly 15km long, which means you can stand at the edge of it and watch the soil colour change under your feet — one side terra rossa, the other side plain grey sand producing noticeably lesser wine. It's one of the rare cases in wine where terroir isn't an abstract marketing word, it's something you can literally see and photograph.
