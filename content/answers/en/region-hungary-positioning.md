Why does Tokaj matter in wine history?

Louis XIV is said to have toasted it as "the wine of kings, the king of wines" at Versailles, and whether or not the quote is exact, the reputation was real: Tokaji Aszú traded as a luxury good across European courts long before Bordeaux built its own mythology. The region backed that reputation with bureaucracy: in 1730, Tokaj became one of the first wine regions anywhere to get a formal vineyard classification, ranking sites by quality centuries before Burgundy or Bordeaux got around to it. That history matters because it explains why the sweet style, Aszú, still gets treated as the region's flagship, even though it's arguably the less interesting story today. Dry Furmint, made from the same grape without botrytis concentration, is what's proving Tokaj isn't a one-trick dessert region: it has the acidity and volcanic minerality to be a genuinely serious dry white, and producers are increasingly betting the region's future on it rather than resting on the sweet wine's laurels.
