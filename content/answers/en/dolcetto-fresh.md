Why Does Dolcetto Taste So Fresh?

Despite the name meaning little sweet one, Dolcetto is bone dry, and its freshness comes from timing more than acidity. Growers in Piedmont pick it early, before Nebbiolo and Barbera, precisely because it doesn't need long hang time to develop flavor, and that early harvest locks in vivid raspberry and cherry fruit before things turn jammy. Its tannins are soft and round rather than gripping, sometimes with a slight bitter edge on the finish, so there's nothing to soften with years in a cellar. While Nebbiolo sits in barrel for extended aging and Barbera gets some oak treatment, Dolcetto goes into bottle young and is meant to be drunk within a year or two, it's the wine Piedmontese producers open on a random Tuesday while their serious bottles are still sleeping. Serve it lightly chilled, around 14 to 16°C, with salumi or pasta. It's proof that a wine doesn't need structure or age-worthiness to be genuinely satisfying, sometimes uncomplicated and well-made is exactly the point.
