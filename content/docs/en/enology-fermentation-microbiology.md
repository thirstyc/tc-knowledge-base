## Alcoholic fermentation — the core reaction {#alcoholic-fermentation-the-core-reaction}

Alcoholic fermentation is the conversion of grape sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide by yeast, with heat released as a byproduct. In simple terms: glucose and fructose (the two grape sugars) are metabolized, roughly 17 grams of sugar per liter yielding about 1% alcohol by volume. A must at 220 g/L of sugar therefore produces a wine near 13% alcohol.

Fermentation also generates hundreds of secondary compounds — glycerol, higher alcohols, esters, aldehydes, and organic acids — that shape a wine's texture and aroma far beyond mere ethanol. The reaction is anaerobic in its main phase: yeast ferments sugar in the absence of oxygen, though a small amount of oxygen early on helps yeast build healthy cell membranes.

Why it matters to tasting: the completeness of fermentation determines dryness. If yeast converts all the sugar, the wine is dry; if fermentation stops early (naturally or by intervention), residual sugar remains and the wine is off-dry or sweet.

## Wine yeasts — Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the others {#wine-yeasts-saccharomyces-cerevisiae-and-the-others}

The dominant wine yeast is Saccharomyces cerevisiae, prized because it tolerates high alcohol, ferments reliably to dryness, and produces clean flavors. It is present naturally on grape skins and cellar surfaces, but most modern wineries inoculate with selected cultured strains for predictability.

Before Saccharomyces takes over, non-Saccharomyces "wild" yeasts (genera such as Hanseniaspora/Kloeckera, Candida, Metschnikowia, Torulaspora) dominate the early hours. These are less alcohol-tolerant and die off as ethanol rises, but they contribute aromatic complexity — which is part of why spontaneous (wild) ferments taste different from inoculated ones.

Cultured vs. ambient yeast — the stylistic debate:

- Cultured yeast behaves predictably, ferments cleanly, and can be chosen to emphasize particular aromas or to handle difficult conditions (high sugar, low nutrients, sparkling base wine).
- Ambient/indigenous yeast is less predictable but, its advocates argue, adds interest and a sense of place — arguably an aspect of terroir.
- Practically, a strong strain established in one vat will often kick-start neighbors.

## Yeast nutrition and stuck fermentation {#yeast-nutrition-and-stuck-fermentation}

Yeast needs more than sugar: it requires nitrogen (as ammonium and amino acids — measured as YAN, yeast assimilable nitrogen), plus vitamins, minerals, and survival factors. When nitrogen is deficient, fermentation slows or halts.

A stuck fermentation — where sugar remains but yeast activity has stopped — is the winemaker's nightmare, because a sweet, low-alcohol, partially fermented liquid is highly vulnerable to spoilage microbes. Causes include nitrogen deficiency, excessive temperature, very high initial sugar, toxic levels of ethanol, and inhibition by excess SO₂.

Why it matters to tasting: a well-nourished, clean fermentation gives fresh, fruity, fault-free wine. Nutrient stress can throw off reductive (sulfurous, struck-match, rotten-egg) aromas from hydrogen sulfide production.

## Fermentation temperature and its effect on style {#fermentation-temperature-and-its-effect-on-style}

Temperature is one of the winemaker's most powerful levers:

- Cool fermentation (12–17°C) for aromatic whites preserves delicate, fruity, floral esters. Too cool and fermentation is sluggish.
- Warm fermentation (20–32°C) for reds extracts more color, tannin, and flavor from the skins, and produces fuller-bodied, more structured wines.
- Fermentation is exothermic — it generates heat — so vats naturally warm up and often need cooling. Too hot (above ~35°C) and fermentation can "boil off" volatile aromatics or even kill the yeast, causing a stuck ferment.

Rule of thumb for the sommelier: bright, primary-fruit whites imply cool ferments; deeply colored, structured reds imply warmer ones.

## Malolactic conversion (MLF) — the second fermentation {#malolactic-conversion-mlf-the-second-fermentation}

Malolactic conversion (also called malolactic fermentation, though it's bacterial not yeast-driven) is carried out by lactic acid bacteria, chiefly Oenococcus oeni. It converts harsh malic acid (the sharp acid of green apples) into softer lactic acid (the mild acid of milk), releasing CO₂.

Effects:

- Lowers total acidity and raises pH, making the wine rounder and less tart.
- Adds texture and flavor — the buttery, creamy note in many Chardonnays comes from diacetyl, a byproduct of MLF.
- Microbial stability — once malic acid is consumed, it can't be fermented later in bottle, avoiding unwanted fizz and haze.

Where it's used:

- Nearly all red wines go through MLF — it softens them and adds complexity.
- Most premium Chardonnay (including white Burgundy) is encouraged through MLF for texture.
- Aromatic, high-acid whites (Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc) are usually blocked from MLF — by cool temperatures, SO₂ additions, or filtering out the bacteria — to keep them crisp and preserve fruit.

Why it matters to tasting: buttery, creamy, "milky" texture in a white signals MLF; racy, tart, green-apple freshness signals it was suppressed.

## Lactic acid bacteria as spoilage agents {#lactic-acid-bacteria-as-spoilage-agents}

The same family of lactic acid bacteria that performs beneficial MLF can spoil wine under the wrong conditions. If they act on sugars rather than malic acid — for instance in a stuck ferment with residual sugar, or at high pH with low SO₂ — they can produce:

- Volatile acidity (excess acetic acid — vinegar).
- Mousiness — an off-flavor perceptible on the finish, associated with certain bacteria.
- Biogenic amines and other off-compounds.

Control relies on completing fermentation cleanly, maintaining adequate SO₂, managing pH, and hygiene.

## Acetic acid bacteria and volatile acidity {#acetic-acid-bacteria-and-volatile-acidity}

Acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter, Gluconobacter) require oxygen and convert ethanol into acetic acid (vinegar) and ethyl acetate (nail-polish-remover smell). Together these make up much of a wine's volatile acidity (VA).

Small amounts of VA can add lift and complexity; excess is a fault. The main defenses are limiting oxygen exposure (keeping barrels topped up, tanks full, sound closures) and maintaining protective SO₂. Wines most at risk are those left with air contact, in ullaged barrels, or with high pH and low sulfur.

Why it matters to tasting: a sharp vinegar or solvent note on the nose is VA. Natural wines made with minimal SO₂ sometimes show elevated VA, which is why the style can be polarizing.

## Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) — the winemaker's essential tool {#sulfur-dioxide-so2-the-winemakers-essential-tool}

Sulfur dioxide is the most important additive in winemaking, valued for three properties:

1. Antimicrobial — inhibits unwanted yeasts and bacteria (spoilage organisms, and it can be used to block MLF).
1. Antioxidant — protects against oxidation that browns the wine and dulls fruit.
1. Antioxidasic — deactivates oxidative enzymes from the grape (especially important with rot-affected fruit).

SO₂ exists in wine in free and bound forms; only free SO₂ (particularly molecular SO₂, which depends on pH) is protective. Lower-pH wines need less SO₂ to stay protected, which is one reason high-acid wines are more stable.

Regulations cap total SO₂, and "contains sulfites" must be declared on labels because a minority of people are sensitive. "Natural" and low-intervention winemaking minimizes SO₂, trading some stability for a particular expression — and accepting higher spoilage risk.

Why it matters to tasting: freshly bottled or heavily sulfured wine can show a struck-match or matchstick note that blows off with air. Under-sulfured wine is more prone to oxidation (bruised-apple, sherry-like notes) and VA.

## Oxygen — friend and enemy {#oxygen-friend-and-enemy}

Oxygen's role flips depending on stage:

- Early (fermentation): a little oxygen helps yeast build healthy membranes and ferment strongly.
- Ageing (controlled): slow, tiny oxygen ingress through barrels or via deliberate micro-oxygenation softens tannins and stabilizes color in reds.
- Excess/uncontrolled: oxygen browns wine, fades fruit, and feeds acetic acid bacteria.

Protective vs. oxidative white winemaking is a key stylistic fork:

- Protective (reductive): juice is shielded from oxygen with SO₂ and cool temperatures to preserve fresh primary fruit — standard for Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling, aromatic whites.
- Oxidative: grapes and juice are deliberately exposed to some oxygen early, pre-empting later oxidation and building secondary, more complex flavors — traditional for top Chardonnay and white Burgundy.

## Must composition — what's in grape juice {#must-composition-whats-in-grape-juice}

Grape must (unfermented juice) is roughly 70–80% water, plus:

- Sugars — glucose and fructose, in near-equal amounts at ripeness; the raw material for alcohol.
- Acids — mainly tartaric (grape's signature acid, stable and strong) and malic (sharper, decreases as grapes ripen); small amounts of citric.
- Phenolics — tannins and pigments, concentrated in skins and seeds.
- Nitrogen compounds — amino acids and ammonium, the yeast's nitrogen supply.
- Minerals — potassium, calcium, magnesium.
- Aroma precursors — often flavorless in the grape, released as aroma during fermentation and ageing.

Sugar accumulates and acid falls as grapes ripen — the central tension of harvest timing. Pick too early and the wine is thin and tart; too late and it's high in alcohol and flabby.

## Acidity, pH, and why they're different {#acidity-ph-and-why-theyre-different}

- Total (titratable) acidity measures the concentration of acids — how sour the wine tastes.
- pH measures acid strength/activity on a logarithmic scale — and it governs microbial stability, SO₂ effectiveness, and color.
- Low pH (high acidity) wines are fresher-tasting, more microbially stable, need less SO₂, and hold brighter red color. High pH wines are rounder but more fragile and prone to spoilage and browning.

Warm-climate grapes often have high sugar but low acidity and high pH — which is why winemakers in hot regions routinely acidify with tartaric acid. Cool-climate grapes may need the opposite (deacidification, or MLF to soften).

## Chaptalization and enrichment {#chaptalization-and-enrichment}

Chaptalization is adding sugar to the fermenting must to raise the final alcohol (not sweetness — the added sugar ferments out). Named for Napoleon's minister Jean-Antoine Chaptal, it's a centuries-old practice in cool regions where grapes struggle to reach full ripeness. AOC rules typically cap the boost at around 2% alcohol. Warmer vintages and better viticulture have steadily reduced the need for it.

## Concentration and alcohol adjustment {#concentration-and-alcohol-adjustment}

Modern cellars can adjust must and wine mechanically:

- Concentration (reverse osmosis, vacuum evaporation, cryoextraction) removes water to intensify sugar/flavor.
- Alcohol reduction (reverse osmosis, spinning cone, low-temperature distillation) trims excess alcohol in wines that fermented too hot — a growing concern in warm regions and with climate change.
- Saignée ("bleeding" off a portion of juice early in a red ferment) increases the skin-to-juice ratio, concentrating color and tannin in what remains — and yields rosé as a byproduct.

Why it matters to tasting: these tools explain why a wine from a hot vintage can still be balanced, and why alcohol levels have crept up over recent decades.

## The fermentation-to-glass summary {#the-fermentation-to-glass-summary}

1. Ripe grapes supply sugar, acid, phenolics, and aroma precursors.
1. Yeast (wild then Saccharomyces) ferments sugar to alcohol, CO₂, heat, and hundreds of flavor compounds; temperature controls style.
1. Lactic acid bacteria may perform MLF, softening acidity and adding creamy texture.
1. SO₂ and oxygen management protect the wine and steer it between fresh/reductive and complex/oxidative styles.
1. Acidity, pH, and any adjustments (chaptalization, acidification, concentration) set the final balance the taster perceives.
