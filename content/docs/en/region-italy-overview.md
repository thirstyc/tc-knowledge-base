## Why Italy matters {#why-italy-matters}

Italy vies with France as the world's largest wine producer and has the richest diversity of indigenous grape varieties on earth — hundreds in commercial use, most found nowhere else. Its long mountainous spine, running from the Alps almost to North Africa, offers nearly every combination of altitude, latitude, and exposure, plus volcanic, limestone, and clay soils. Every one of its 20 regions makes wine and has its own grapes, traditions, and personality. The trade-off for this variety is complexity: Italian wine labels are famously a labyrinth.
Recommend when: the user wants distinctive indigenous grapes, high-acid food wines, regional variety, or great value.

## The DOC / DOCG / IGT system {#the-doc-docg-igt-system}

Italy's quality pyramid, modeled on France's but with its own quirks:

- Vino (formerly Vino da Tavola) — basic table wine; no region or vintage historically. Now also used by experimental producers whose wines don't fit the rules.
- IGT (Indicazione Geografica Tipica) — the equivalent of France's IGP; regional wines allowing stated grape and vintage. Home to much good-value and some ambitious wine. Common IGTs carry region names (e.g. Toscana, Sicilia — though Sicilia was later promoted to DOC).
- DOC (Denominazione di Origine Controllata) — the main quality tier, like France's AOC; defined boundaries, yields, grapes, and methods.
- DOCG (…e Garantita) — the top tier, with stricter rules and a guarantee of origin; reserved for the most prestigious zones. Bottles carry a numbered government seal.

Practical use: DOCG signals a top zone, but Italy's system is imperfect — some brilliant wines are "only" IGT by choice. The grape and region often matter more than the tier.

## The Super Tuscan phenomenon {#the-super-tuscan-phenomenon}

A key thing for the sommelier to understand: in the 1970s–80s, ambitious Tuscan producers began making high-quality wines using international grapes (Cabernet, Merlot) or non-traditional methods that fell outside DOC rules — so they were labeled mere "table wine." These "Super Tuscans" became some of Italy's most celebrated and expensive wines, and the system eventually adapted (creating IGT Toscana and DOCs like Bolgheri to accommodate them). The lesson: in Italy, a humble classification doesn't mean humble wine.
Recommend when: the user asks what a "Super Tuscan" is, or why a great Italian wine is labeled IGT.

## Italys grape treasury — the essentials {#italys-grape-treasury-the-essentials}

Italy's indigenous grapes are its glory. The most important to know:
Reds: Sangiovese (Tuscany — Chianti, Brunello), Nebbiolo (Piedmont — Barolo, Barbaresco), Barbera and Dolcetto (Piedmont everyday reds), Corvina (Veneto — Valpolicella, Amarone), Aglianico (the south — "the Barolo of the south"), Nero d'Avola (Sicily), Negroamaro and Primitivo (Puglia), Montepulciano (Abruzzo), Sagrantino (Umbria).
Whites: Pinot Grigio (northeast), Garganega (Soave), Trebbiano (widespread), Vermentino (coastal/Sardinia), Cortese (Gavi), Verdicchio (Marche), Fiano, Greco, Falanghina (Campania), Glera (Prosecco).
Recommend when: matching a user's taste or dish to an Italian grape; explaining a regional specialty.

## The through-line: acidity and food {#the-through-line-acidity-and-food}

The unifying trait of Italian wine is high acidity and a savory, sometimes bitter edge — because Italian wine evolved with food. This makes Italian wines exceptional table companions (especially with tomato, which defeats many wines) but can make them seem austere on their own. When in doubt, Italian wine wants food.
Recommend when: the user is eating Italian food, wants a high-acid food wine, or finds an Italian wine "too sharp" on its own.

## Quick summary italy-overview {#quick-summary-italy-overview}

- What: France's rival in volume; the world's deepest well of indigenous grapes; 20 distinct regions.
- Classification: Vino → IGT → DOC → DOCG (top). But great wines exist at every tier (Super Tuscans are often IGT).
- Flagship grapes: Sangiovese (Tuscany), Nebbiolo (Piedmont), Corvina (Veneto), Aglianico/Nero d'Avola (south).
- Signature: high acidity, savory bite, built for food.
