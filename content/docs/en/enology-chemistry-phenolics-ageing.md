## Phenolics — the family that shapes red wine {#phenolics-the-family-that-shapes-red-wine}

Phenolic compounds (polyphenols) are the group responsible for a red wine's color, tannin, structure, bitterness, and much of its ageability. They come mostly from grape skins, seeds, and stems, which is why red winemaking (fermenting on skins) extracts far more phenolics than white winemaking (pressing before fermentation).

The two headline categories are anthocyanins (pigments — color) and tannins (structure and astringency). Both are far more concentrated in red grapes and in thick-skinned varieties, and both are central to why some wines age for decades and others don't.

Why it matters to tasting: phenolics are what you feel as much as taste — the grip, the drying sensation, the weight. A pale, low-tannin wine and a dense, gripping one differ mostly in phenolic content and extraction.

## Anthocyanins — where red color comes from {#anthocyanins-where-red-color-comes-from}

Anthocyanins are the pigments in dark grape skins that give red wine its color. The pulp of almost all wine grapes is greyish-clear; color comes from skin contact during fermentation, which is why:

- Red wine ferments on its skins.
- White wine is pressed off the skins before fermentation.
- Rosé takes a short skin contact, then presses off — tinting the juice pink.
- "Teinturier" grapes are the rare exception with red-fleshed pulp.

Anthocyanin color is pH-sensitive: brighter, more purple-red at low pH (high acidity); duller and bluer at high pH. Free anthocyanins are unstable on their own — over time they combine with tannins into stable polymeric pigments, which is how a wine keeps color as it ages even as the original pigments fade.

Why it matters to tasting: a young red's purple rim signals fresh anthocyanins and high acidity; a brick-orange rim signals age and the shift to polymerized pigment. Color at the rim is one of the most reliable visual clues to a red's maturity.

## Tannins — structure, astringency, and age {#tannins-structure-astringency-and-age}

Tannins are phenolic compounds that bind to proteins — including the proteins in your saliva — which is what produces the drying, grippy sensation of astringency. They come from:

- Grape skins — generally riper, softer, more desirable tannins.
- Grape seeds (pips) — harsher, more bitter; why gentle pressing avoids crushing seeds.
- Stems — green, astringent if unripe; some winemakers use whole-cluster fermentation deliberately for structure and aroma when stems are ripe.
- Oak — barrels contribute their own wood tannins.

Tannins serve as a natural preservative and are the main reason age-worthy reds can improve for decades. Young tannins are firm and sometimes harsh; over time they polymerize (link into larger molecules) and eventually precipitate as sediment, so an old red is both paler and softer than it was in youth.

Managing tannin extraction during fermentation is a core red-winemaking skill — punch-downs, pump-overs, and time on skins all control how much tannin is drawn out and how smooth it ends up.

Why it matters to tasting: high, firm tannin plus deep color usually means a wine built to age. Soft, resolved tannin means it's ready now. Bitter, green tannin suggests underripe fruit or seed/stem extraction.

## How tannin ripeness differs from sugar ripeness {#how-tannin-ripeness-differs-from-sugar-ripeness}

A crucial distinction for understanding modern wine: phenolic (tannin) ripeness and sugar ripeness don't always arrive together. In warm climates and with climate change, sugar can race ahead — reaching high potential alcohol — while tannins and flavors lag behind, still green.

This drives the "hang time" dilemma: growers delay picking to let tannins ripen, but the extra time pushes sugar (and therefore alcohol) higher. It's a major reason modern reds have crept up in alcohol. The reverse problem — ripe sugar but unripe tannin — gives high-alcohol wines with harsh, green structure.

Why it matters to tasting: a wine that's both high in alcohol and green/astringent points to a mismatch between sugar and phenolic ripeness at harvest.

## Organic acids in the finished wine {#organic-acids-in-the-finished-wine}

The main acids carried from grape to glass:

- Tartaric acid — grape's signature acid, strong and stable, the primary contributor to a wine's low pH and freshness.
- Malic acid — sharper, "green apple"; abundant in underripe/cool-climate fruit, consumed by MLF.
- Lactic acid — milder, "milky"; produced by MLF from malic acid.
- Citric acid — minor.
- Acetic acid — the volatile one; small amounts normal, excess is the vinegar fault (VA).
- Succinic, lactic, and others — produced during fermentation.

Acidity provides freshness, balances sweetness and alcohol, sharpens flavor, and — via low pH — protects color and microbial stability. As a wine ages, perceived acidity can seem to soften even though measured acidity stays roughly constant, because developing complexity distracts from it.

## Tartrate stability and "wine diamonds" {#tartrate-stability-and-wine-diamonds}

Tartaric acid combines with potassium to form potassium bitartrate (cream of tartar), which is soluble in warm liquid but precipitates as harmless crystals when wine gets cold. These glassy "wine diamonds" alarm consumers but are a sign of nothing wrong.

To prevent them appearing in bottle, many wines are cold-stabilized — chilled to near freezing (about 0°C) before bottling so the crystals drop out in the tank instead. Alternatives include contact seeding and electrodialysis. Some fine-wine producers skip it, accepting crystals as natural.

Why it matters to tasting: crystals on a cork or at the bottom of a bottle are cosmetic, not a fault — useful for the sommelier to reassure a worried drinker.

## Aroma — the three layers {#aroma-the-three-layers}

A wine's smell is built in three overlapping layers, and naming them helps a taster place a wine:

- Primary aromas — from the grape itself: fruit, floral, herbal, mineral character. Dominant in young wines.
- Secondary aromas — from winemaking: yeast/lees (bread, brioche), MLF (butter, cream), and oak (vanilla, spice, toast, coconut).
- Tertiary aromas — from ageing: the complex "bouquet" of maturity — dried fruit, nuts, honey, forest floor, leather, savory and earthy notes formed slowly by oxidation and molecular evolution in bottle.

Why it matters to tasting: a wine dominated by primary fruit reads as young; one showing tertiary bouquet reads as mature. Recognizing which layer dominates is central to estimating a wine's age and readiness.

## Aroma chemistry — the compound families {#aroma-chemistry-the-compound-families}

The molecules behind those aromas:

- Esters — fruity and floral; formed during fermentation, prominent in young wines, and fading with age. Responsible for much of the "primary fruit" in youthful whites and light reds.
- Higher alcohols (fusel alcohols) — contribute body and, in excess, harsh solvent notes.
- Terpenes — floral and grapey; the signature of aromatic varieties like Muscat, Riesling, and Gewürztraminer.
- Thiols (volatile sulfur compounds) — the passionfruit/gooseberry/grapefruit punch of Sauvignon Blanc; also, in the wrong form, the reductive rotten-egg/struck-match faults.
- Methoxypyrazines — green bell pepper, herbaceous; marker of underripe Cabernet family and Sauvignon Blanc.
- Rotundone — the black-pepper compound, characteristic of Syrah.
- Norisoprenoids — from carotenoid breakdown; contribute to aged/tertiary complexity.
- Aldehydes — notably acetaldehyde, a marker of oxidation (and intentional in sherry).

Why it matters to tasting: these are the vocabulary behind tasting notes — bell pepper (pyrazine), pepper (rotundone), passionfruit (thiol), rose/lychee (terpene). The sommelier can reason backward from a described smell to grape and ripeness.

## Why wine ages — the bottle chemistry {#why-wine-ages-the-bottle-chemistry}

Ageing in bottle is driven by slow reactions among phenolics, acids, and the small amount of oxygen dissolved in the wine and held in the headspace under the cork:

- Tannins and pigments polymerize — linking into larger molecules that soften astringency, shift color from purple to brick, and eventually precipitate as sediment.
- Primary fruit fades as esters break down; tertiary bouquet develops through oxidative and other reactions.
- The wine moves toward a harmonious whole at its peak, then eventually declines — losing fruit, with acidity or tannin coming to dominate as harmony breaks down.

Not all wine improves with age — this is a persistent myth. The majority of wine is made to drink young. Ageability depends on having enough tannin, acidity, and concentration to evolve rather than simply fade.

Why it matters to tasting: the sommelier should match ageing advice to structure — high tannin/acid/concentration = candidate for the cellar; light, low-tannin, fruity = drink now.

## Ageing potential by style (practical guide) {#ageing-potential-by-style-practical-guide}

Approximate windows, all else equal:

- Light, fruity reds (Beaujolais/Gamay, simple Pinot, Dolcetto): drink within 1–4 years.
- Aromatic whites (Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, most Muscat): 1–3 years, best fresh.
- Mid-weight reds (Merlot, Chianti, Rioja Crianza): 3–8 years.
- Structured reds (Cabernet, Barolo, Northern Rhône, Bordeaux Cru Classé, Barossa Shiraz): 10–30+ years.
- High-acid ageworthy whites (Riesling, Chablis, Chenin, Hunter Semillon): 10–20+ years.
- Great sweet wines (Sauternes, Tokaji, botrytized Riesling, icewine): 20–50+ years.
- Fortified (vintage port longest of all; tawny, sherry, madeira ready on release): variable, vintage port 20–50+.

Half-bottles age faster and magnums slower, because the ratio of headspace oxygen to wine volume differs — a reason collectors pay premiums for large formats.

## The dumb phase {#the-dumb-phase}

Many fine wines pass through an awkward "dumb" or closed stage — often around the point where youthful primary fruit has faded but tertiary complexity hasn't yet emerged, and complex compounds are still forming. White Rhône and top reds are especially prone to it. A wine tasted in this phase can seem mute and disappointing; patience usually rewards.

Why it matters to tasting: the sommelier can reassure a drinker that a closed young wine of good pedigree may simply be in its dumb phase, not flawed.

## Clarification and fining {#clarification-and-fining}

A finished wine is naturally cloudy and must be clarified before bottling:

- Settling and racking — the most natural method: solids sink, clear wine is drawn off the top; repeated over time.
- Fining — adding an agent that binds to suspended particles and drags them down. Traditional fining agents include egg white, gelatin, isinglass (fish), casein (milk), and bentonite (clay); vegan alternatives (pea protein, bentonite alone) are increasingly common. Fining can also soften harsh tannins or remove bitterness.
- Filtration — physically straining the wine through membranes or pads to remove particles and microbes.

The filtration debate: overdone, it can strip flavor and ageing potential; underdone, it leaves the wine prey to microbes and refermentation. Many "natural" wines are unfined and unfiltered, accepting some cloudiness for a fuller expression.

Why it matters to tasting: slight haze or sediment in a natural/unfiltered wine is stylistic, not necessarily a fault; fined-and-filtered wines are bright and stable but some argue less texturally complete.

## Stabilization — keeping wine sound in bottle {#stabilization-keeping-wine-sound-in-bottle}

Before bottling, wine must be made stable so it doesn't throw deposits, haze, refermentation, or spoilage later:

- Tartrate stability — cold stabilization (above) to precipitate bitartrate crystals in advance.
- Protein stability (whites) — unstable grape proteins can form haze if the wine warms; bentonite fining removes them.
- Microbial stability — ensuring no fermentable sugar plus live yeast/bacteria remain that could referment; achieved through fermenting dry, sterile filtration, and adequate SO₂.
- Oxidative stability — sufficient free SO₂ and good closures to prevent browning.

Why it matters to tasting: most faults a consumer encounters (haze, refermentation fizz, browning) trace back to a stabilization step skipped or a closure that let in oxygen.

## The main wine faults (recognition guide) {#the-main-wine-faults-recognition-guide}

- Cork taint (TCA) — musty wet-cardboard/damp-basement smell, mutes fruit; caused by the compound trichloroanisole, usually from cork. Not harmful, but ruins the wine.
- Oxidation — bruised apple, sherry-like, flat; browning color. From excess oxygen or a failed seal.
- Volatile acidity (VA) — vinegar and/or nail-polish (ethyl acetate); from acetic acid bacteria.
- Reduction — rotten egg, struck match, boiled cabbage, garlic; from volatile sulfur compounds, often in low-oxygen (screwcap) conditions. Frequently blows off with air or a decant.
- Brettanomyces ("Brett") — barnyard, band-aid, smoky, horsey; a spoilage yeast. Divisive: a fault in excess, "complexity" to some in small doses.
- Heat damage (maderization) — cooked, stewed, flat; from storage too warm.
- Light strike — cabbage/wet-wool notes in wines (especially sparkling and in clear bottles) exposed to light.

Why it matters to tasting: distinguishing a genuine fault from a stylistic choice (natural-wine VA/Brett, reductive struck-match) is one of the most useful things a sommelier does. Reduction often clears with air; TCA never does.

## From chemistry to the glass — summary {#from-chemistry-to-the-glass-summary}

1. Phenolics (anthocyanins + tannins) set a red's color, structure, and ageability; extraction during fermentation controls how much ends up in the wine.
1. Acids and pH govern freshness, stability, and color; tartrates precipitate harmlessly in the cold.
1. Aroma builds in three layers — primary (grape), secondary (winemaking), tertiary (age) — from identifiable compound families.
1. Ageing is slow polymerization and oxidation: tannins soften, color shifts, fruit fades, bouquet develops, then harmony eventually breaks.
1. Clarification, fining, and stabilization make the wine bright and sound; skipping steps (by choice or error) explains most deposits and faults a drinker meets.
