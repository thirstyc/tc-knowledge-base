## Overview

Wine Region / France

Burgundy

The world's most detailed expression of terroir. Almost entirely single-variety — Pinot Noir for reds, Chardonnay for whites — with a fine-grained vineyard hierarchy that decides everything.

Key Grapes: Pinot Noir, Chardonnay
Climate: Cool continental
Known For: Terroir hierarchy, small fragmented vineyards
Homes: Chablis to the Mâconnais, eastern France

## Burgundy — overview {#burgundy-overview}

Burgundy (Bourgogne) is a long, narrow region in eastern France that represents the world's most detailed expression of terroir — the idea that a specific plot of land gives a wine a specific character. Almost all Burgundy is single-variety: Pinot Noir for reds, Chardonnay for whites. What varies is the place, sliced into a fine hierarchy of vineyards. Holdings are small and fragmented (a legacy of inheritance law), so the producer (domaine) matters as much as the vineyard. Burgundy runs north to south: Chablis, the Côte dOr (Côte de Nuits + Côte de Beaune), the Côte Chalonnaise, and the Mâconnais.
Recommend when: the user wants the benchmark for Pinot Noir or Chardonnay, is interested in terroir, or wants an elegant, food-versatile wine.

## The Burgundy quality hierarchy {#the-burgundy-quality-hierarchy}

Burgundy classifies vineyards, not producers, in four ascending tiers:

- Regional (Bourgogne) — the broadest appellation; grapes from anywhere in the region. Everyday Burgundy.
- Village — wines from a named commune (e.g. Gevrey-Chambertin, Meursault), blended from vineyards around that village.
- Premier Cru (1er Cru) — specific superior named vineyards within a village (labeled village + vineyard name).
- Grand Cru — the pinnacle: a small number of historic vineyards so renowned they stand alone on the label (e.g. Chambertin, Montrachet, Corton).

Practical use: the hierarchy is the single best guide to price and ambition in Burgundy — but producer quality can make a village wine outshine a lesser grower's Premier Cru.

## Chablis — steely northern Chardonnay {#chablis-steely-northern-chardonnay}

Chablis, in Burgundy's cool far north, makes Chardonnay in a distinctively lean, high-acid, mineral style — green apple, citrus, oyster-shell, "flinty" — often unoaked or only lightly oaked. Its Kimmeridgian limestone soils (full of tiny marine fossils) are credited with its signature stony character. It has its own hierarchy: Petit Chablis, Chablis, Chablis Premier Cru, and seven Grand Cru vineyards. Spring frost is its perennial hazard.
Serve at: 8–11°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a crisp, mineral, unoaked-style Chardonnay; a match for oysters, shellfish, or sushi; or a leaner alternative to rich Chardonnay.

## Côte de Nuits — the great reds {#c-te-de-nuits-the-great-reds}

The Côte de Nuits, the northern half of the Côte dOr, is Pinot Noir's greatest home — a narrow strip of slopes producing the most powerful, structured, long-lived red Burgundies. Its villages read like a roll call of legendary names: Gevrey-Chambertin, Morey-Saint-Denis, Chambolle-Musigny, Vougeot, Vosne-Romanée (home to Romanée-Conti and other Grands Crus), and Nuits-Saint-Georges. Most of Burgundy's red Grands Crus are here.
Serve at: 14–16°C.
Recommend when: the user wants the finest, most ageworthy Pinot Noir, or a special-occasion red for duck, game, or mushroom dishes.

## Côte de Beaune — great whites, elegant reds {#c-te-de-beaune-great-whites-elegant-reds}

The Côte de Beaune, the southern half of the Côte dOr, is famous above all for the world's greatest dry Chardonnay — from Meursault (rich, nutty, buttery), Puligny-Montrachet and Chassagne-Montrachet (precise, mineral, age-worthy), and the Grand Cru Montrachet itself. It also makes elegant Pinot Noir reds (Volnay, Pommard, Beaune) and, at Corton, both. Aloxe-Corton and the hill of Corton host the Côte's red and white Grands Crus.
Serve at: 11–13°C whites; 14–16°C reds.
Recommend when: the user wants a benchmark rich dry Chardonnay, a white for lobster or poultry in cream, or an elegant mid-weight Pinot.

## Côte Chalonnaise — value Burgundy {#c-te-chalonnaise-value-burgundy}

The Côte Chalonnaise, south of the Côte dOr, makes Pinot Noir and Chardonnay in a similar but less prestigious (and more affordable) style. Villages to know: Rully and Montagny (whites, plus sparkling Crémant base), Mercurey and Givry (reds), and Bouzeron (unusual for its Aligoté whites).
Recommend when: the user wants real Burgundy character at a gentler price, or a value Pinot/Chardonnay.

## Mâconnais — sunny, affordable Chardonnay {#m-connais-sunny-affordable-chardonnay}

The Mâconnais, Burgundy's warm southern end, is Chardonnay country producing rounder, riper, more affordable whites than the Côte dOr. Basic Mâcon and Mâcon-Villages are everyday whites; Pouilly-Fuissé is the top appellation (richer, ageworthy); Saint-Véran and Viré-Clessé offer excellent value.
Serve at: 10–12°C.
Recommend when: the user wants an approachable, good-value Chardonnay with a little more roundness than Chablis.

## Aligoté and Crémant de Bourgogne {#aligot-and-cr-mant-de-bourgogne}

Beyond the two stars, Burgundy grows Aligoté — a crisp, high-acid white grape (its own appellation Bouzeron), traditionally the base of a Kir with crème de cassis. Crémant de Bourgogne is the region's traditional-method sparkling wine, made from Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Aligoté, and Gamay — often excellent value versus Champagne.
Recommend when: Aligoté for a sharp, simple apéritif white; Crémant for value sparkling.

## Beaujolais note {#beaujolais-note}

Though administratively linked to Burgundy and made from Gamay (not Pinot Noir), Beaujolais has its own distinct identity and doc. Mentioned here only so the sommelier doesn't conflate it with Pinot-based Burgundy.

## Understanding Burgundys producer focus {#understanding-burgundys-producer-focus}

Because vineyards are subdivided among many owners, the same Grand Cru can be made by dozens of producers of wildly varying skill. In Burgundy, therefore, "who made it" is as important as "where it's from" — more so than in most of Bordeaux. Négociants (who buy grapes/wine) and domaines (who grow their own) both operate here.
Practical use: when recommending Burgundy, the sommelier should treat producer reputation as a primary quality signal, not just the appellation tier.

## Quick regional summary burgundy {#quick-regional-summary-burgundy}

- Grapes: Pinot Noir (red), Chardonnay (white), plus Aligoté and Gamay (Beaujolais).
- Hierarchy: Regional → Village → Premier Cru → Grand Cru (vineyard-based).
- North to south: Chablis (steely Chardonnay) → Côte de Nuits (great reds) → Côte de Beaune (great whites + elegant reds) → Côte Chalonnaise (value) → Mâconnais (sunny, affordable Chardonnay).
- Key idea: terroir taken to its finest resolution; producer matters as much as place.
