## Beaujolais — overview {#beaujolais-overview}

Beaujolais lies just south of Burgundy, on granite and schist hills, and makes light, juicy, low-tannin red wine from the Gamay grape. It is famous for a joyful, gulpable style — often made by carbonic maceration (whole-berry fermentation), which emphasizes bright fruit and reduces tannin. Beaujolais ranges from simple, cheerful bottles to serious, terroir-driven crus that rival village Burgundy at a fraction of the price. It is a cornerstone of the natural-wine movement.
Recommend when: the user wants a light, chillable, low-tannin red; a versatile food or apéritif red; or a value alternative to Pinot Noir.

## Beaujolais quality tiers {#beaujolais-quality-tiers}

- Beaujolais Nouveau — released the third Thursday of November, weeks after harvest; ultra-fresh, fruity (sometimes banana/bubblegum from carbonic maceration), meant to be drunk immediately. A marketing phenomenon more than a serious wine.
- Beaujolais / Beaujolais-Villages — everyday, fruity, easy reds; Villages a step up.
- The ten Crus — the top tier, named villages that don't use "Beaujolais" on the label: Morgon (structured, dark-fruited, ageworthy), Moulin-à-Vent ("the king," firmest and most Burgundian), Fleurie (floral, silky), Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly (fruity, largest), plus Chénas, Chiroubles, Juliénas, Régnié, Saint-Amour. Cru Beaujolais can age 3–10 years.

Serve at: 12–14°C — genuinely chillable.
Recommend when: matching seriousness — Nouveau for a party, Villages for everyday, crus (Morgon, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie) for a real wine that over-delivers on value.

## Provence — the rosé heartland {#provence-the-ros-heartland}

Provence, in the sunny Mediterranean southeast, is the world's benchmark for dry rosé — pale, crisp, and elegant, with delicate red-fruit, citrus, and herb notes. Rosé is roughly 90% of production. Made mainly from Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah, and Mourvèdre, Provençal rosé is dry and refreshing, built for the apéritif hour and Mediterranean food. The main appellation is Côtes de Provence, with Coteaux dAix-en-Provence and others alongside.
Serve at: 8–10°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a dry, elegant, pale rosé; a summer or apéritif wine; or a match for salads, seafood, and Mediterranean food.

## Bandol — Provences serious reds {#bandol-provences-serious-reds}

Bandol is Provence's most prestigious appellation, a coastal zone where Mourvèdre ripens fully to make deep, structured, ageworthy reds — dark fruit, leather, wild herbs, firm tannin — that can improve for a decade or more. Bandol also makes a highly regarded, more structured rosé.
Serve at: 16–18°C reds.
Recommend when: the user wants a serious, structured Mediterranean red (Mourvèdre), a match for game or lamb, or a rosé with more substance.

## Cassis & other Provence whites {#cassis-other-provence-whites}

Beyond rosé and Bandol, Provence makes distinctive whites — notably Cassis (a small coastal appellation, unrelated to the blackcurrant liqueur), producing fresh, textured whites from Marsanne, Clairette, and others, classic with bouillabaisse and Mediterranean seafood.
Recommend when: the user wants a Provençal white for seafood, or something beyond rosé from the region.

## Southwest France — overview {#southwest-france-overview}

Southwest France ("Sud-Ouest") is a diverse patchwork of appellations inland and south of Bordeaux, sharing some Bordeaux grapes but rich in distinctive local varieties. It is one of France's best sources of characterful, great-value, individual wines, both red and white, dry and sweet.
Recommend when: the user wants distinctive, affordable, off-the-beaten-path French wines with local character.

## Southwest reds — Cahors, Madiran & more {#southwest-reds-cahors-madiran-more}

- Cahors — dark, structured red from Malbec (locally Côt/Auxerrois); the historic home of the grape, more rustic and savory than Argentine Malbec.
- Madiran — powerful, tannic red from the local Tannat grape; deeply colored, ageworthy, needs time or food (classic with cassoulet and duck).
- Bergerac / Pécharmant — Bordeaux-style Merlot/Cabernet blends near Bordeaux, at friendlier prices.
- Fronton (Négrette), Marcillac (Fer Servadou/Mansois), Gaillac (many local grapes) — distinctive regional reds.

Serve at: 16–18°C.
Recommend when: the user wants a dark, structured red (Cahors Malbec, Madiran Tannat), a Bordeaux alternative (Bergerac), or something unusual and local.

## Southwest whites & sweet wines {#southwest-whites-sweet-wines}

- Jurançon (near the Pyrenees) — from Petit and Gros Manseng; both a racy dry white (sec) and a honeyed, high-acid sweet white (moelleux), the latter a classic with foie gras.
- Gaillac — dry, sweet, and sparkling whites from local grapes (Mauzac, Loin de lŒil).
- Monbazillac — botrytized sweet white near Bergerac (Sémillon-based), a great-value Sauternes alternative.
- Côtes de Gascogne (IGP) — crisp, zesty, inexpensive dry whites (often Colombard/Ugni Blanc), hugely popular as everyday value.

Recommend when: the user wants a racy or sweet Manseng (Jurançon), a value dessert wine (Monbazillac), or a cheap, crisp everyday white (Côtes de Gascogne).

## Quick regional summary beaujolais {#quick-regional-summary-beaujolais}

- Beaujolais: granite hills south of Burgundy; Gamay — light, juicy, chillable reds; ten serious crus (Morgon, Moulin-à-Vent, Fleurie) over-deliver on value; Nouveau is the November party wine.
- Provence: Mediterranean southeast; benchmark dry rosé (Grenache/Cinsault/Syrah/Mourvèdre); serious Mourvèdre reds in Bandol; whites in Cassis.
- Southwest France: diverse local grapes — Malbec (Cahors), Tannat (Madiran), Manseng (Jurançon), botrytized Monbazillac, value whites (Côtes de Gascogne); characterful and affordable.
