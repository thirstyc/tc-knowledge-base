Why does Friulano fly under the radar?

Short Answer

Friulano gets squeezed from both sides. Friuli-Venezia Giulia is famous for two things that aren't Friulano: internationally, it's Pinot Grigio territory, pumping out easy, high-volume whites that dominate supermarket shelves; on the more serious end, sommeliers and collectors chase the region's native reds like Schioppettino and Refosco, or skin-contact whites from producers doing orange wine. Friulano sits awkwardly in the middle, too refined and food-focused to compete on volume, too traditional to catch the natural-wine spotlight. It also took a branding hit: it used to be called Tocai Friulano, until an EU ruling forced a name change to avoid confusion with Hungary's Tokaji, and that kind of identity reset never helps recognition. The result is a grape making genuinely serious, ageable, textured whites in Collio and Colli Orientali del Friuli that most people have simply never had a reason to look for. It's the wine list equivalent of a great restaurant with no sign out front.
