Why does Bonarda taste so juicy and low-tannin?

Short Answer

Argentina's Bonarda isn't actually related to the Italian grapes of the same name, DNA testing traced it to Corbeau, an old Savoie variety also called Charbono. It's thin-skinned and vigorous, which means less tannin extraction to begin with, so the wine never builds the grip you'd get from something like Malbec. Most of it grows in Mendoza at real altitude, where big daily temperature swings let grapes hold onto acidity even as sugars climb, so you get ripe cherry and plum fruit without the flabbiness that heat usually brings. Producers also tend to pick it a touch earlier and skip heavy oak, since the point of the grape is freshness, not structure. The result is a wine that's genuinely food-friendly and low-commitment, soft enough to drink on its own but with enough acid to keep it lively. Serve it slightly cool, around 14-16°C, and it drinks like the easiest thing in the glass.
