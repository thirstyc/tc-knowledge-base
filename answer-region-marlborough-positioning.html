Why did Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc take over the world?

Short Answer

Marlborough hit on a flavour profile nobody else was making at scale, that punchy combination of passionfruit, gooseberry, and a grassy, cut-grass edge that comes from methoxypyrazine compounds thriving in the region's cool nights and long, sunny days. The Wairau Valley's stony, free-draining soils and big diurnal temperature swing let Sauvignon Blanc hang onto racy acidity while still ripening tropical fruit flavours, a combination that's genuinely hard to replicate elsewhere. New Zealand producers also leaned into stainless steel fermentation and modern viticulture from the start, no old-world baggage to unlearn, which meant consistency vintage after vintage at a price point that undercut white Burgundy and Sancerre. That reliability is what let it become a category unto itself rather than just another regional bottling, bartenders and grocery shoppers alike could count on the same zippy, unmistakable profile every time. It became New Zealand's calling card because it solved a problem the market didn't know it had: an intensely flavoured white wine that never asked you to think too hard about it.
