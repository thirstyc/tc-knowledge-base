Why does Nero d'Avola taste so dark?

Short Answer

Here's the correction worth making: most Nero d'Avola isn't grown on volcanic soil or at altitude, that's Etna's story, a small and separate corner of Sicilian winemaking. The grape's real home is the flatter, warmer southeast of the island, on limestone and clay, where relentless sun does the heavy lifting. Nero d'Avola ripens fully in that heat, packing its thick skins with anthocyanins, the pigment compounds responsible for deep color, along with plenty of sugar that becomes alcohol. That's why the wine looks almost opaque in the glass. Tannin, though, is usually moderate rather than high, soft and rounded compared to something like Nebbiolo, which is part of why the wine drinks so easily young despite its color. Dark fruit, a touch of licorice or dried herb, and supple structure are the real signature, not grip. Serve it around 16°C to keep the fruit lively without dulling it with too much chill.
