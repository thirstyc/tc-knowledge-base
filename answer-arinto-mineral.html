Why is Arinto so mineral and crisp?

Short Answer

Arinto is a late-ripening Portuguese grape that keeps its malic acid even after it's fully ripe, which is unusual — most grapes lose acidity as sugar builds. That trait alone explains the crispness; this isn't a grape that needs a cool vintage to stay fresh, it's built that way. It's grown mostly around Lisbon, in Bucelas, and along the Atlantic coast where sea air keeps daytime heat in check, plus it shows up as a backbone blending grape in Vinho Verde. The mineral, almost saline edge comes from a mix of limestone and clay soils and winemaking that stays out of the way — stainless steel, low fermentation temperatures, no oak — so nothing masks the citrus and wet-stone character. Serve it cold, around 8–10°C, and you'll taste why it's held onto its acidity so stubbornly: there's a tension in the glass that oak-aged whites rarely manage.
