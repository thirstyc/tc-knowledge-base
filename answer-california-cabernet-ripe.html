Why does California Cabernet taste so ripe and powerful?

Short Answer

California's Cabernet regions, Napa especially, get long stretches of sunny, dry days with big diurnal swings, warm afternoons cooling into the evening. That sun pushes sugar levels up fast, but growers there generally wait for phenolic ripeness too: tannins softening, seeds turning brown, green pepper notes fading. The tradeoff is that by the time tannins are fully ripe, sugar has often climbed well past what cooler regions like Bordeaux see, so alcohol frequently lands between 14.5 and 15.5 percent. High sugar at harvest means more alcohol after fermentation, and alcohol reads on the palate as weight, sweetness of fruit, and warmth. Add moderate new oak aging, common in the Napa style, and you get vanilla and baking-spice layered over dense black currant and blackberry fruit. It's a different philosophy from Bordeaux, which picks earlier and leans on structure over power. Neither is wrong, but if you want restraint, look toward cooler pockets like Santa Cruz Mountains or coastal Sonoma instead.
