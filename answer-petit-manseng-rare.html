Why is Petit Manseng hard to find outside southwest France?

Short Answer

It's a homebody by nature. Petit Manseng has thick skins and hangs onto the vine well past normal harvest, sometimes into late autumn, letting the berries shrivel and concentrate through a process called passerillage. That trick depends on the specific dry autumn winds of Gascony and Jurançon in southwest France, without them the grapes just rot instead of raisining. The vine is also a shy producer, yields are naturally low, so it was never going to be the kind of grape a large commercial estate elsewhere plants at scale hoping for volume. Add to that a style, sweet-leaning, high-acid whites, that isn't in huge international demand outside a niche of curious drinkers, and you get a grape that stayed rooted to a small corner of France instead of spreading the way Chardonnay or Sauvignon Blanc did. A handful of growers in the New World have planted it as a curiosity, but it remains genuinely rare outside its home turf.
